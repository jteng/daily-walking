## 本章脉络

从第七章起，保罗开始回答哥林多人来信所问的事：「论到你们信上所提的事」（7:1）。这一章讲婚姻和独身，分五段。**夫妻之间**（1-7节）：夫妻彼此有责任，不可彼此亏负。**没有嫁娶的和寡妇**（8-9节）：独身是好的，嫁娶也是好的。**离婚与信与不信的婚姻**（10-16节）：主吩咐不可离婚；与不信的配偶，只要对方情愿同住，就不要离弃。**各人守住蒙召的身分**（17-24节）：受割礼与否、为奴或自主，都不是最重要的，最重要的是在神面前活出他的呼召。**童身的人与寡妇**（25-40节）：因为「时候减少了」，「这世界的样子将要过去了」，保罗愿意信徒「殷勤服事主，没有分心的事」。

这一章的中心是17节：「只要照主所分给各人的，和神所召各人的而行。」不论是已婚、独身、离婚、丧偶，一个人在神面前的价值，不在于婚姻状况，而在于他是神所召、基督用重价买来的人（7:23）。

## 深读 7:1-9 夫妻之间与独身的恩赐

**1. 「男不近女倒好」。** 「论到你们信上所提的事，我说男不近女倒好。」（7:1）「不近女」是委婉的说法，指不同房。和合本把这句话当作保罗自己的话；但许多解经家（如戈登·费依、锡塞尔顿）认为，这是哥林多人信上的话，有些现代译本（如ESV）也加上引号。第六章里有人以为身体做什么都无所谓，这里却好像走到另一个极端：有人以为属灵的人连夫妻同房都应当避免。保罗在2-5节的回应，说明他不赞同已婚的人长期禁欲，只容许「两相情愿，暂时分房」，专心祷告（7:5）。

**2. 彼此相属。** 「妻子没有权柄主张自己的身子，乃在丈夫；丈夫也没有权柄主张自己的身子，乃在妻子。」（7:4）在古代社会，丈夫对妻子的身体有权柄，人人都接受；可是说妻子对丈夫的身体也有同样的权柄，在当时是非常新的说法。保罗每一句都是双向的，丈夫怎样，妻子也怎样。婚姻中的性，是彼此委身、彼此给予，不是一方向另一方索取的权利（参弗5:25）。

**3. 这段经文的界限。** 「夫妻不可彼此亏负」（7:5）是叫夫妻彼此关爱，不是给任何人强迫配偶的理由。在婚姻中用威胁或暴力强迫配偶，是罪，在许多地方也是违法的，受害人可以，也应当寻求保护，包括报警（罗13:4）。教会不可用这节经文要求受害人顺从，也不可为了面子把这样的事压下来（「家丑不可外扬」）。

**4. 各有恩赐。** 「我愿意众人像我一样；只是各人领受神的恩赐，一个是这样，一个是那样。」（7:7）保罗写这封信时没有妻子。他看独身是神的恩赐，耶稣也说过「有为天国的缘故自阉的」（太19:12）。但保罗没有说独身的人比已婚的人更属灵：「一个是这样，一个是那样」，两者都是恩赐。对那些「自己禁止不住」的人，他说「倒不如嫁娶为妙」（7:9）。

> 要点：婚姻和独身都是神的恩赐，没有哪一种更属灵。婚姻中的身体是彼此给予，不是彼此索取。

## 深读 7:10-16 离婚与信与不信的婚姻

**1. 主的吩咐。** 「至于那已经嫁娶的，我吩咐他们；其实不是我吩咐，乃是主吩咐说：妻子不可离开丈夫，若是离开了，不可再嫁，或是仍同丈夫和好。丈夫也不可离弃妻子。」（7:10-11）保罗在这里引用的是耶稣在地上的教导：「神配合的，人不可分开。」（可10:9）保罗也知道现实中已经有人离开了，所以他说：这样的人当保持不再嫁，或与丈夫和好。

**2. 「不是主说」。** 「我对其余的人说（不是主说）……」（7:12）这不表示接下来的话权威较低，好像只是保罗的私人意见。意思是：耶稣在地上没有处理过信徒与不信的配偶的婚姻，保罗以使徒的身分来处理（参7:40；14:37）。

**3. 不信的配偶「成了圣洁」。** 「因为不信的丈夫就因着妻子成了圣洁……不然，你们的儿女就不洁净，但如今他们是圣洁的了。」（7:14）有些哥林多信徒可能担心，与不信的人同住会使自己被玷污。保罗说正好相反：这婚姻在神面前是正当的，不信的配偶和儿女，因为与信主的一方联合，就被摆在一个特别的地位上，常常受到福音的影响。这里的「成了圣洁」不是说他们已经得救，因为16节还说：「你……怎么知道不能救你的丈夫呢？」至于「儿女……是圣洁的」，改革宗中主张婴儿洗礼的传统（如加尔文）认为，信徒的儿女在神的约中有特别的地位；浸信会的传统则多半理解为：这些儿女是合法婚姻所生，在敬虔的影响下长大。两种看法都同意：这里不是说儿女自动得救。

**4. 「不必拘束」。** 「倘若那不信的人要离去，就由他离去吧！无论是弟兄，是姊妹，遇着这样的事都不必拘束。神召我们原是要我们和睦。」（7:15）信主的一方不要主动离弃；但如果不信的一方坚持离去，信徒不必用尽一切办法挽留。「不必拘束」原文是不受奴役。这是否包括可以再婚，历代有不同的理解：
- 宗教改革家和《威斯敏斯德信条》（24章5-6节）认为，因配偶淫乱（太19:9），或因无法挽回的故意遗弃而离婚的人，可以再婚。
- 天主教的立场较窄：圣事婚姻不能解除；但根据这节经文的保禄特权（《天主教法典》1143条），若结婚时双方都未受洗，后来一方受洗，而未受洗的一方离去，受洗的一方可以再婚。
- 另一些人（包括许多早期教父和今天的派博〔John Piper〕）认为，「不必拘束」只是说不必勉强维持婚姻，只要原配偶还活着，就不可再婚。

两种看法都出于尊重圣经；有这样处境的弟兄姊妹，应当请教会成熟的牧者同行，慢慢寻求神的心意。

**5. 遭受虐待的时候。** 保罗在这里没有直接讨论家庭暴力，但有几件事是清楚的：遭受暴力或虐待的人，离开危险、寻求保护，不是犯罪，也可以报警（罗13:4）；第11节本身就预想到夫妻可能分开。教会不可以为了「神配合的，人不可分开」，就要求受害人留在危险中，或劝他们与不安全的配偶和好，也不可让儿女继续处在危险中，更不可为了面子隐瞒。严重的虐待是否也让信徒可以离婚，福音派内部有不同看法：有人认为这本身就是一种遗弃；古德恩（Wayne Grudem）在2019年改变立场，认为「遇着这样的事」（7:15）不只限于遗弃，也包括严重虐待这一类情况；也有人认为虐待可以分居（参7:11），却不构成离婚的理由。第一步永远是保障安全。

> 要点：神看重婚姻，信徒不可轻易离婚。但神召我们「要我们和睦」，不是要人留在危险和暴力中。

## 深读 7:17-24 照神所召各人的而行

**1. 蒙召时的身分。** 「只要照主所分给各人的，和神所召各人的而行。我吩咐各教会都是这样。」（7:17）保罗用割礼和奴隶两个例子，说明一个原则：信主以后，不必急着改变自己的外在处境，好像这样才能更讨神喜悦。「受割礼算不得什么，不受割礼也算不得什么，只要守神的诫命就是了。」（7:19）

**2. 「求自由更好」。** 「你是作奴隶蒙召的吗？不要因此忧虑；若能以自由，就求自由更好。」（7:21）后半句的原文很简短，可以有两种理解：和合本和许多译本理解为：若有机会得自由，就把握机会；也有人理解为：即使能得自由，也要好好利用现在的处境。上下文中保罗说「不要作人的奴仆」（7:23），所以许多解经家认为前一种理解比较自然。保罗不是赞成奴隶制度，他在别处把「抢人口」列为罪（提前1:10）。

**3. 主所释放的人。** 「因为作奴仆蒙召于主的，就是主所释放的人；作自由之人蒙召的，就是基督的奴仆。你们是重价买来的，不要作人的奴仆。」（7:22-23）在基督里，身分被重新定义了：奴隶在主里是自由人，自由人在主里是基督的仆人。人一生最重要的身分，不是社会给的，而是神给的（参西3:11）。

> 要点：我在神面前的价值，不在于我的婚姻、职业或社会地位，而在于我是神所召、基督用重价买来的人。

## 深读 7:25-40 时候减少了

**1. 现今的艰难。** 「因现今的艰难，据我看来，人不如守素安常才好。」（7:26）「现今的艰难」可能是指当时哥林多一带的饥荒等困难（布鲁斯·温特〔Bruce Winter〕的看法），也可能是指基督第一次与第二次降临之间，信徒在世上所受的苦难。无论哪一种理解，这都是劝勉，不是命令：「你若娶妻，并不是犯罪」（7:28；参7:35）。

**2. 「要像没有」。** 「时候减少了。从此以后，那有妻子的，要像没有妻子；哀哭的，要像不哀哭；快乐的，要像不快乐……因为这世界的样子将要过去了。」（7:29-31）这不是叫人冷漠，不爱配偶、不哀哭、不快乐，而是叫我们不把今生的任何东西当作终极。婚姻、哀伤、喜乐、财产都是真实的，却都是暂时的。

**3. 没有分心。** 「没有娶妻的，是为主的事挂虑，想怎样叫主喜悦。娶了妻的，是为世上的事挂虑，想怎样叫妻子喜悦。」（7:32-33）保罗不是说照顾配偶是坏事，而是说已婚的人心思难免分在两处，这是很自然的。独身的人有更多的自由，可以「殷勤服事主，没有分心的事」（7:35）。他也说：「我说这话是为你们的益处，不是要牢笼你们。」

**4. 两种翻译。** 36-38节有两种理解：和合本理解为父亲决定是否把女儿嫁出去；大多数现代译本（如ESV）理解为一个已经订婚的男人，决定是否与未婚妻成亲。原文两种都可能，但无论哪一种，结论都一样：嫁娶「不算有罪」，不嫁娶「更是好」。

**5. 只要在主里。** 「丈夫若死了，妻子就可以自由，随意再嫁，只是要嫁这在主里面的人。」（7:39）寡妇可以再嫁，这是自由；可是只能嫁给信主的人（参林后6:14）。

> 要点：「这世界的样子将要过去了。」不论已婚或独身，我们最终的盼望和满足都不在今生的关系中，而在那位永远不变的主。

## 默想

**默想：** 「各人领受神的恩赐，一个是这样，一个是那样。」在华人家庭里，一个三十岁还没有结婚的人，过年回家最怕的，常常是亲戚问「什么时候结婚」；父母着急，有时教会的弟兄姊妹也会不经意地说：「你条件这么好，怎么还单着？」好像一个人要结了婚，人生才算完整。也有人带着一段破碎的婚姻坐在教会里，觉得自己低人一等。我有没有在心里认定：没有婚姻，或婚姻失败了，我在神面前就少了什么？保罗却说，在神面前，我最重要的身分是「神所召」的人、「重价买来的」人（7:17，23）。我们的主耶稣在地上没有结婚，他的人生却是最完整的；他应许为他撇下一切的人，在今世就要得着弟兄、姊妹、母亲、儿女（可10:29-30）。今天，我能不能把自己的婚姻状况，不论是单身、已婚、离婚还是丧偶，放在那位爱我、为我舍己的主面前？我也可以问：在教会里，我能不能成为单身或离婚的弟兄姊妹的家人，而不是在旁边议论的人？

## The Shape of the Chapter

From chapter 7 on, Paul answers questions the Corinthians had sent him: "Now concerning the matters about which you wrote" (7:1). This chapter is about marriage and singleness, in five parts. **Husband and wife** (vv. 1–7): spouses have duties to each other and must not deprive one another. **The unmarried and widows** (vv. 8–9): singleness is good, and so is marriage. **Divorce and mixed marriages** (vv. 10–16): the Lord commands that the married not divorce; with an unbelieving spouse who consents to stay, the believer should not divorce. **Remaining in one's calling** (vv. 17–24): circumcised or not, slave or free, none of these is what matters most; what matters is living out God's call before him. **The betrothed and widows** (vv. 25–40): because "the appointed time has grown very short" and "the present form of this world is passing away," Paul wants believers to have "undivided devotion to the Lord."

The center of the chapter is verse 17: "Only let each person lead the life that the Lord has assigned to him, and to which God has called him." Married, single, divorced or widowed, a person's worth before God lies not in marital status but in being someone God has called and Christ has bought with a price (7:23).

## Going Deeper: 7:1–9 Husband and Wife, and the Gift of Singleness

**1. "It is good for a man not to have sexual relations with a woman."** (7:1). The CUV reads this as Paul's own statement, but many interpreters (e.g. Gordon Fee and Anthony Thiselton) take it as a line from the Corinthians' letter, and some modern translations, including the ESV, put it in quotation marks. In chapter 6 some thought it didn't matter what one did with the body; here some seem to have swung to the other extreme, thinking that truly spiritual people should avoid sexual relations even within marriage. Paul's answer in verses 2–5 shows that he does not approve of ongoing abstinence within marriage; he allows only a brief pause, "by agreement for a limited time," for prayer (7:5).

**2. Belonging to each other.** "For the wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. Likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does" (7:4). In the ancient world everyone accepted that a husband had authority over his wife's body; to say that a wife has the same authority over her husband's body was strikingly new. Every sentence Paul writes runs both ways. Sex in marriage is mutual self-giving, not a right one spouse extracts from the other (cf. Eph 5:25).

**3. The limits of this passage.** "Do not deprive one another" (7:5) calls spouses to care for each other; it gives no one a reason to coerce a spouse. Forcing a spouse by threats or violence is sin, and in many places a crime, and the victim may, and should, seek protection, including from the police (Rom 13:4). The church must not use this verse to demand a victim's compliance, nor hush such things up to save face.

**4. Each has his own gift.** "I wish that all were as I myself am. But each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another" (7:7). Paul had no wife when he wrote. He sees singleness as a gift from God, and Jesus too spoke of those who "have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 19:12). But Paul does not say the single are more spiritual than the married: "one of one kind and one of another"; both are gifts. To those who "cannot exercise self-control" he says, "it is better to marry" (7:9).

> The point: Marriage and singleness are both gifts from God; neither is more spiritual. In marriage the body is given to each other, not taken from each other.

## Going Deeper: 7:10–16 Divorce and Mixed Marriages

**1. The Lord's command.** "To the married I give this charge (not I, but the Lord): the wife should not separate from her husband (but if she does, she should remain unmarried or else be reconciled to her husband), and the husband should not divorce his wife" (7:10–11). Paul is citing Jesus' teaching on earth: "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Mark 10:9). He also knows that some have already separated, so he says such a person should remain unmarried or be reconciled.

**2. "I, not the Lord."** "To the rest I say (I, not the Lord)..." (7:12). This doesn't make what follows less authoritative, as if it were only Paul's private opinion. It means that Jesus in his earthly ministry did not address marriages between a believer and an unbeliever, and Paul now addresses them as an apostle (cf. 7:40; 14:37).

**3. The unbelieving spouse "made holy."** "For the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife... Otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is, they are holy" (7:14). Some Corinthian believers may have feared that living with an unbeliever would defile them. Paul says the opposite: the marriage is legitimate before God, and the unbelieving spouse and the children, through their union with the believer, are set in a special position, often under the gospel's influence. "Made holy" here does not mean they are saved, for verse 16 still asks, "how do you know whether you will save your husband?" As for the children being "holy," the Reformed paedobaptist tradition (e.g. Calvin) sees believers' children as having a special place in God's covenant; the Baptist tradition mostly reads it as meaning that these children are born of a legitimate marriage and raised under godly influence. Both agree that the children are not automatically saved.

**4. "Not enslaved."** "But if the unbelieving partner separates, let it be so. In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved. God has called you to peace" (7:15). The believer should not initiate the separation, but if the unbeliever insists on leaving, the believer need not hold on at all costs. Whether "not enslaved" includes freedom to remarry has been read in different ways:
- The Reformers and the Westminster Confession (24.5–6) hold that one divorced because of a spouse's adultery (Matt 19:9) or "such wilful desertion as can no way be remedied" may remarry.
- Catholic canon law takes a narrower path: it holds a sacramental marriage indissoluble, but its "Pauline privilege" (Canon 1143), drawn from this verse, lets a convert remarry when both spouses were unbaptized at marriage and the unbaptized partner departs.
- Others (including many early church fathers and, today, John Piper) hold that "not enslaved" only means the believer need not force the marriage to continue, and that remarriage is not permitted while the former spouse lives.

Both views come from a desire to honor Scripture; a brother or sister in this situation should ask a mature pastor to walk with them as they seek God's will without haste.

**5. When there is abuse.** Paul does not directly discuss domestic violence here, but some things are clear. A person suffering violence or abuse who leaves the danger and seeks protection is not sinning, and may go to the police (Rom 13:4); verse 11 itself envisions spouses separating. The church must not invoke "what God has joined together, let not man separate" to demand that a victim stay in danger or reconcile with an unsafe spouse, or leave children exposed to danger, still less cover it up to save face. Whether severe abuse also frees a believer to divorce is debated among evangelicals. Some argue it is itself a form of desertion; Wayne Grudem, changing his view in 2019, argued that "in such cases" (7:15) reaches beyond desertion to situations such as severe abuse; others hold that abuse warrants separation (cf. 7:11) but not divorce. The first step is always safety.

> The point: God honors marriage, and believers must not divorce lightly. But God "has called you to peace"; he does not ask anyone to stay in danger and violence.

## Going Deeper: 7:17–24 Lead the Life God Has Called You To

**1. The condition in which you were called.** "Only let each person lead the life that the Lord has assigned to him, and to which God has called him. This is my rule in all the churches" (7:17). Paul uses two examples, circumcision and slavery, to show one principle: after coming to faith, a person need not rush to change outward circumstances, as if that would please God more. "For neither circumcision counts for anything nor uncircumcision, but keeping the commandments of God" (7:19).

**2. "Avail yourself of the opportunity."** "Were you a slave when called? Do not be concerned about it. But if you can gain your freedom, avail yourself of the opportunity" (7:21). The Greek of the second half is very terse and can be read two ways: the CUV, ESV and many translations take it as "if you have the chance to be free, take it"; others take it as "even if you can be free, make the most of your present condition." Since Paul goes on to say "do not become slaves of men" (7:23), many think the first reading is more natural. Paul does not endorse slavery; elsewhere he lists "enslavers" among sinners (1 Tim 1:10).

**3. The Lord's freedman.** "For he who was called in the Lord as a slave is a freedman of the Lord. Likewise he who was free when called is a slave of Christ. You were bought with a price; do not become slaves of men" (7:22–23). In Christ, status is redefined: the slave is free in the Lord, the free man is Christ's servant. A person's most important identity is not the one society gives, but the one God gives (cf. Col 3:11).

> The point: My worth before God lies not in my marriage, job or social standing, but in being someone God has called and Christ has bought with a price.

## Going Deeper: 7:25–40 The Time Has Grown Short

**1. The present distress.** "I think that in view of the present distress it is good for a person to remain as he is" (7:26). "The present distress" may refer to hardships such as famine in and around Corinth at the time (Bruce Winter's view), or to the sufferings believers face in the world between Christ's first and second comings. On either reading this is counsel, not command: "if you do marry, you have not sinned" (7:28; cf. 7:35).

**2. "As though they had none."** "The appointed time has grown very short. From now on, let those who have wives live as though they had none, and those who mourn as though they were not mourning, and those who rejoice as though they were not rejoicing... For the present form of this world is passing away" (7:29–31). This is not a call to coldness, to not loving one's spouse, not grieving, not rejoicing, but a call not to treat anything in this life as ultimate. Marriage, grief, joy and possessions are all real, but all temporary.

**3. Undivided devotion.** "The unmarried man is anxious about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But the married man is anxious about worldly things, how to please his wife, and his interests are divided" (7:32–34). Paul isn't saying that caring for a spouse is bad, only that the married person's interests are naturally "divided." The single person has more freedom "to secure... undivided devotion to the Lord" (7:35). And he adds: "I say this for your own benefit, not to lay any restraint upon you."

**4. Two translations.** Verses 36–38 are read in two ways: the CUV takes them as a father deciding whether to give his daughter in marriage; most modern translations (including the ESV) take them as a man deciding whether to marry his fiancée. The Greek allows either, but on either reading the conclusion is the same: to marry "is no sin," and to refrain "will do even better."

**5. Only in the Lord.** "But if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord" (7:39). A widow may remarry; that is her freedom. But she may marry only a believer (cf. 2 Cor 6:14).

> The point: "The present form of this world is passing away." Married or single, our final hope and satisfaction lie not in the relationships of this life but in the Lord who never changes.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "Each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another." In Chinese families, what a thirty-year-old who is still single often dreads most at New Year is relatives asking, "When are you getting married?" Parents worry, and sometimes church friends say without thinking, "You have so much going for you; why are you still single?" as if life only becomes complete with marriage. Others sit in church carrying a broken marriage and feel they are second-class. Have I decided in my heart that without a marriage, or with a failed one, I am somehow less before God? Paul says my most important identity before God is one whom God "has called" and one "bought with a price" (7:17, 23). Our Lord Jesus never married on earth, yet his was the most complete human life ever lived; and he promises that those who leave everything for him will receive, even in this age, brothers, sisters, mothers and children (Mark 10:29–30). Today, can I bring my marital status, whether single, married, divorced or widowed, before the Lord who loved me and gave himself for me? And I can ask: in my church, can I be family to brothers and sisters who are single or divorced, rather than one who talks about them behind their backs?
