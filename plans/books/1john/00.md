## 这卷书在讲什么

约翰一书是写给一群心里不安的信徒的。他们的教会刚刚经历了分裂：「他们从我们中间出去，却不是属我们的；若是属我们的，就必仍旧与我们同在；他们出去，显明都不是属我们的。」（2:19）离开的人不是一般的旁观者，而是曾经和他们一起聚会、一起祷告的人。这些人对耶稣有另一套说法，并且很可能还自称有更深的属灵知识。留下来的人难免会问：到底谁是对的？我们所信的，真的是从起初所传的那位基督吗？我们真的认识神吗？

约翰写信，是要回答这些问题。全书的目的写在接近结尾的地方：「我将这些话写给你们信奉神儿子之名的人，要叫你们知道自己有永生。」（5:13）约翰福音写成，是「要叫你们信耶稣是基督，是神的儿子，并且叫你们信了他，就可以因他的名得生命」（约20:31）；约翰一书却是写给已经信了的人，要他们「知道」自己有这生命。全书讲到「知道」「晓得」「认识」的地方，有好几十处。

约翰的回答很朴素。他不另外给读者一套更高的知识，而是领他们回到「从起初」所听见的（1:1；2:7、24；3:11）。他反复讲三件事：
- **信：** 「凡信耶稣是基督的，都是从神而生。」（5:1）分辨真假的第一个标准，是人怎样认耶稣：「凡灵认耶稣基督是成了肉身来的，就是出于神的。」（4:2）
- **顺服：** 「我们若遵守他的诫命，就晓得是认识他。」（2:3）说自己认识神，却照样活在罪中的人，「便是说谎话的」（2:4）。
- **爱：** 「我们因为爱弟兄，就晓得是已经出死入生了。」（3:14）说自己爱神却恨弟兄的人，也是「说谎话的」（4:20）。

一百多年前，苏格兰学者罗伯特·劳写了一本讲这封信的书，书名叫《生命的试验》，全书就按这三个试验来讲。后来约翰·斯托得在他的注释里，也用这三个试验来讲这封信。不过这些试验不是要把读者吓走。约翰称读者为「小子们」「亲爱的弟兄」，他要的是让真信的人心里安稳（3:19-20），也让离开真道的人显露出来。这一切的根基，是神先爱了我们：「不是我们爱神，乃是神爱我们，差他的儿子为我们的罪作了挽回祭，这就是爱了。」（4:10）

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 信上怎样说。** 这封信没有写作者的名字，开头也没有问安。作者却清楚地说，他是亲眼见过主的人：「论到从起初原有的生命之道，就是我们所听见、所看见、亲眼看过、亲手摸过的。」（1:1）他称读者为「小子们」（2:1、12、28），像一位年老的牧者在对自己牧养多年的人说话。约翰二书和三书的作者自称「作长老的」（约二1；约三1）。

**2. 和约翰福音的关系。** 这封信和约翰福音的用词、思路非常接近：「从起初」和「太初」（1:1；约1:1）、生命与光（1:2、5；约1:4）、真理、「住在」、「新命令」（2:7-8；约13:34），以及神「差他独生子到世间来」（4:9；参约3:16）。连全书的写作目的，也和约翰福音几乎一样（5:13；约20:31）。多数学者认为，两卷书出于同一个人，或至少出于同一个教会圈子。

**3. 谁写的？** 早期教会一致认为，这封信是使徒约翰写的，就是「耶稣所爱的那门徒」（约21:20、24）。二世纪初的士每拿主教坡旅甲在《致腓立比人书》第七章，有一句话和4:2-3（参约二7）非常接近；据优西比乌记载，帕皮亚也用过这封信（《教会史》第三卷第三十九章）。二世纪末的爱任纽明白地说，这封信是主的门徒约翰写的。也有学者认为作者是另一位约翰。帕皮亚有一段话，先在主的几个门徒的名字中提到约翰，接着又提到长老约翰（同上）。优西比乌认为这是两个人，有人因此认为约翰二书和三书中的「长老」是另一位约翰；也有不少学者认为，帕皮亚两次说的是同一位约翰。天主教学者雷蒙德·布朗等人另有看法：当时有一个约翰群体，源头是「耶稣所爱的那门徒」（布朗认为这位门徒不是西庇太的儿子约翰）；这封信是这群体中的一位教师写的，不是写约翰福音的那一位。本研读和斯托得、克鲁斯、亚伯勒等学者一样，采用传统的看法：作者是使徒约翰。无论采用哪一种看法，作者都清楚地表明，他是亲眼见过主的见证人（1:1-3）。

**4. 什么时候写的？在哪里写的？** 据爱任纽记载，约翰晚年住在小亚细亚的以弗所，一直活到图拉真在位的时候（图拉真于主后98年即位）。多数学者认为这封信写在约翰福音之后，约主后85-95年，写给以弗所一带的教会。

**5. 离开的人教导什么？** 信上没有完整地说明他们的错误，只能从约翰的回答推想。他们大概：
- **否认耶稣是基督：** 「谁是说谎话的呢？不是那不认耶稣为基督的吗？」（2:22）他们可能不是否认有一位「基督」，而是否认那位在历史上真实活过的人耶稣就是基督。
- **否认基督成了肉身：** 他们「不认耶稣基督是成了肉身来的」（约二7；参约一4:2-3）。
- **轻看罪：** 1:6、8、10三次说到「我们若说」，可能是在回应他们的口号：自称与神相交，又自称无罪。
- **不爱弟兄：** 2:9说「人若说自己在光明中，却恨他的弟兄」，可能也是在说他们。

许多人把他们和二世纪的诺斯底主义联想在一起。诺斯底派认为物质是低劣的，得救在于得着隐秘的「知识」。有些人（称为「幻影派」）认为基督只是看起来有身体。爱任纽也记载，与约翰同时代有一个叫克林妥的人，教导说「基督」在耶稣受洗的时候降在他身上，在他受难以前又离开了他，所以受苦、死去的只是人耶稣（《驳异端》第一卷第二十六章第一节）。这或许可以解释约翰为什么强调：「这藉着水和血而来的，就是耶稣基督；不是单用水，乃是用水又用血。」（5:6）爱任纽还说，有人从坡旅甲那里听过一件事：约翰有一次在以弗所的澡堂里，听说克林妥也在里面，就立刻跑出来（《驳异端》第三卷第三章第四节）。不过，约翰一书所对付的，不一定是克林妥本人，也不一定是后来成形的诺斯底主义，而可能是这些思想早期的雏形。

## 钥匙经文：1:9；2:1-2；4:10；5:13

**1. 「我们若认自己的罪」。** 「我们若认自己的罪，神是信实的，是公义的，必要赦免我们的罪，洗净我们一切的不义。」（1:9）约翰前后说了三次「我们若说」（1:6、8、10），揭穿种种虚假的说法。他给信徒的路不是假装无罪，而是认罪。神赦免认罪的人，不只是因为他怜悯，也是因为他「公义」：基督已经替我们担当了罪的刑罚（1:7；2:2）。赦免的根基是基督的血，不是我认罪认得够不够完全；认罪是回到光中，不是向神补交欠款。

**2. 「在父那里我们有一位中保」。** 「我小子们哪，我将这些话写给你们，是要叫你们不犯罪。若有人犯罪，在父那里我们有一位中保，就是那义者耶稣基督。他为我们的罪作了挽回祭，不是单为我们的罪，也是为普天下人的罪。」（2:1-2）这里译作「中保」的词（*paraklētos*），和合本在约翰福音译作「保惠师」（约14:16），原意是站在旁边帮助人、替人说话的人；提摩太前书2:5「一位中保」的原文，是另一个词。「挽回祭」指的是平息神对罪的忿怒、使人与神和好的祭；神的公义得了满足，罪人才能蒙赦免。这挽回祭是神自己因爱预备的（4:10）。所以犯了罪的信徒，不必先替自己辩护，也不必躲着神；那位义者正在父面前为我们说话。至于「普天下人的罪」是什么意思，有不同的读法，第二章会再讲。

**3. 「这就是爱了」。** 「不是我们爱神，乃是神爱我们，差他的儿子为我们的罪作了挽回祭，这就是爱了。」（4:10）约翰两次说「神就是爱」（4:8、16），但他没有让「爱」变成一个空泛的概念。什么是爱？爱的定义不是从人的感受来的，而是要看神在十字架上做了什么。我们的爱只是回应：「我们爱，因为神先爱我们。」（4:19）

**4. 「要叫你们知道自己有永生」。** 「我将这些话写给你们信奉神儿子之名的人，要叫你们知道自己有永生。」（5:13）全书的试验，最终是为了这句话。约翰不愿意信徒一直活在惧怕里，不知道自己到底有没有得救。永生不是靠我们的表现来维持的，因为「这永生也是在他儿子里面。人有了神的儿子就有生命」（5:11-12）。反过来说，「没有神的儿子就没有生命」（5:12）。所以真正的问题不是我够不够好，而是我有没有神的儿子。

## 全书结构

约翰一书的思路不像保罗书信那样一步一步往前推，而是像螺旋一样，几个主题（光、真理、爱、信、住在主里面）一再出现，每一次又往深处走一点。所以各家的分段不太一样。下面是一个简单的读法：
- **序言：生命之道显现了**（1:1-4）：使徒所听见、看见、摸过的，传给读者，使他们与使徒相交，也与父和子相交。
- **神就是光，要在光明中行**（1:5-2:29）：承认自己的罪；基督是中保和挽回祭；遵守他的诫命；爱弟兄；不要爱世界；敌基督的已经出来了；要住在主里面。
- **神的儿女要行义、彼此相爱**（3:1-24）：父的慈爱使我们得称为神的儿女；「凡从神生的，就不犯罪」；该隐和基督；在行为和诚实上相爱；我们的心若责备我们，神比我们的心大。
- **神就是爱，信的人得胜**（4:1-5:12）：试验诸灵；神差他的儿子作挽回祭；爱里没有惧怕；信的人胜过世界；水、血和圣灵的见证；有了子就有生命。
- **结语：我们知道**（5:13-21）：知道自己有永生；照神的旨意求，他就听我们；为犯罪的弟兄祷告；三次「我们知道」（5:18-20）；「你们要自守，远避偶像」。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 留意「住在」这个词。** 「住在」是约翰最爱用的词之一：信徒要「住在主里面」（2:28），神的道「常存在」他们心里（2:14），神「住在」我们里面（4:12-13）。和合本有时译作「住在」，有时译作「常存」「存在」，原文都是同一个词（*menō*）。耶稣在约翰福音15章用葡萄树的比喻讲过同一件事。这个词说的不是一时的感动，而是持续的、彼此的同在。

**2. 不要把试验读成自我审判的清单。** 这些试验一方面揭露那些说自己认识神、却不信、不顺服、不爱的人，另一方面也安慰那些心里软弱、常常自责的信徒：「我们的心若责备我们，神比我们的心大，一切事没有不知道的。」（3:20）读的时候，若只问自己够不够好，就会越读越惧怕。约翰要我们先看神做了什么（3:1；4:9-10），再看我们的生命里，神的工作已经结出了什么果子。这些果子是记号，不是根基；根基是那位为我们作挽回祭的基督（2:1-2）。约翰看的是生命的方向，不是毫无瑕疵（1:8）。若我看自己，只看见很少的果子，约翰不是叫我更用力地检查自己，而是叫我回到那位中保面前；爱和顺服，正是在他面前慢慢长出来的。

**3. 读到说信徒不犯罪的经文，留意上下文。** 约翰一面说「我们若说自己无罪，便是自欺」（1:8），一面又说「凡从神生的，就不犯罪」（3:9）。这两句话怎样一起成立？许多释经者认为，3:6、9的动词是现在式，说的是持续、习惯地活在罪中（例如斯托得），英文标准版也是照这个意思翻译的。也有人认为这个解释太倚重文法：5:16说弟兄「犯了」罪，用的也是现在式，可见单凭时态不能定案。他们认为约翰是在讲信徒在基督里的新身份，或是讲将来才完全实现的理想。卫斯理宗传统则用3:9来支持基督徒完全（完全成圣）的教导。第三章会再讲。无论怎样读，约翰的意思都不是说信徒已经不会犯罪，因为他明说「若有人犯罪，在父那里我们有一位中保」（2:1）。

**4. 读到5:7-8的时候，留意经文的差异。** 英文钦定本在这里多出一段话，意思是：在天上作见证的有三，就是父、道与圣灵，这三位是合一的。这段话一般称为约翰短句，早期的希腊文抄本都没有，只出现在少数很晚的抄本中，很可能原是拉丁文抄本里的旁注（最早见于四世纪的拉丁文著作）。伊拉斯谟在1522年第三版的希腊文新约里收进了这段话，后来英文钦定本就跟着他。和合本和多数现代译本都没有这段话。三位一体的教义并不建立在这一节上，而是建立在整本圣经的见证上（例如太28:19；林后13:14）。另外，和合本和英文标准版这几节的分节不同：和合本的5:7相当于英文标准版5:6的后半节，和合本的5:8相当于英文标准版的5:7-8。

**5. 对照约翰福音来读。** 约翰福音告诉我们，那「道成了肉身」的是谁（约1:14）；约翰一书告诉我们，认识这位道成肉身的主，在教会里、在日常生活中是什么样子。耶稣在最后的晚餐上赐给门徒一条「新命令」（约13:34），这命令在约翰一书里一再出现（2:7-8；3:23；4:21）。

## 默想

**默想：** 「我们将所看见、所听见的传给你们，使你们与我们相交。我们乃是与父并他儿子耶稣基督相交的。我们将这些话写给你们，使你们的喜乐充足。」（1:3-4）这封信的读者刚刚经历了一次分裂，一些一起聚会多年的人离开了（2:19）。许多教会也经历过这样的事：为了人事或意见分成两边，几个熟悉的家庭再也没有回来。留下来的人照常聚会、事奉，可是聚会里的喜乐少了，彼此之间多了小心和疲倦。约翰写信给这样一群人，开头却说，他写这些话，是要叫喜乐充足。不过，今天离开教会的人，不一定就像约翰所说的那些人。约翰那时的分裂，是为了耶稣是谁；今天有些人离开，是因为在这里受了伤，甚至受了欺压，也有人是为了正当的理由。我不能把2:19随手贴在他们身上。也许我自己就是离开的那一个。

约翰说的喜乐，不是从人多、气氛好来的，而是从「相交」来的；这相交有实实在在的根基。他先说的不是我们该做什么，而是那生命怎样向我们走过来：那「原与父同在」的永远的生命，「显现与我们」（1:2）；使徒们听见他，看见他，亲手摸过他（1:1）。这相交也不是靠我维持的：「他儿子耶稣的血也洗净我们一切的罪。」（1:7）我们能和父与子相交，不是因为我们比离开的人更好，也不是因为我们留了下来；是那位道成肉身、为我们流血的主，把我们带进了这相交里面。不管我是留下的，还是离开的，都是这样。为失去的弟兄姊妹难过，是对的。可是我也要诚实：我心里是不是对离开的人存着苦毒，或暗暗觉得自己比他们忠心？我是不是把喜乐寄托在教会的人数和气氛上，却忘了我们的相交首先是与父并他儿子的相交？这样的罪，那血也洗净了。

1:4还有一个细节。和合本写的是「使你们的喜乐充足」，和多数后期希腊文抄本一致；几份最早的抄本写的却是使**我们**的喜乐充足，英文标准版就是这样译的。若是后一种读法，约翰是说：你们若与我们同有这相交，我们的喜乐才算充足。年老的使徒把自己的喜乐和这些信徒连在一起。他的喜乐不在于他们留在他身边，而在于他们和他一同与父并子相交。喜乐不是一个人关起门来经营的，是和神的家一同领受的。

这喜乐最深的源头，是耶稣自己的喜乐。被卖的那一夜，他为门徒祷告说：「我还在世上说这话，是叫他们心里充满我的喜乐。」（约17:13）他说这话的时候，离十字架只有几个钟头。他所要给我们的，不是我们自己努力挤出来的高兴，而是他自己的喜乐，是在父的爱里那份永不改变的喜乐。他走上十字架，就是要把我们带进这份喜乐里：「我们乃是与父并他儿子耶稣基督相交的。」（1:3）

## What This Book Is About

1 John was written to believers who were shaken. Their church had just split: "They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us. But they went out, that it might become plain that they all are not of us" (2:19). Those who left were not outsiders; they had worshiped and prayed with them. They had a different account of Jesus, and probably claimed a deeper spiritual knowledge. Those who stayed could not help asking: Who is right? Is what we believe really the Christ proclaimed from the beginning? Do we really know God?

John writes to answer these questions. He states the purpose of the letter near its end: "I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God that you may know that you have eternal life" (5:13). John's Gospel was written "so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (John 20:31); 1 John is written to those who already believe, so that they may know they have that life. The verb "know" appears dozens of times in the letter.

John's answer is plain. He does not offer his readers a higher knowledge. He takes them back to what they heard "from the beginning" (1:1; 2:7, 24; 3:11). He keeps returning to three things:
- **Belief:** "Everyone who believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God" (5:1). The first test of true and false is how someone confesses Jesus: "every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (4:2).
- **Obedience:** "By this we know that we have come to know him, if we keep his commandments" (2:3). Whoever says he knows God but lives carelessly in sin "is a liar" (2:4).
- **Love:** "We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers" (3:14). Whoever says he loves God but hates his brother is also "a liar" (4:20).

More than a hundred years ago the Scottish scholar Robert Law wrote a book on the letter, *The Tests of Life*, built on these three, and John Stott later used the same three tests to explain the letter in his commentary. But the tests are not meant to drive readers away. John calls them "little children" and "beloved." He writes so that true believers may "reassure our heart before him" (3:19–20), and he wants those who have left the truth to be shown for what they are. The ground of it all is that God loved us first: "In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins" (4:10).

## Author and Setting

**1. What the letter says.** The letter gives no author's name and has no greeting. Yet the writer says clearly that he saw the Lord himself: "That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we looked upon and have touched with our hands, concerning the word of life" (1:1). He calls his readers "little children" (2:1, 12, 28), like an old pastor speaking to people he has cared for over many years. The writer of 2 and 3 John calls himself "the elder" (2 John 1; 3 John 1).

**2. Its link with John's Gospel.** The letter's vocabulary and thought are very close to John's Gospel: "from the beginning" and "in the beginning" (1:1; John 1:1), life and light (1:2, 5; John 1:4), truth, "abide," the "new commandment" (2:7–8; John 13:34), and God's "only Son" (4:9; John 3:16). Even the letter's purpose statement runs parallel to the Gospel's (5:13; John 20:31). Most scholars think the two books come from the same person, or at least from the same circle of churches.

**3. Who wrote it?** The early church held with one voice that the apostle John wrote it, the "disciple whom Jesus loved" (John 21:20, 24). Early in the second century Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, closely echoes 4:2–3 (compare 2 John 7) in *To the Philippians* 7; Eusebius reports that Papias also used the letter (*Church History* 3.39). At the end of the second century Irenaeus says plainly that John, the Lord's disciple, wrote it. Some scholars think the author was another John. Papias names John in a list of the Lord's disciples and then mentions "the elder John" (same passage). Eusebius took these to be two men, and some identify the second with "the elder" of 2 and 3 John; many others think Papias speaks of the same John twice. The Catholic scholar Raymond Brown and others think the letter came from a "Johannine community" that traced itself back to the Beloved Disciple (whom Brown did not identify with John son of Zebedee), and that its author was one of that community's teachers, not the evangelist himself. With scholars such as Stott, Kruse and Yarbrough, this study follows the traditional view that the apostle John wrote it. Even on other views, the writer clearly speaks as one of the eyewitnesses who saw the Lord (1:1–3).

**4. When and where?** Irenaeus reports that John lived his last years in Ephesus in Asia Minor and lived on into the reign of Trajan (from AD 98). Most think the letter was written after John's Gospel, around AD 85–95, to churches around Ephesus.

**5. What did those who left teach?** The letter gives no full description of the error; we can only infer it from John's answers. They seem to have:
- **Denied that Jesus is the Christ:** "Who is the liar but he who denies that Jesus is the Christ?" (2:22). Perhaps they did not deny that there was a "Christ," but denied that the historical man Jesus was the Christ.
- **Denied that Christ came in the flesh:** they "do not confess the coming of Jesus Christ in the flesh" (2 John 7; see 1 John 4:2–3).
- **Made light of sin:** the three "if we say" claims (1:6, 8, 10) may echo their slogans: claiming fellowship with God and claiming to have no sin.
- **Not loved the brothers:** "Whoever says he is in the light and hates his brother" (2:9) may also have them in view.

Many connect them with second-century Gnosticism, which held that matter is inferior and that salvation comes through secret "knowledge." Some (called Docetists) held that Christ only seemed to have a body. Irenaeus also reports that a contemporary of John named Cerinthus taught that "the Christ" came down on Jesus at his baptism and left him before his suffering, so that only the man Jesus suffered and died (*Against Heresies* 1.26.1). That would explain why John stresses, "This is he who came by water and blood—Jesus Christ; not by the water only but by the water and the blood" (5:6). Irenaeus also passes on a story that, he says, others heard from Polycarp: once in Ephesus John went into the bathhouse, heard that Cerinthus was inside, and ran straight out (*Against Heresies* 3.3.4). Still, 1 John need not be aimed at Cerinthus himself, or at the Gnosticism that later took shape; it may be answering early forms of those ideas.

## Key Verses: 1:9; 2:1–2; 4:10; 5:13

**1. "If we confess our sins."** "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1:9). Three times John says "if we say" (1:6, 8, 10) to expose false claims. The way he gives believers is not to pretend to be sinless, but to confess. God forgives those who confess not only because he is merciful, but because he is "just": Christ has borne sin's penalty in our place (1:7; 2:2). Forgiveness rests on Christ's blood, not on how complete my confessing is; to confess is to come back into the light, not to pay God what I still owe.

**2. "We have an advocate with the Father."** "My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin. But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous. He is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world" (2:1–2). The word "advocate" (*paraklētos*) is the word John's Gospel uses for the "Helper" (John 14:16); it means one who stands beside another and speaks for him. (It is not the same Greek word as "one mediator" in 1 Tim 2:5.) "Propitiation" means a sacrifice that turns away God's wrath against sin and so brings people back to God; God's justice is satisfied, so sinners can be forgiven. This propitiation is God's own provision in love (4:10). So a believer who has sinned need not first defend himself or hide from God; the Righteous One is already speaking for us before the Father. Readers differ on what "the sins of the whole world" means; chapter 2 returns to this.

**3. "In this is love."** "In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins" (4:10). John twice says "God is love" (4:8, 16), but he never lets "love" become a vague idea. What is love? We do not define it from our feelings; we look at what God did at the cross. Our love is only a response: "We love because he first loved us" (4:19).

**4. "That you may know that you have eternal life."** "I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God that you may know that you have eternal life" (5:13). Every test in the letter serves this sentence. John does not want believers to live in constant fear of not knowing whether they are saved. Eternal life is not kept up by our performance, because "this life is in his Son. Whoever has the Son has life" (5:11–12). Conversely, "whoever does not have the Son of God does not have life" (5:12). The real question is not whether I am good enough, but whether I have the Son.

## Structure of the Book

1 John does not move forward step by step like Paul's letters. It moves in a spiral: a few themes (light, truth, love, faith, abiding) keep coming back, each time a little deeper. So commentators divide it in different ways. Here is a simple way to read it:
- **Prologue: the word of life made manifest** (1:1–4): what the apostles heard, saw and touched they proclaim, so that the readers may have fellowship with them, and with the Father and the Son.
- **God is light; walk in the light** (1:5–2:29): confessing our sins; Christ our advocate and propitiation; keeping his commandments; loving the brothers; not loving the world; the antichrists who have gone out; abiding in him.
- **God's children practice righteousness and love one another** (3:1–24): the Father's love makes us his children; those born of God do not keep on sinning; Cain and Christ; love in deed and in truth; when our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart.
- **God is love; those who believe overcome** (4:1–5:12): testing the spirits; God sent his Son as propitiation; no fear in love; faith overcomes the world; the testimony of the Spirit, the water and the blood; whoever has the Son has life.
- **Conclusion: we know** (5:13–21): knowing we have eternal life; he hears us when we ask according to his will; praying for a brother who sins; three times "we know" (5:18–20); "keep yourselves from idols."

## How to Read This Book

**1. Notice the word "abide."** "Abide" is one of John's favorite words: believers are to "abide in him" (2:28), the word of God "abides" in them (2:14), God "abides" in us (4:12–13). The Chinese Union Version sometimes translates it "live in" and sometimes "remain," but in Greek it is the same word (*menō*). Jesus spoke of the same thing with the vine in John 15. The word is not about a passing feeling; it means a lasting, mutual presence.

**2. Do not read the tests as a checklist for judging yourself.** John's tests cut two ways. They expose those who say they know God but do not believe, obey or love. They also comfort weak believers who often condemn themselves: "for whenever our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything" (3:20). If we read asking only, "Am I good enough?", we will grow more afraid with every page. John wants us to look first at what God has done (3:1; 4:9–10), and then at the fruit God's work has already produced in our lives. These fruits are signs, not the foundation; the foundation is Christ, the propitiation for our sins (2:1–2). John is looking at the direction of a life, not at a spotless record (1:8). If I look at myself and see little fruit, John does not send me to inspect myself harder; he sends me back to the advocate, and it is before him that love and obedience grow.

**3. When you read that believers do not sin, notice the context.** John says both "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1:8) and "No one born of God makes a practice of sinning" (3:9). How can both be true? Many commentators note that the verbs in 3:6 and 3:9 are present tense and take them to describe ongoing, habitual life in sin (for example, Stott; the ESV translates "keeps on sinning," "makes a practice of sinning"). Others think this leans too much on grammar: 5:16 uses the same present tense of a brother sinning, so the tense alone cannot settle it. They take John to be speaking of the believer's new identity in Christ, or of the ideal that will be complete at the end. The Wesleyan tradition read 3:9 in support of Christian perfection (entire sanctification). Chapter 3 returns to this. On any reading, John does not mean believers can no longer sin, for he says plainly, "if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father" (2:1).

**4. At 5:7–8, notice the difference between Bibles.** The King James Version has extra words here: "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." This is called the "Johannine Comma." No early Greek manuscript has it; it appears only in a few very late manuscripts and probably began as a gloss in Latin manuscripts (it is first found in Latin writers of the fourth century). Erasmus put it into the third edition of his Greek New Testament in 1522, and the King James Version followed him. The Chinese Union Version and most modern translations do not have it. The doctrine of the Trinity does not rest on this verse; it rests on the witness of the whole Bible (for example, Matt 28:19; 2 Cor 13:14). Note too that the Chinese Union Version and the ESV number these verses differently: CUV 5:7 matches the second half of ESV 5:6, and CUV 5:8 matches ESV 5:7–8.

**5. Read it alongside John's Gospel.** John's Gospel tells us who "the Word became flesh" is (John 1:14); 1 John tells us what knowing this incarnate Lord looks like in the church and in daily life. The "new commandment" Jesus gave at the Last Supper (John 13:34) becomes, in 1 John, a command the letter keeps coming back to (2:7–8; 3:23; 4:21).

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "That which we have seen and heard we proclaim also to you, so that you too may have fellowship with us; and indeed our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ. And we are writing these things so that our joy may be complete" (1:3–4). The first readers of this letter had just been through a split; people they had worshiped with for years had left (2:19). Many churches know this: a division over leaders or opinions, and several familiar families who never come back. Those who stay keep worshiping and serving, but the joy has drained out of the gatherings, and caution and weariness have taken its place. To people like these John writes, and at the very start he says he is writing so that joy may be complete. Still, those who leave a church today are not necessarily like the people John describes. The split in his day was over who Jesus is; some leave today because they were hurt here, even mistreated, and some leave for good reasons. I must not pin 2:19 on them. Perhaps I am the one who left.

The joy John speaks of does not come from a full room or a warm atmosphere. It comes from "fellowship," and that fellowship rests on something solid. John does not begin with what we should do, but with how that life came to us: the eternal life "which was with the Father," "was made manifest to us" (1:2); the apostles heard him, saw him, and touched him with their hands (1:1). Nor is the fellowship kept up by me: "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1:7). We have fellowship with the Father and the Son not because we are better than those who left, nor because we stayed; the Lord who became flesh and shed his blood for us brought us into it. That is true whether I stayed or left. It is right to grieve the brothers and sisters we have lost. But I must also be honest: do I hold bitterness toward those who left, or quietly think myself more faithful than they are? Have I rested my joy on the size and mood of the church, and forgotten that our fellowship is first of all with the Father and his Son? That blood cleanses these sins too.

There is one more detail in 1:4. The Chinese Union Version says "that *your* joy may be complete," agreeing with most later Greek manuscripts; several of the earliest manuscripts read "our joy," and the ESV translates it that way. On that reading, John is saying: when you share this fellowship with us, then our joy is complete. The old apostle ties his own joy to these believers. His joy lies not in their loyalty to him but in their sharing with him the fellowship of the Father and the Son. Joy is not something one person works up behind a closed door; we receive it together with God's family.

The deepest source of this joy is Jesus' own joy. On the night he was betrayed, he prayed for his disciples: "these things I speak in the world, that they may have my joy fulfilled in themselves" (John 17:13). He said this only hours before the cross. What he gives us is not a gladness we squeeze out by effort. It is his own joy, the unchanging joy of the Son in the Father's love. He went to the cross to bring us into that joy: "our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ" (1:3).
