## 这卷书在讲什么

彼得前书是写给一群受苦的信徒的。他们没有被关进监狱，也还没有大批地殉道，可是他们的日子不好过：邻舍「毁谤你们是作恶的」（2:12），从前一起吃喝玩乐的朋友「见你们不与他们同奔那放荡无度的路，就以为怪，毁谤你们」（4:4），作仆人的遭主人无理苛待（2:18-20），作妻子的嫁给不信主的丈夫（3:1）。彼得说，他们是「寄居的」（1:1、17；2:11），在自己的城里，却像外人一样。今天许多华人信徒也有同样的经历：信主后不再拜祖先、不随从节庆的拜拜，就被亲戚说成不孝。

彼得怎样安慰他们？他一开头不是先讲苦难，而是先赞美神：「愿颂赞归与我们主耶稣基督的父神！他曾照自己的大怜悯，藉耶稣基督从死里复活，重生了我们，叫我们有活泼的盼望。」（1:3）全书反复交织着三个主题：
- **盼望：** 信徒有一份「不能朽坏、不能玷污、不能衰残」的基业，存留在天上（1:4）；现在的苦难是「暂时」的（1:6；参5:10「暂受」）。
- **身份：** 被世人看不起的信徒，在神眼中是「被拣选的族类，是有君尊的祭司，是圣洁的国度，是属神的子民」（2:9）。
- **基督的受苦：** 彼得一再把信徒的苦难和基督的苦难连在一起：「基督也为你们受过苦，给你们留下榜样」（2:21）；「他被挂在木头上，亲身担当了我们的罪」（2:24）；「基督也曾一次为罪受苦，就是义的代替不义的」（3:18）。但基督的受苦首先是独一无二的：只有他能担当我们的罪，义的代替不义的。正因为他先替我们受了苦，他的脚踪才成了我们这些蒙赎之人当跟随的榜样（2:21）。

所以可以说，在新约书信中，彼得前书最完整地教导信徒怎样在苦难中生活。它不叫人逃避苦难，也不叫人默默忍受一切，而是叫人在苦难中看见基督，盼望荣耀，继续行善。彼得自己总结说：「我略略地写了这信……劝勉你们，又证明这恩是神的真恩。你们务要在这恩上站立得住。」（5:12）

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 使徒彼得。** 信一开头就说是「耶稣基督的使徒彼得」写的（1:1），后来他又称自己是「作长老、作基督受苦的见证」的（5:1）。彼得原名西门，是加利利的渔夫，耶稣给他起名叫矶法，就是彼得（「石头」的意思，约1:42）。他是十二使徒中常常第一个说话的人，也是在五旬节第一个公开传讲福音的人（徒2:14-41）。

**2. 谁执笔？** 许多人认为这封信的希腊文写得相当优美，引用旧约多半按七十士译本，因此怀疑一个被人看为「没有学问的小民」（徒4:13）的渔夫，能否写出这样的文字。不少学者（如阿克特迈尔、艾略特）认为，这是彼得死后，罗马教会中他的门生借他的名字写的（常定在主后70-95年）；也有许多学者（如凯伦·乔布斯、施莱纳）认为，这封信出于彼得本人：
- **希腊文：** 一世纪的加利利通行希腊文。彼得是伯赛大人（约1:44），又作了三十年使徒，四处传道，很可能早已能说流利的希腊文。「没有学问」多半是说他没有受过拉比的训练，不是说他不识字。乔布斯从文法分析指出，这封信的希腊文带有闪族语言的痕迹，正合乎彼得这样母语不是希腊文的作者。
- **西拉的角色：** 彼得说「我略略地写了这信，托……西拉转交你们」（5:12）。原文直译是藉着西拉写的。当时的书信这样说，通常是指送信的人，伊格那丢和坡旅甲的书信都是这样用。西拉也曾把耶路撒冷会议的信送到安提阿（徒15:22-23）。所以许多学者（如理查兹、乔布斯）认为西拉是送信的人，和合本译作「转交」，也是这个意思。也有人认为西拉还帮彼得执笔、润饰文字（如塞尔温）。两种角色也可能兼有。西拉（这里的希腊文是 *Silouanos*，是「西拉」较正式的写法）是耶路撒冷教会的领袖，曾和保罗一同传道（徒15:22、40）。
- **早期的见证：** 彼得后书已经提到这封信：「我现在写给你们的是第二封信。」（彼后3:1；也有人认为这里指的是另一封信）二世纪初，帕皮亚已经引用过这封信（见优西比乌《教会史》第三卷第三十九章）；士每拿的主教坡旅甲在《致腓立比人书》中也多次引用。二世纪末，爱任纽第一个指名引用彼得前书（《驳异端》第四卷第九章）。到了四世纪，优西比乌把这封信列为教会公认、没有争议的书卷（《教会史》第三卷第三章、第二十五章）。

本研读采用彼得本人所写的看法。

**3. 在哪里写的？** 彼得说：「在巴比伦与你们同蒙拣选的教会问你们安。」（5:13）
- **罗马：** 多数学者认为，「巴比伦」是罗马的代称。旧约的巴比伦是掳掠神百姓的大城，启示录也用「大巴比伦」称呼那逼迫圣徒的城（启17:5；18:2）。早期教会的传统也说彼得最后在罗马。二世纪初的帕皮亚已经说，彼得是在罗马写这封信的，并且用「巴比伦」比喻那城（见优西比乌《教会史》第二卷第十五章）；彼得称为「我儿子」的马可，那时也在罗马（西4:10；门24）。和合本的「教会」二字是补上的；原文只用一个阴性的代词，多数人认为指的就是教会。
- **真正的巴比伦：** 少数人（包括加尔文）认为是指美索不达米亚的巴比伦（那时已经很荒凉），或埃及的一个同名军营，但支持的证据很少。

**4. 什么时候写的？** 教会传统说，彼得在尼禄作皇帝的时候，在罗马殉道（约主后64-68年；参约21:18-19）。若是彼得本人所写，这封信必定写在他殉道以前，大约在主后60年代初到中期。许多学者（如施莱纳）定在主后62-64年，也有人定得稍晚。那时可能正是主后64年罗马大火以后、尼禄开始逼迫罗马基督徒的前后。认为是别人借名写的学者，多定在主后70-95年，理由之一是用「巴比伦」称呼罗马，多见于主后70年耶路撒冷被毁以后的作品。信中所说的苦难，多半是社会的排斥和毁谤，还不是官方有系统的逼迫。

**5. 收信人是谁？** 收信人住在「本都、加拉太、加帕多家、亚细亚、庇推尼」（1:1），就是今天土耳其的北部、中部和西部一大片地区。
- **多半是外邦信徒：** 彼得说他们从前「蒙昧无知」（1:14），是从「祖宗所传流虚妄的行为」中被赎出来的（1:18），又说他们从前随从外邦人拜偶像（4:3）。这些话不太像是对犹太人说的。从前有人（如俄利根、加尔文）认为收信人是分散各地的犹太信徒；今天多数学者认为，这些话更像是对外邦人说的。教会中可能犹太人和外邦人都有。
- **却用以色列的称呼：** 彼得把旧约给以色列的名号用在他们身上：「分散」「寄居的」（1:1），「被拣选的族类……属神的子民」（2:9）。他们本来「算不得子民」，现在却作了神的子民（2:10）。
- **「寄居的」：** 有学者（如艾略特）认为，这些人在社会上本来就是外来的、没有公民权的人；多数学者（如施莱纳、阿克特迈尔）认为，这主要是属灵的意思：信主以后，他们在自己的城里成了外人，他们真正的家在天上。乔布斯则认为，有些收信人可能真是被迁移到那里定居的。彼得在2:11说「客旅」「寄居的」，是回应亚伯拉罕的话：「我在你们中间是外人，是寄居的」（创23:4；参诗39:12；来11:13）。几种意思也可能都有。

## 钥匙经文：1:3-5；2:9-10；5:10

**1. 「活泼的盼望」。** 「愿颂赞归与我们主耶稣基督的父神！他曾照自己的大怜悯，藉耶稣基督从死里复活，重生了我们，叫我们有活泼的盼望，可以得着不能朽坏、不能玷污、不能衰残、为你们存留在天上的基业。你们这因信蒙神能力保守的人，必能得着所预备、到末世要显现的救恩。」（1:3-5）这盼望是「活泼的」，因为它建立在一位活着的主身上：耶稣基督已经从死里复活了。基业存留在天上，我们也「因信蒙神能力保守」在地上。

**2. 「属神的子民」。** 「惟有你们是被拣选的族类，是有君尊的祭司，是圣洁的国度，是属神的子民，要叫你们宣扬那召你们出黑暗入奇妙光明者的美德。你们从前算不得子民，现在却作了神的子民；从前未曾蒙怜恤，现在却蒙了怜恤。」（2:9-10）被邻舍看不起的人，在神眼中是他的珍宝。信徒的身份不是从社会来的，而是从神的怜悯来的。

**3. 「那赐诸般恩典的神」。** 「那赐诸般恩典的神曾在基督里召你们，得享他永远的荣耀，等你们暂受苦难之后，必要亲自成全你们，坚固你们，赐力量给你们。」（5:10）苦难是「暂受」的，荣耀是「永远」的；而那位召我们的神，要「亲自」把我们带到底。

## 全书结构

彼得前书的思路，是先讲神为我们做了什么，再讲我们在世上怎样活。大致可以这样分：
- **问安**（1:1-2）：写给分散寄居、蒙父拣选、圣灵成圣、基督宝血所洒的人；一开头就提到三位一体的神。
- **活泼的盼望和圣洁的生活**（1:3-2:10）：神藉基督复活重生了我们；在试炼中仍有喜乐；要圣洁，因为神是圣洁的；我们是用基督的宝血赎回来的；要彼此相爱；要爱慕灵奶；我们是活石，被建造成为灵宫，是神的子民。
- **在世寄居的日子**（2:11-4:11）：在外邦人中品行端正；顺服人间的制度；仆人顺服主人，跟随基督受苦的脚踪；妻子和丈夫；不以恶报恶；为义受苦的有福了；基督义的代替不义的；不再随从外邦人的私欲；万物的结局近了，要彼此相爱、互相款待、按恩赐服事。
- **火炼的试验和最后的劝勉**（4:12-5:14）：与基督一同受苦，不要以为奇怪；长老要牧养神的群羊；要谦卑，将忧虑卸给神；要抵挡魔鬼；那赐诸般恩典的神必亲自成全你们；问安。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 留意「因为」和「所以」。** 彼得的命令总是建立在神的作为上。他先说神「重生了我们」（1:3），然后说「所以要约束你们的心」（1:13）；1:17「存敬畏的心度你们在世寄居的日子」的命令，是建立在「知道你们得赎」（1:18-19）上。读的时候，可以先找出每一段的「因为」，再看「所以」。

**2. 对照旧约来读。** 彼得引用旧约很多：以赛亚书40章的「凡有血气的，尽都如草」（1:24），诗篇118篇和以赛亚书28章的「房角石」（2:6-8），出埃及记19章的「祭司的国度」（2:9），以赛亚书53章的受苦仆人（2:22-25），诗篇34篇（3:10-12），箴言（4:18；5:5），还有利未记的「你们要圣洁，因为我是圣洁的」（1:16）和以赛亚书8:14的「绊脚的石头」（2:8）。彼得把旧约称呼以色列的名号用在基督里的教会身上。许多人（特别是改革宗传统）认为这些应许在教会身上得着应验；也有人（如时代论者）认为，教会分享这些名号，并没有取消神给以色列民族的应许（罗11:26-29）。

**3. 苦难不是一个题目，而是一个处境。** 彼得没有写一篇讨论为什么有苦难的文章，而是对正在受苦的人说话。他分辨两种苦难：因犯罪受苦（2:20；4:15）和因行善、为基督的名受苦（3:17；4:14）。前者要悔改，而悔改的人可以放心，因为基督正是「义的代替不义的」（3:18）；后者有福。

**4. 读到「顺服」的时候，看清楚处境。** 彼得叫信徒顺服君王和臣宰（2:13-14），叫仆人顺服主人（2:18），叫妻子顺服丈夫（3:1）。历代教会对这些经文有正确的应用，也有人拿它们来压制人。彼得说这一切是「为主的缘故」（2:13），是对「自由的」、「作神的仆人」的人说的（2:16）；他同时说「务要尊敬众人」（2:17），叫妻子「不因恐吓而害怕」（3:6），叫丈夫敬重妻子，因为她是「与你一同承受生命之恩的」，否则祷告会有阻碍（3:7），又叫众人「彼此顺服」（5:5）。彼得是对当时在法律上无法脱身的家仆说话，这不是认可奴隶制度。保罗也说：「若能以自由，就求自由更好。」（林前7:21）顺服从来不是叫人顺从别人去犯罪（徒5:29）；受害的人不必留在危险中；向警察或法院举报虐待和犯罪是对的（罗13:4）；教会绝不可因为「家丑不可外扬」而遮掩虐待。本研读在相关的章节会仔细讲这一点。

## 默想

**默想：** 「愿颂赞归与我们主耶稣基督的父神！他曾照自己的大怜悯，藉耶稣基督从死里复活，重生了我们，叫我们有活泼的盼望。」（1:3）写这封信的彼得，曾经是很不能接受苦难的人。耶稣第一次说他「必须……受……许多的苦，并且被杀」的时候，彼得把他拉到一边，劝他说：「主啊，万不可如此！这事必不临到你身上。」（太16:21-22）耶稣却对他说：「撒但，退我后边去吧！……你不体贴神的意思，只体贴人的意思。」（太16:23）我心里也常有彼得的那句话：主啊，万不可如此。我喜欢一位得胜的主，却不太愿意要一位被钉十字架的主，更不愿意自己也走那条路。此刻，我心里对神说「万不可如此」的，是哪一件事？可是我很少想过：那天耶稣若听了彼得的劝，彼得就没有救主了，我也没有。耶稣没有听他的，照样去了，「义的代替不义的，为要引我们到神面前」（3:18）。几十年后，这个曾经说「万不可如此」的彼得，写了一封通篇讲苦难的信，基督的受苦贯穿全信（1:11、19；2:21-24；3:18；4:13）。是什么改变了他？是那位他曾经劝阻不要去受苦的主，真的去受了苦，又真的从死里复活了。彼得作了基督受苦的见证（5:1），又见了复活的主（路24:34），所以他才说「活泼的盼望」。我的盼望若只是盼望今生不受苦，苦难一来就会破碎；可是建立在复活的基督身上的盼望，苦难夺不走。我不必假装不怕苦，彼得也怕过，怕到三次不认主。可是那位主没有放弃他。苦难来的时候，我也可以和他一样，在开口问别的以前，先赞美那位父神：他照自己的大怜悯，藉着耶稣基督从死里复活，已经重生了我。我的基业存留在天上，而这基业最宝贵的，是我要见到那位我「虽然没有见过……却是爱他」的主（1:8）。

## What This Book Is About

1 Peter is written to a group of suffering believers. They had not been thrown into prison, nor were they yet being martyred in large numbers, but life was hard. Neighbors "speak against you as evildoers" (2:12); old friends who used to party with them "are surprised when you do not join them in the same flood of debauchery, and they malign you" (4:4); servants were treated unjustly by their masters (2:18–20); wives were married to husbands who did not believe (3:1). Peter calls them "exiles" (1:1, 17; 2:11): in their own towns, they lived like outsiders. Many Chinese believers know this: once they stop joining ancestral rites, relatives call them unfilial.

How does Peter comfort them? He does not begin with suffering but with praise: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1:3). Three themes weave through the whole letter:
- **Hope:** believers have "an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven" (1:4); present suffering is "for a little while" (1:6; 5:10).
- **Identity:** believers whom the world looks down on are, in God's eyes, "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession" (2:9).
- **Christ's suffering:** Peter keeps joining the believers' suffering to Christ's: "Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example" (2:21); "He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (2:24); "Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous" (3:18). Christ's suffering is first of all unique: he alone bore our sins, once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous; because he suffered in our place, his steps also become the pattern for those he has redeemed (2:21).

So 1 Peter could be called the New Testament's fullest letter on how to live in suffering. It does not tell people to escape suffering, nor to bear everything in silence, but to see Christ in suffering, hope for glory, and go on doing good. Peter sums it up himself: "I have written briefly to you, exhorting and declaring that this is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it" (5:12).

## Author and Setting

**1. The apostle Peter.** The letter opens, "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ" (1:1), and later he calls himself "a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ" (5:1). Peter's name was Simon, a fisherman from Galilee; Jesus named him Cephas, that is, Peter ("rock," John 1:42). He was often the first of the Twelve to speak, and the first to preach the gospel publicly at Pentecost (Acts 2:14–41).

**2. Who held the pen?** Many regard the letter's Greek as quite polished, and its Old Testament quotations mostly follow the Septuagint, so some doubt that a fisherman regarded as one of the "uneducated, common men" (Acts 4:13) could write it. Many scholars (for example, Paul Achtemeier and John Elliott) think the letter came from Peter's followers in Rome, written in his name after his death (often dated AD 70–95). Many others (for example, Karen Jobes and Thomas Schreiner) hold that the letter comes from Peter himself:
- **Greek was widely used in first-century Galilee,** Peter came from Bethsaida (John 1:44), and after thirty years as an apostle, preaching everywhere, he may well have become fluent. "Uneducated" probably means he had no rabbinic training, not that he could not read or write. Jobes argues, from its grammar, that the Greek shows traces of a Semitic first language, which fits Peter.
- **Silvanus's role:** Peter says, "By Silvanus... I have written briefly to you" (5:12). In letters of the time, "through [name] I wrote" usually named the person who carried the letter (so Ignatius and Polycarp; Silas also carried the Jerusalem letter, Acts 15:22–23). Many therefore take Silvanus as the courier (for example, E. Randolph Richards; Jobes); others think he also helped write and polish it (for example, E. G. Selwyn). The two roles do not exclude each other. Silvanus (Greek *Silouanos*, the fuller form of Silas) was a leader in the Jerusalem church who traveled with Paul (Acts 15:22, 40).
- **Early witness:** 2 Peter already mentions this letter: "This is now the second letter that I am writing to you" (2 Pet 3:1). (Some think that verse refers to another letter.) Early in the second century Papias used it (Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.17), and Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, quoted 1 Peter several times in his letter to the Philippians; late in that century Irenaeus was the first to quote it by name (*Against Heresies* 4.9.2); in the fourth century Eusebius listed it among the books the church accepted without dispute (*Church History* 3.3; 3.25).

This study follows the view that Peter himself wrote it.

**3. Written from where?** Peter says, "She who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen, sends you greetings" (5:13).
- **Rome:** most scholars take "Babylon" as a code name for Rome. The Babylon of the Old Testament was the great city that carried God's people into exile, and Revelation calls the city that persecutes the saints "Babylon the great" (Rev 17:5; 18:2). Early church tradition also places Peter in Rome at the end of his life. Papias (early second century, reported by Eusebius, *Church History* 2.15.2) already says Peter wrote this letter in Rome and called the city "Babylon." Mark, whom Peter calls "my son" (5:13), was also in Rome (Col 4:10; Phlm 24). The Greek says simply "she"; most take it to mean the church.
- **Literal Babylon:** a few (Calvin among them) take it as Babylon in Mesopotamia (by then largely deserted), or a military camp of the same name in Egypt, but there is little evidence for either.

**4. When?** Church tradition says Peter was martyred in Rome under the emperor Nero (around AD 64–68; see John 21:18–19). If Peter wrote it, the letter must come before his death, probably in the early to mid-60s (many, such as Schreiner, suggest about AD 62–64; others a little later), perhaps around the time Nero began persecuting Christians in Rome (after the great fire of AD 64). Those who think it was written in Peter's name date it AD 70–95, partly because "Babylon" as a name for Rome is found mostly in writings after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. The suffering the letter describes is mostly social rejection and slander, not yet systematic official persecution.

**5. To whom?** The readers lived in "Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (1:1), a large area of the northern, central and western parts of what is now Turkey.
- **Mostly Gentile believers:** Peter speaks of their "former ignorance" (1:14), says they were ransomed from "the futile ways inherited from your forefathers" (1:18), and says they once lived as the Gentiles do, in idolatry (4:3). These words fit Jewish readers poorly. Some older interpreters (Origen, Calvin) took the readers to be Jewish believers, but most today think these words fit Gentiles better; the churches were probably mixed.
- **Yet called by Israel's names:** Peter uses the Old Testament's names for Israel: "the dispersion," "exiles" (1:1), "a chosen race... a people for his own possession" (2:9). Once they "were not a people," but now they are God's people (2:10).
- **"Exiles":** some scholars (for example, John Elliott) think these readers were already social outsiders without citizenship. Most (for example, Schreiner and Achtemeier) take it mainly in a spiritual sense: after coming to faith, they became outsiders in their own towns, because their true home is in heaven. Jobes suggests some readers may literally have been resettled from elsewhere. Peter's pair "sojourners and exiles" (2:11) echoes Abraham: "I am a sojourner and foreigner among you" (Gen 23:4; see Ps 39:12; Heb 11:13). More than one sense may be present.

## Key Verses: 1:3–5; 2:9–10; 5:10

**1. "A living hope."** "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, to an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for you, who by God's power are being guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time" (1:3–5). This hope is "living" because it rests on a living Lord: Jesus Christ has risen from the dead. The inheritance is kept in heaven, and we are "guarded through faith" by God's power on earth.

**2. "A people for his own possession."** "But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light. Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy" (2:9–10). Those their neighbors looked down on are God's treasure. A believer's identity does not come from society but from God's mercy.

**3. "The God of all grace."** "And after you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ, will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you" (5:10). The suffering is "a little while"; the glory is "eternal"; and the God who called us will "himself" bring us through.

## Structure of the Book

1 Peter first tells what God has done for us, then how we are to live in the world. One simple outline:
- **Greeting** (1:1–2): to exiles of the dispersion, chosen by the Father, sanctified by the Spirit, sprinkled with Christ's blood; the triune God is named from the start.
- **A living hope and a holy life** (1:3–2:10): God has caused us to be born again through Christ's resurrection; joy in trials; be holy, for God is holy; we were ransomed with Christ's precious blood; love one another; long for spiritual milk; we are living stones built into a spiritual house, God's people.
- **The time of our exile** (2:11–4:11): honorable conduct among the Gentiles; submission to human institutions; servants and masters, following in Christ's steps of suffering; wives and husbands; not repaying evil for evil; blessed are those who suffer for righteousness; Christ the righteous for the unrighteous; no longer living for human passions; the end of all things is at hand, so love, show hospitality and serve with your gifts.
- **The fiery trial and final appeals** (4:12–5:14): sharing Christ's sufferings, not surprised; elders are to shepherd God's flock; humility, casting anxieties on God; resisting the devil; the God of all grace will himself restore you; greetings.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Watch for "because" and "therefore."** Peter's commands always rest on what God has done. He first says God "has caused us to be born again" (1:3), then "Therefore, preparing your minds for action" (1:13); the command of 1:17, "conduct yourselves with fear throughout the time of your exile," rests on "knowing that you were ransomed" (1:18–19). As you read, look for each passage's "because" before its "therefore."

**2. Keep the Old Testament open.** Peter quotes it often: Isaiah 40's "All flesh is like grass" (1:24); the "cornerstone" of Psalm 118 and Isaiah 28 (2:6–8); the "kingdom of priests" of Exodus 19 (2:9); Isaiah 53's suffering servant (2:22–25); Psalm 34 (3:10–12); Proverbs (4:18; 5:5); also Leviticus's "You shall be holy, for I am holy" (1:16) and the stumbling stone of Isaiah 8:14 (2:8). Peter applies Israel's titles to the church in Christ. Many (for example, the Reformed tradition) see the church as the fulfillment of these promises; others (for example, dispensational interpreters) hold that the church shares these titles without cancelling God's promises to ethnic Israel (Rom 11:26–29).

**3. Suffering is not a topic but a setting.** Peter does not write an essay on "why there is suffering"; he speaks to people who are suffering. He distinguishes two kinds of suffering: suffering for doing wrong (2:20; 4:15) and suffering for doing good, for the name of Christ (3:17; 4:14). The first calls for repentance, and the one who repents can take heart, because Christ suffered precisely as "the righteous for the unrighteous" (3:18); the second is blessed.

**4. When you read "be subject," look carefully at the setting.** Peter tells believers to be subject to the emperor and governors (2:13–14), servants to their masters (2:18), wives to their husbands (3:1). Through the centuries these texts have been rightly applied, and also wrongly used to crush people. Peter says all this is "for the Lord's sake" (2:13). It is given to people who are "free" and "servants of God" (2:16), and the letter also says "Honor everyone" (2:17). He tells wives to "not fear anything that is frightening" (3:6). He tells husbands to honor their wives as "heirs with you of the grace of life," or their prayers will be hindered (3:7). He calls all believers to humility "toward one another" (5:5). Peter is speaking to household slaves who had no legal way out; he does not approve of slavery, and where freedom can be had, it is better (1 Cor 7:21). Submission never means following anyone into sin (Acts 5:29). A victim does not have to stay in danger, and reporting abuse or crime to the police or courts is right (Rom 13:4). The church must never hide abuse to protect its reputation. This study will look at this carefully in the relevant chapters.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1:3). The Peter who wrote this letter was once a man who could hardly accept suffering. The first time Jesus said he "must... suffer many things... and be killed," Peter took him aside and rebuked him: "Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you" (Matt 16:21–22). Jesus said to him, "Get behind me, Satan!... you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man" (Matt 16:23). My heart often says Peter's words too: far be it from you, Lord. I like a Lord who wins; I am less eager for a Lord who is crucified, and less eager still to walk that road myself. What is the one thing I am saying "far be it from you" about right now? Yet I rarely stop to think: if Jesus had taken Peter's advice that day, Peter would have had no Savior, and neither would I. Jesus did not take it. He went on, "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (3:18). Decades later, the Peter who once said "Far be it from you" wrote a letter about suffering from beginning to end, and Christ's suffering runs through the whole letter (1:11, 19; 2:21–24; 3:18; 4:13). What changed him? The Lord he had tried to keep from suffering really did suffer, and really did rise from the dead. Peter was a witness of Christ's sufferings (5:1) and met the risen Lord (Luke 24:34), and that is why he speaks of a "living hope." If my hope is only that I will not suffer in this life, it will break when suffering comes; but a hope built on the risen Christ cannot be taken away by suffering. I do not need to pretend I am not afraid of suffering; Peter was afraid too, afraid enough to deny his Lord three times, and that Lord did not give him up. When suffering comes, I can do what he did: before I ask anything else, bless the God and Father who, according to his great mercy, has already caused me to be born again through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead. My inheritance is kept in heaven, and its greatest treasure is that I will see the One whom, "though you have not seen him, you love" (1:8).
