## 本章脉络

第五章是全书的最后一章，可以分成四段。**给长老的劝勉**（1-4节）：彼得自称是同作长老的，也是作基督受苦见证的；他劝长老们牧养神的群羊，不是出于勉强，乃是出于甘心；不是因为贪财，乃是出于乐意；不是辖制人，乃是作群羊的榜样；到了牧长显现的时候，他们必得那永不衰残的荣耀冠冕。**谦卑与交托**（5-7节）：年幼的要顺服年长的，众人都要以谦卑束腰，彼此顺服，因为神阻挡骄傲的人，赐恩给谦卑的人；要自卑，服在神大能的手下，将一切的忧虑卸给神，因为他顾念我们。**抵挡魔鬼**（8-11节）：要谨守警醒，因为魔鬼如同吼叫的狮子；要用坚固的信心抵挡他，知道各地的弟兄也经历同样的苦难；那赐诸般恩典的神，等我们暂受苦难之后，必要亲自成全我们；愿权能归给他。**问安**（12-14节）：彼得托西拉转交这信，证明这是神的真恩，要我们在这恩上站立得住；巴比伦的教会和马可问安；要用爱心彼此亲嘴问安。

全书在第10节达到高峰：受苦是「暂受」的，召我们的是「赐诸般恩典的神」，他要「亲自」成全我们。彼得开头说神「重生了我们，叫我们有活泼的盼望」（1:3），结尾说这位神必保守我们走到底。

## 深读 5:1-4 牧养神的群羊

**1. 「同作长老」。** 「我这作长老、作基督受苦的见证、同享后来所要显现之荣耀的，劝你们中间与我同作长老的人。」（5:1）
- **使徒称自己为「同作长老」：** 彼得没有说「我是使徒，你们要听我的」，而是站在长老们中间，和他们一同劝勉。
- **「作基督受苦的见证」：** 主被捉拿、受审、被戏弄的时候，彼得就在那里（可14:54、66-72；路22:61），也就在那时候三次不认主。这里的「见证」也指作证的人：他不只看见，也一生传讲基督的受苦。如今他见证的，正是他当年想要逃避的。
- **「同享……荣耀」：** 受苦和荣耀是连在一起的，正如先知所预言的「基督受苦难，后来得荣耀」（1:11）。

**2. 「务要牧养在你们中间神的群羊」。** 「务要牧养在你们中间神的群羊，按着神旨意照管他们；不是出于勉强，乃是出于甘心；也不是因为贪财，乃是出于乐意；也不是辖制所托付你们的，乃是作群羊的榜样。」（5:2-3）
- **「神的群羊」：** 羊群不是长老的，而是神的。保罗也对以弗所的长老说，教会是神「用自己血所买来的」（徒20:28）。
- **「牧养」：** 这叫人想起复活的主对彼得说的话：「你牧养我的羊。」（约21:16）当年三次不认主的彼得，被主重新托付牧养他的羊；如今他把同样的托付交给别的长老。
- **三组对比：** 甘心，不是勉强；乐意，不是贪财；作榜样，不是辖制。这三样正好针对牧者最常见的三种试探：事奉疲乏、只是尽本分，贪图好处，滥用权柄。
- **旧约的警告：** 神曾责备以色列的牧人「只知牧养自己」，「用强暴严严地辖制」羊群（结34:2、4）。主耶稣也说：「外邦人有君王为主治理他们……只是在你们中间，不可这样。」（太20:25-26，原文与彼得这里的「辖制」是同一个词）
- **属灵权柄的滥用：** 教会领袖若用「属灵权柄」操控、恐吓、羞辱会众，或者利用职位剥削钱财、侵犯别人的身体，这就是彼得所禁止的「辖制」。受害的人不必因为对方是牧者或长老就默默忍受，可以向可信任的人、教会的长执或所属宗派的负责人求助；涉及犯罪的，要向警察举报（罗13:4）。教会绝不可为了面子、为了「家丑不可外扬」而遮掩。

**3. 「牧长显现的时候」。** 「到了牧长显现的时候，你们必得那永不衰残的荣耀冠冕。」（5:4）
- **「牧长」：** 原文是牧人长（*archipoimēn*），就是在众牧人之上的那一位，指主耶稣（参2:25「灵魂的牧人监督」；来13:20「群羊的大牧人」）。长老只是在他以下的牧者。
- **「永不衰残」：** 和1:4的基业「不能衰残」同一个字根。地上的冠冕会枯萎，主所赐的荣耀却永远不会。

> 要点：教会是神的群羊，长老只是在牧长以下的仆人，要甘心、乐意、作榜样地牧养，不可辖制人。忠心的牧者所盼望的，不是地上的好处，而是牧长显现时永不衰残的荣耀。羊群真正的牧长，是那为我们担当罪、把迷路的羊领回来的基督（2:24-25）；羊群最终是在他手里的。

## 深读 5:5-7 谦卑与交托

**1. 「都要以谦卑束腰」。** 「你们年幼的，也要顺服年长的。就是你们众人也都要以谦卑束腰，彼此顺服；因为神阻挡骄傲的人，赐恩给谦卑的人。」（5:5）
- **「年幼的……年长的」：** 「年长的」原文和第1节的「长老」是同一个词，可能是指年纪大的人，也可能是指教会的长老。年轻人要尊重长辈，但下一句马上说「众人」都要彼此顺服：谦卑不只是年轻人的事，也是长老的事。在看重辈分的华人教会里，这句话特别要紧：资历和年纪都不是骄傲的理由。
- **「以谦卑束腰」：** 这个词的意思是把衣服系紧，有人认为与仆人做工时系上的围裙有关。这叫人想起主耶稣「拿一条手巾束腰」为门徒洗脚（约13:4，原文用的是另一个词，画面却相近）。谦卑就是穿上仆人的衣服。
- **引箴言3:34：** 彼得是照七十士译本引用的（和合本箴言作「他讥诮那好讥诮的人」），雅各也引用同一句（雅4:6）。神「赐恩给谦卑的人」；骄傲的人以为自己不需要恩典，就把恩典关在门外。

**2. 「服在神大能的手下」。** 「所以，你们要自卑，服在神大能的手下，到了时候，他必叫你们升高。你们要将一切的忧虑卸给神，因为他顾念你们。」（5:6-7）
- **「神大能的手」：** 旧约常说神「用大能的手和伸出来的膀臂」领以色列人出埃及（申26:8）。那只手既能管教人，也能拯救人。受苦的信徒在这只手下面，不是被压碎，而是被保护。
- **「到了时候」：** 升高的时候由神决定，不由我们决定。
- **「将一切的忧虑卸给神」：** 原文这一句和上一句是连在一起的：自卑的一个具体表现，就是把忧虑卸给神。骄傲的人以为什么都要自己扛；谦卑的人承认自己扛不动。这叫人想起诗篇：「你要把你的重担卸给耶和华，他必抚养你。」（诗55:22）彼得的用词和七十士译本这一节几乎一样。
- **「因为他顾念你们」：** 这是卸下忧虑的理由。不是因为问题已经解决了，而是因为神在乎我们。
- **交托不等于不求助：** 把忧虑卸给神，不是叫人独自硬撑、不找医生、不告诉别人。神常常藉着教会的弟兄姊妹、专业辅导和医疗来照顾我们。

> 要点：主耶稣先束上手巾为我们洗脚；所以我们也能穿上仆人的衣服，彼此顺服，服在神大能的手下。谦卑的人把一切的忧虑卸给神，因为他确实顾念我们。

## 深读 5:8-11 抵挡魔鬼，等候成全

**1. 「如同吼叫的狮子」。** 「务要谨守，警醒。因为你们的仇敌魔鬼，如同吼叫的狮子，遍地游行，寻找可吞吃的人。你们要用坚固的信心抵挡他，因为知道你们在世上的众弟兄也是经历这样的苦难。」（5:8-9）
- **「谨守，警醒」：** 主耶稣在客西马尼园对门徒说：「总要警醒祷告，免得入了迷惑。」（可14:38）那天彼得睡着了。
- **「吼叫的狮子」：** 魔鬼常常藉着逼迫和苦难，要吓倒信徒，叫他们离开信仰。狮子一吼，就叫人惊惶（参诗22:13「好像抓撕吼叫的狮子」）。
- **「用坚固的信心抵挡他」：** 我们不是靠自己的力量抵挡魔鬼，而是靠「坚固的信心」，信靠已经得胜的基督；「众天使和有权柄的，并有能力的，都服从了他」（3:22）。雅各也说：「务要抵挡魔鬼，魔鬼就必离开你们逃跑了。」（雅4:7）
- **「在世上的众弟兄」：** 受苦的人常常觉得只有自己在受苦。彼得说：世界各地的弟兄姊妹都在经历同样的事。你不是孤单的。
- **不是一切的苦难都是魔鬼直接的攻击：** 圣经也讲到疾病、天灾和人的罪带来的苦难。彼得的重点不是叫人疑神疑鬼，把什么都归给魔鬼；也不是叫人靠符咒、仪式或风水去驱邪，而是叫人在苦难中靠着基督，不丢弃信心。

**2. 「那赐诸般恩典的神」。** 「那赐诸般恩典的神曾在基督里召你们，得享他永远的荣耀，等你们暂受苦难之后，必要亲自成全你们，坚固你们，赐力量给你们。愿权能归给他，直到永永远远。阿们！」（5:10-11）
- **「赐诸般恩典的神」：** 不是只赐一点点恩典，而是各样的恩典，正对着我们所受「百般的试炼」（1:6；4:10）。
- **「暂受」和「永远」：** 苦难是「暂受」的，荣耀是「永远」的。保罗也说：「我们这至暂至轻的苦楚，要为我们成就极重无比、永远的荣耀。」（林后4:17）
- **「亲自」：** 成全我们的，不是我们自己，也不是别人，而是神亲自成全。
- **这几个动词：** 「成全」原文有修补的意思，像渔夫补网（可1:19用同一个词）；「坚固」是叫人站稳；「赐力量」是叫人有能力。许多古卷还有第四个动词，意思是把人建立在稳固的根基上；和合本所根据的古卷没有这个词。
- **「愿权能归给他」：** 彼得以颂赞结束，正如他以颂赞开始（1:3）。

> 要点：魔鬼像吼叫的狮子，我们要用坚固的信心抵挡他，也知道自己并不孤单。苦难是暂时的，那赐诸般恩典的神必要亲自修补、坚固我们，叫我们得享他永远的荣耀。

## 深读 5:12-14 在这恩上站立得住

**「我略略地写了这信，托我所看为忠心的兄弟西拉转交你们，劝勉你们，又证明这恩是神的真恩。你们务要在这恩上站立得住。在巴比伦与你们同蒙拣选的教会问你们安。我儿子马可也问你们安。你们要用爱心彼此亲嘴问安。愿平安归与你们凡在基督里的人！」**（5:12-14）
- **全书的总结：** 「这恩是神的真恩。你们务要在这恩上站立得住。」读者所经历的苦难，不是因为他们信错了；他们所领受的，正是神真实的恩典。
- **西拉：** 关于西拉的角色，参看本书导读。
- **「巴比伦」：** 多数学者认为是指罗马（参导读）。
- **「我儿子马可」：** 马可不是彼得的亲生儿子，而是属灵的儿子。彼得出监以后，就到马可的母亲马利亚家里去，那里有好些人聚集祷告（徒12:12）。早期教会的传统（帕皮亚，见优西比乌《教会史》第三卷第三十九章）说，马可福音是根据彼得的讲道写成的。马可曾经在第一次宣教旅程中途离开保罗（徒13:13），保罗因此不肯再带他（徒15:37-39）；后来保罗却说：「他在传道的事上于我有益处。」（提后4:11）
- **「用爱心彼此亲嘴问安」：** 这是当时家人之间问安的方式，保罗称为「圣洁的」亲嘴（罗16:16）。今天各地教会的问安方式不同，但彼此相爱的心是一样的。
- **「愿平安归与你们」：** 全书以平安结束。在苦难中，信徒仍然有从基督来的平安。

> 要点：彼得最后的话是：这是神的真恩，要在这恩上站立得住。受苦并不表示信错了；神的恩典是真实的，足以使我们站立到底。

## 默想

**默想：** 「我儿子马可也问你们安……那赐诸般恩典的神曾在基督里召你们，得享他永远的荣耀，等你们暂受苦难之后，必要亲自成全你们。」（5:13、10）我曾经半途而废过。也许是一个事奉的岗位，带了几年小组，累了，失望了，就悄悄退下来；也许是当年在特会上走到台前，说要一生献身事奉，后来却没有走下去。后来也不好意思回去，怕人问起。从那以后，我很少再提起这件事，心里却默默认定：在神的工作里，我已经是一张用过、破了的网。说实话，最痛的也许不是我离开了，而是我曾经把「我是个事奉的人」当成了我是谁。马可也曾经半途而废。他跟着保罗和巴拿巴出去传道，走到半路就「离开他们，回耶路撒冷去」（徒13:13）。后来保罗不肯再带他，两位同工还为他起了争论。可是在彼得这封信的最后，马可就在彼得身边，彼得叫他「我儿子」。一个曾经不认主的人，和一个曾经半途离开的人，如今一同在巴比伦，向受苦的教会问安。没有人比他们更知道，「这恩是神的真恩」。马可没有靠自己把名誉挣回来；是神的恩典把他带回来，后来又使他「于我有益处」（提后4:11）。但请留意，彼得称马可为「我儿子」，不是有用的人。在神面前，马可首先是儿子，然后才是同工。神修补我，首先不是为了让我重新上岗，而是因为我是他的儿女；以后会不会再把哪一个岗位交给我，「到了时候」由他决定（5:6）。彼得在这一章说，神要「亲自成全」我们；「成全」这个词，也用来指渔夫补网（可1:19），保罗在加6:1也用这个词，说要把被过犯所胜的人「挽回过来」。网破了，渔夫不会把它丢掉，而是坐下来一针一针地补好。主耶稣在海边呼召过正在补网的渔夫；如今，他自己就是那位补网的。彼得自己就是这样被补好的：当年在院子里不敢承认主的人，如今自称「作基督受苦的见证」（5:1），又把主托付他的羊，托付给别的长老。神藉着那位为我们的罪受苦、又从死里复活的基督修补我们（2:24）。网也可能不是我自己弄破的：一场病，一次逼迫，一段被人伤害的日子。我不必假装自己从来没有破过，也不必一个人先把自己补好才回到他面前。如果我离开是出于骄傲、怨恨，或者伤了别人，我可以先向他认罪，也求他给我勇气去道歉；如果是别人伤了我，我不必回到不安全的地方去。那位在基督里召我得享他永远荣耀的神，召我的时候就知道我会破；他召我，本来就不是因为我结实。破网在他手里，不是废物。他要亲自把它补好；补好的网，最后显出来的，是补网那一位的手。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 5 is the letter's last chapter, and it falls into four parts. **An appeal to the elders** (vv. 1–4): as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, Peter urges the elders to shepherd God's flock, not under compulsion but willingly; not for shameful gain but eagerly; not domineering but as examples; and when the chief Shepherd appears they will receive the unfading crown of glory. **Humility and entrusting** (vv. 5–7): the younger are to be subject to the elders, and all are to clothe themselves with humility toward one another, for God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble; humble yourselves under God's mighty hand, casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you. **Resisting the devil** (vv. 8–11): be sober-minded and watchful, for the devil prowls like a roaring lion; resist him, firm in your faith, knowing that your brotherhood throughout the world suffers the same; after you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace will himself restore you; to him be the dominion. **Greetings** (vv. 12–14): Peter writes by Silvanus, declaring that this is the true grace of God and urging them to stand firm in it; the church in Babylon and Mark send greetings; greet one another with the kiss of love.

The whole letter reaches its height in verse 10: suffering is "for a little while," the one who called us is "the God of all grace," and he will "himself" restore us. Peter began by saying God "has caused us to be born again to a living hope" (1:3); he ends by saying this God will bring us through.

## Going Deeper: 5:1–4 Shepherd the Flock of God

**1. "A fellow elder."** "So I exhort the elders among you, as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, as well as a partaker in the glory that is going to be revealed" (5:1).
- **An apostle calls himself a "fellow elder":** Peter does not say, "I am an apostle; you must listen to me." He stands among the elders and exhorts them as one of them.
- **"A witness of the sufferings of Christ":** Peter was there when the Lord was arrested, tried and mocked (Mark 14:54, 66–72; Luke 22:61), and in those very hours he denied him three times. "Witness" also means one who testifies: he not only saw but spent his life proclaiming Christ's sufferings. What he now bears witness to is the very thing he once tried to avoid.
- **"A partaker in the glory":** suffering and glory belong together, as the prophets foretold "the sufferings of Christ and the subsequent glories" (1:11).

**2. "Shepherd the flock of God that is among you."** "Shepherd the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you; not for shameful gain, but eagerly; not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock" (5:2–3).
- **"The flock of God":** the flock does not belong to the elders but to God. Paul likewise told the Ephesian elders that the church is God's, "which he obtained with his own blood" (Acts 20:28).
- **"Shepherd":** this recalls the risen Lord's words to Peter: "Tend my sheep" (John 21:16). Peter, who had denied the Lord three times, was entrusted again with his sheep; now he passes the same charge on to other elders.
- **Three contrasts:** willingly, not under compulsion; eagerly, not for gain; as an example, not domineering. These meet the three most common temptations of shepherds: weariness or mere duty, greed and the abuse of authority.
- **The Old Testament warning:** God rebuked Israel's shepherds "who have been feeding yourselves" and ruled the flock "with force and harshness" (Ezek 34:2, 4). The Lord Jesus said, "the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them... It shall not be so among you" (Matt 20:25–26; the same Greek verb as Peter's "domineering").
- **The abuse of spiritual authority:** when church leaders use "spiritual authority" to manipulate, intimidate or shame people, or use their position to exploit people financially or violate their bodies, this is the "domineering" Peter forbids. Those harmed need not suffer in silence because the other person is a pastor or elder; they may seek help from someone trustworthy, the church's elders or the denomination, and where a crime is involved, report it to the police (Rom 13:4). The church must never cover it up to save face or because "family shame should stay in the family."

**3. "When the chief Shepherd appears."** "And when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory" (5:4).
- **"Chief Shepherd":** Greek *archipoimēn*, the shepherd over the other shepherds, is the Lord Jesus (see 2:25, "the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls"; Heb 13:20, "the great shepherd of the sheep"). Elders are only shepherds under him.
- **"Unfading":** from the same root as the "unfading" inheritance of 1:4. Earthly crowns wither; the glory the Lord gives never fades.

> The point: The church is God's flock, and elders are only servants under the chief Shepherd, to shepherd willingly, eagerly and by example, never domineering. What faithful shepherds hope for is not earthly gain but the unfading glory of the chief Shepherd's appearing. The flock's true Shepherd is the Christ who bore our sins and brought the straying sheep home (2:24–25); in the end the flock is in his hands.

## Going Deeper: 5:5–7 Humility and Entrusting

**1. "Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility."** "Likewise, you who are younger, be subject to the elders. Clothe yourselves, all of you, with humility toward one another, for 'God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble'" (5:5).
- **"Younger... elders":** "elders" here is the same word as in verse 1; it may mean older people or the church's elders. The young are to respect their elders, but the very next sentence says "all of you" are to be humble toward one another: humility is not only for the young but for elders too. In Chinese churches that prize seniority, this matters: age and standing are no ground for pride.
- **"Clothe yourselves... with humility":** the word means to tie something on, and some link it to the apron a servant tied on for work. It recalls the Lord Jesus, who took a towel and "tied it around his waist" to wash his disciples' feet (John 13:4; a different Greek word, but a similar picture). Humility is putting on a servant's clothes.
- **Proverbs 3:34:** Peter quotes the Greek Old Testament (the Hebrew reads "Toward the scorners he is scornful"); James quotes the same line (Jas 4:6). God "gives grace to the humble"; the proud think they need no grace, and so shut it out.

**2. "Under the mighty hand of God."** "Humble yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God so that at the proper time he may exalt you, casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (5:6–7).
- **"The mighty hand of God":** the Old Testament often says God brought Israel out of Egypt "with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm" (Deut 26:8). That hand can discipline and can save. Suffering believers under that hand are not being crushed but protected.
- **"At the proper time":** God decides when to lift us up, not we.
- **"Casting all your anxieties on him":** in the Greek this clause is joined to the previous one: one concrete form of humbling ourselves is casting our anxieties on God. The proud think they must carry everything themselves; the humble admit they cannot. This recalls the psalm: "Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you" (Ps 55:22); Peter's wording is almost identical to the Greek Old Testament of this verse.
- **"Because he cares for you":** this is the reason for casting our anxieties on him. Not because the problem has been solved, but because God cares about us.
- **Entrusting is not refusing help:** casting our anxieties on God does not mean struggling on alone, refusing to see a doctor, or telling no one. God often cares for us through brothers and sisters in the church, through counseling and through medical care.

> The point: The Lord Jesus first tied on the towel and washed our feet; so we too can put on a servant's clothes, submitting to one another and to God's mighty hand. The humble cast all their anxieties on God, because he truly cares for them.

## Going Deeper: 5:8–11 Resisting the Devil, Waiting to Be Restored

**1. "Like a roaring lion."** "Be sober-minded; be watchful. Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour. Resist him, firm in your faith, knowing that the same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world" (5:8–9).
- **"Be sober-minded; be watchful":** in Gethsemane the Lord Jesus told his disciples, "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Mark 14:38). That night Peter fell asleep.
- **"A roaring lion":** the devil often uses persecution and suffering to terrify believers into abandoning their faith. A lion's roar strikes terror (compare Ps 22:13, "like a ravening and roaring lion").
- **"Resist him, firm in your faith":** we do not resist the devil by our own strength but "firm in your faith," trusting in the Christ who has triumphed, to whom "angels, authorities, and powers" have been subjected (3:22). James also says, "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (Jas 4:7).
- **"Your brotherhood throughout the world":** people who suffer often feel they suffer alone. Peter says brothers and sisters all over the world are going through the same thing. You are not alone.
- **Not every hardship is a direct attack of the devil:** Scripture also speaks of suffering from illness, disaster and human sin. Peter's point is not to see demons everywhere, nor to ward them off with charms, rituals or feng shui, but to hold on to faith in Christ in suffering.

**2. "The God of all grace."** "And after you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ, will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you. To him be the dominion forever and ever. Amen" (5:10–11).
- **"The God of all grace":** not a little grace, but grace of every kind, matching our trials of every kind (1:6; 4:10).
- **"A little while" and "eternal":** suffering is "a little while"; glory is "eternal." Paul says, "this slight momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (2 Cor 4:17).
- **"Himself":** it is not we who restore ourselves, nor anyone else, but God "himself."
- **Four verbs:** "restore" can mean mending, like fishermen mending their nets (the same word is used in Mark 1:19); "confirm" means to make firm; "strengthen" means to give strength; "establish" means to set on a sure foundation (the Chinese Union Version follows manuscripts without this fourth word).
- **"To him be the dominion":** Peter ends with praise, as he began with praise (1:3).

> The point: The devil prowls like a roaring lion; we resist him with firm faith, knowing we are not alone. Suffering is brief, and the God of all grace will himself mend and strengthen us, and bring us into his eternal glory.

## Going Deeper: 5:12–14 Stand Firm in This Grace

**"By Silvanus, a faithful brother as I regard him, I have written briefly to you, exhorting and declaring that this is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it. She who is at Babylon, who is likewise chosen, sends you greetings, and so does Mark, my son. Greet one another with the kiss of love. Peace to all of you who are in Christ"** (5:12–14).
- **The whole letter in a sentence:** "this is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it." The suffering the readers face does not mean they believed wrongly; what they have received is God's true grace.
- **Silvanus:** on Silvanus's role, see the introduction.
- **"Babylon":** most scholars take it as Rome (see the introduction).
- **"Mark, my son":** Mark was not Peter's son by birth but his son in the faith. When Peter was released from prison, he went to the house of Mark's mother Mary, where many believers were gathered praying (Acts 12:12). Early church tradition (Papias, in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39) says Mark's Gospel was based on Peter's preaching. Mark had left Paul partway through the first missionary journey (Acts 13:13), and Paul refused to take him again (Acts 15:37–39); yet later Paul said, "he is very useful to me for ministry" (2 Tim 4:11).
- **"The kiss of love":** this was how family members greeted one another at the time; Paul calls it a "holy kiss" (Rom 16:16). Churches today greet one another in different ways, but the love behind it is the same.
- **"Peace to all of you":** the letter ends in peace. In suffering, believers still have the peace that comes from Christ.

> The point: Peter's last word is: this is the true grace of God; stand firm in it. Suffering does not mean we believed wrongly; God's grace is real, and it is enough to keep us standing to the end.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "So does Mark, my son... And after you have suffered a little while, the God of all grace, who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ, will himself restore... you" (5:13, 10). I have given up partway before. Perhaps it was a ministry: I led a small group for a few years, grew tired and disappointed, and quietly stepped down. Perhaps at a conference I once went forward to give my life to full-time ministry, and never followed through. Since then I have been too ashamed to go back, afraid of being asked. I seldom mention it, but I have quietly concluded that in God's work I am a used and torn net. Honestly, what hurts most may not be that I left, but that I had made "someone who serves" into who I am. Mark gave up partway too. He set out with Paul and Barnabas, and partway through he "left them and returned to Jerusalem" (Acts 13:13). Later Paul refused to take him, and the two co-workers even fell out over him. Yet at the end of Peter's letter, Mark is at Peter's side, and Peter calls him "my son." A man who had once denied his Lord and a man who had quit partway through now stand together in Babylon, sending greetings to a suffering church. No one knew better than they did that "this is the true grace of God." Mark did not earn back his reputation by himself; God's grace brought him back, and later made him "very useful to me for ministry" (2 Tim 4:11). But notice that Peter calls Mark "my son," not "a useful man." Before God, Mark was first a son, and only then a co-worker. God mends me first not to put me back on the roster but because I am his child; whether he ever hands me a post again is his to decide, "at the proper time" (5:6). In this chapter Peter says God will "himself restore" us, and "restore" is the same word used of fishermen mending their nets (Mark 1:19), and of restoring a fallen brother (Gal 6:1). When a net is torn, a fisherman does not throw it away; he sits down and mends it, stitch by stitch. The Lord Jesus once called fishermen who were mending their nets by the sea; now he himself is the one who mends. Peter had been mended this way himself: the man who once would not own his Lord in the courtyard now calls himself "a witness of the sufferings of Christ" (5:1), and passes on to other elders the sheep the Lord entrusted to him. God mends us through the Christ who suffered for our sins and rose again (2:24). Or the tear may not be my own doing: an illness, a season of persecution, a time when others wounded me. I do not need to pretend I have never been torn, nor mend myself on my own before I come back to him. If I left in pride or bitterness, or hurt others when I left, I can first confess it to him and ask him for courage to apologize; if others hurt me, I need not go back to an unsafe place. The God who called me to his eternal glory in Christ knew when he called me that I would tear; he never called me because I was strong. A torn net in his hands is not rubbish. He means to mend it himself, and in the end a mended net shows the hand of the one who mended it.
