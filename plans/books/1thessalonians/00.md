## 这卷书在讲什么

帖撒罗尼迦前书是保罗写给一个刚刚诞生的教会的信。保罗在那里传福音的时间不长，就被逼离开了（徒17:1-10）。他走的时候，这群信徒信主才几个月，有的也许只有几个星期，却已经在「大难之中」（1:6），受着「本地人的苦害」（2:14）。保罗放心不下，像父母被迫离开年幼的孩子一样，「极力地想法子」要回去看他们（2:17）。去不成，就打发提摩太去；提摩太带回了好消息，保罗就写了这封信。

所以这封信读起来特别温暖。保罗一再说「你们自己知道」，提醒他们当初怎样领受福音；他说自己在他们中间，「如同母亲乳养自己的孩子」（2:7），又「好像父亲待自己的儿女一样」（2:11）。信中有感谢，有思念，有劝勉，也回答了他们心里的几个问题：信主的亲人死了怎么办？主什么时候再来？

这封信有几条线交织在一起：
- **福音改变了一群人：** 「你们是怎样离弃偶像，归向神，要服事那又真又活的神，等候他儿子从天降临」（1:9-10）。福音不只是一套新的想法，而是叫人掉转方向。
- **神的道在受苦的人心里运行：** 他们领受这道，「不以为是人的道，乃以为是神的道」，这道「运行在你们信主的人心中」（2:13）。患难没有把他们冲走，反倒叫他们的信心传遍各处（1:8）。
- **在神面前过圣洁的生活：** 「神的旨意就是要你们成为圣洁」（4:3）。这包括身体和性的事，也包括彼此相爱、安静做工（4:3-12）。
- **主必再来：** 每一章都以主的再来作结（1:10；2:19；3:13；4:17；5:23）。这盼望安慰丧亲的人（4:18），也叫人警醒谨守（5:6）。

保罗在5:9-10把全信的根基说了出来：「因为神不是预定我们受刑，乃是预定我们藉着我们主耶稣基督得救。他替我们死，叫我们无论醒着，睡着，都与他同活。」

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 城市。** 帖撒罗尼迦是罗马马其顿省的省会，也是一个港口城市，横贯东西的埃格那提亚大道就从城中穿过。主前42年，这城因为在罗马内战中站在胜利者一边，得了自由城的地位，由本地的官员管理。使徒行传称这些官员为「地方官」（徒17:6），原文是一个很少见的词，后来出土的帖撒罗尼迦碑文正好用了这个词，支持了路加记载的准确。城里有许多神庙，也敬拜罗马皇帝；尊荣凯撒，是城市荣耀和安全的一部分。难怪有人控告信徒「违背凯撒的命令，说另有一个王耶稣」（徒17:7）。

**2. 教会是怎样开始的。** 保罗、西拉和提摩太在第二次宣教旅程中，离开腓立比来到这里；他们在腓立比刚刚「被害受辱」（2:2；参徒16:19-24）。保罗照常先到会堂，「一连三个安息日」本着圣经辩论，讲解基督必须受害、从死里复活（徒17:2-3）。有些犹太人信了，「并有许多虔敬的希腊人，尊贵的妇女也不少」（徒17:4）。但从这封信看，教会里多数人原是拜偶像的外邦人（1:9）。
- **住了多久？** 使徒行传只提到三个安息日。但保罗在那里「昼夜做工」（2:9），腓立比教会也「一次两次地」送钱给他（腓4:16），所以许多解经家认为他在城里前后住了几个月，三个安息日只是在会堂里辩论的时间。无论多长，都不算久。
- **被逼离开：** 不信的犹太人「招聚了些市井匪类」闯进耶孙的家（徒17:5-9），弟兄们只好连夜送走保罗和西拉。保罗后来说，他们是「暂时与你们离别」（2:17），许多解经家认为，原文带着成了孤儿的意思：被硬生生拆开。

**3. 写信的经过。** 保罗到了庇哩亚，又被追赶，就到了雅典（徒17:10-15）。他「既不能再忍」，就打发提摩太回帖撒罗尼迦去坚固他们（3:1-2）。后来保罗到了哥林多，提摩太从马其顿回来（徒18:5），带来了好消息：他们的信心和爱心都站立得稳，也很想见保罗（3:6）。保罗大喜，就写了这封信。（使徒行传说西拉和提摩太留在庇哩亚，后来在哥林多与保罗会合；这封信说提摩太是从雅典被打发回去的。保罗到雅典后曾叫他们「速速」来（徒17:15），所以最简单的理解是：提摩太先到雅典见了保罗，又被差回帖撒罗尼迦，路加没有记下这一段来回。3:1说保罗「独自」留在雅典，也许西拉也被差往别处，例如腓立比。）

**4. 写作时间。** 保罗在哥林多住了一年零六个月（徒18:11）。在希腊的德尔斐出土了一块碑文，可以推算出亚该亚方伯迦流（徒18:12）的任期大约是主后51-52年。所以这封信大约写于主后50-51年，离耶稣受死复活只有二十年左右。它若不是保罗现存最早的书信，也是最早的之一，是新约中最早写成的书卷之一。许多持南加拉太说的学者（如弗雷德里克·布鲁斯、理查德·朗格内克）认为加拉太书写于主后48-49年，比本书更早；持北加拉太说的学者（如莱特福特、汉斯·贝茨）和部分持南加拉太说的学者，则把加拉太书定在本书之后（参本研读加拉太书导读）。

**5. 作者。** 信的开头写着「保罗、西拉、提摩太」（1:1），信中多数地方用「我们」，有时保罗也单独说「我」（2:18；3:5；5:27）。初期教会一致接受这是保罗的书信，今天几乎所有学者也这样看。
- **2:14-16的问题：** 有少数学者（如伯格·皮尔逊）认为2:14-16论到犹太人的几句话太严厉，不像保罗写的，也似乎与罗马书9-11章不合，可能是后人在主后70年耶路撒冷被毁以后加进去的。但现存所有的抄本都有这几句，大多数学者（如杰弗里·韦玛、吉恩·格林）认为这是保罗自己写的。这段话要怎样理解，留到第二章再谈。

## 钥匙经文：1:9-10

**1. 「离弃偶像，归向神」。** 原文先说「归向神」，再说「离弃偶像」。这是同一个转身，不是先后两步；但保罗把重点放在神身上。福音不是先叫人努力丢掉什么，而是叫人看见那「又真又活的神」，偶像就失去了吸引力。对第一世纪的帖撒罗尼迦人来说，这代价很大：家族的祭祀、行会的宴席、城市的节庆，都和拜偶像连在一起。离弃偶像，常常就是离开原来的人际网络。

**2. 「服事」和「等候」。** 这两个动词说出了信徒的整个生活：现在服事又真又活的神，同时等候他儿子从天降临。服事不是为了赚得得救，因为后面马上说，救我们的是耶稣；等候也不是闲坐，而是在盼望中服事。

**3. 「救我们脱离将来忿怒的耶稣」。** 神的忿怒是真实的，是神对罪公义的审判（参5:3、9）。但这里的重点是：耶稣「救我们脱离」。他怎样救？5:10说：「他替我们死。」他担当了我们当受的刑罚，所以信靠他的人可以安心等候他来，不必惧怕。这位要从天降临的，就是神「从死里复活」的那一位。

## 全书结构

帖撒罗尼迦前书是一封书信，大致可以这样分：
- **问安和感谢**（1:1-10）：保罗为他们因信心所做的工夫、因爱心所受的劳苦、因盼望所存的忍耐感谢神；他们离弃偶像归向神，成了各处信徒的榜样。
- **保罗怎样在他们中间**（2:1-12）：不是讨人喜欢，也没有贪心；存心温柔，像母亲，又像父亲。
- **神的道与患难**（2:13-16）：他们领受神的道，也像犹太的教会一样受苦。
- **思念、打发提摩太和祷告**（2:17–3:13）：保罗想回去，却被撒但阻挡；提摩太带回好消息；保罗为他们祷告，愿他们在主来的时候心里坚固，成为圣洁。
- **圣洁和相爱**（4:1-12）：远避淫行，用圣洁、尊贵守着自己的身体；弟兄相爱，安静做工。
- **睡了的人**（4:13-18）：信主的人死了不是没有指望；主必降临，死了的人必先复活，我们要和主永远同在。
- **主的日子**（5:1-11）：那日子像夜间的贼一样来到；信徒是光明之子，要警醒谨守。
- **教会生活和结语**（5:12-28）：敬重劳苦的人，彼此和睦；常常喜乐，不住地祷告，凡事谢恩；「那召你们的本是信实的，他必成就这事」（5:24）。

全信可以分成两大部分：前三章多是回顾和感谢，讲神已经在他们中间做了什么；后两章多是劝勉，讲他们要怎样继续走下去。两部分之间的枢纽是4:1：「你们既然受了我们的教训，知道该怎样行可以讨神的喜悦，就要照你们现在所行的更加勉励。」

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 留意信心、爱心、盼望。** 保罗一开头就提到「因信心所做的工夫，因爱心所受的劳苦，因盼望我们主耶稣基督所存的忍耐」（1:3），快结束时又说「把信和爱当作护心镜遮胸，把得救的盼望当作头盔戴上」（5:8）。读的时候，可以留意这三样在信中怎样一再出现。

**2. 留意主的再来。** 每一章都以主的再来作结（1:10；2:19；3:13；4:17；5:23）。保罗讲主的再来，不是为了满足好奇心，也不是为了排出时间表，而是为了安慰（4:18）、坚固（3:13）、劝人圣洁（5:23）。读的时候可以问：这个盼望怎样改变今天的生活？

**3. 留意「你们自己知道」。** 保罗一再说「你们知道」「你们自己原晓得」（1:5；2:1、2、5、11；3:3、4；4:2；5:2）。他没有给他们新的东西，而是叫他们记得已经领受的。信徒在患难中，常常需要的不是新的道理，而是重新想起自己所信的。

**4. 留意家人的语言。** 保罗称他们为「弟兄」十几次，又说自己像母亲、像父亲，离开他们像成了孤儿。教会不是一个机构，而是神的家。对今天许多因信主而在家里受压力、甚至被家人排斥的华人信徒来说，这封信说明：你在神的家里有弟兄姊妹。

**5. 和使徒行传17章、帖撒罗尼迦后书一起读。** 使徒行传17:1-10记载了教会怎样开始；帖撒罗尼迦后书大概是不久以后写的，继续讨论主的日子和那些不肯做工的人。

## 默想

**默想：** 「我们也不住地感谢神，因你们听见我们所传神的道就领受了；不以为是人的道，乃以为是神的道。这道实在是神的，并且运行在你们信主的人心中。」（2:13）帖撒罗尼迦人听到的，是几个刚被赶出另一座城、在城里辛苦做工的外地人讲的话。可是他们听出来，这是神的道。我还记得神的话第一次对我说话的时候吗？也许是一节经文忽然叫我流泪，也许是一篇讲道正好说中了我的心事。可是今天，读经可能已经变成了一件例行公事：读完一章，合上圣经，什么也没有留下，好像只是读了一些古人的话。有时我甚至不敢承认，自己已经很久没有被神的话感动了。保罗并没有说，这道只在信徒热心的时候才运行；他说这道「实在是神的」，并且「运行在你们信主的人心中」。神的话不靠我的感觉才有能力。在我觉得最干涩的日子，它仍然在做工，像种子在土里，看不见，却在长。圣经不是冷冰冰的文字，因为借着它说话的，是那位「藉着他儿子晓谕我们」的神（来1:2），而这儿子就是「道成了肉身」的耶稣（约1:14）；神的灵也使这道在我心里运行。今天，让我在打开圣经以前先安静一下，求他亲自对我说话；读完以后，也不必勉强自己感受到什么，只要相信：这是神的道，而神的道从来不会徒然返回（赛55:11）。

## What This Book Is About

1 Thessalonians is Paul's letter to a newborn church. Paul had not been preaching there long before he was forced to leave (Acts 17:1–10). When he left, these believers had been Christians for only a few months, some perhaps only a few weeks, and they were already "in much affliction" (1:6), suffering "from your own countrymen" (2:14). Paul could not stop worrying about them. Like a parent forced to leave young children, he "endeavored the more eagerly and with great desire to see you face to face" (2:17). When he could not go, he sent Timothy; Timothy came back with good news, and Paul wrote this letter.

So the letter reads with unusual warmth. Paul keeps saying "you yourselves know," reminding them how they first received the gospel. He says that among them he was "like a nursing mother taking care of her own children" (2:7), and "like a father with his children" (2:11). The letter holds thanksgiving, longing and exhortation, and it answers some questions on their minds: what about believing loved ones who have died? When will the Lord come?

Several threads run through the letter:
- **The gospel changed a group of people:** "how you turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God, and to wait for his Son from heaven" (1:9–10). The gospel is not just a new set of ideas; it turns people around.
- **God's word at work in suffering people:** they received the word "not as the word of men but as what it really is, the word of God, which is at work in you believers" (2:13). Affliction did not sweep them away; instead their faith became known everywhere (1:8).
- **A holy life before God:** "this is the will of God, your sanctification" (4:3). That covers the body and sexual life, and also loving one another and working quietly (4:3–12).
- **The Lord will come:** every chapter ends with the Lord's coming (1:10; 2:19; 3:13; 4:17; 5:23). This hope comforts the grieving (4:18) and calls people to stay awake and sober (5:6).

In 5:9–10 Paul states the foundation of the whole letter: "For God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us so that whether we are awake or asleep we might live with him."

## Author and Setting

**1. The city.** Thessalonica was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia and a port city, and the Via Egnatia, the great east–west highway, ran right through it. In 42 BC, because it had backed the winning side in Rome's civil war, it was made a free city, governed by its own local officials. Acts calls these officials "city authorities" (Acts 17:6). Luke's Greek word, *politarchēs*, is rare, and inscriptions later found in Thessalonica use exactly this title, supporting his accuracy. The city had many temples and also honored the Roman emperor; honoring Caesar was part of the city's glory and security. No wonder the believers were accused of "acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus" (Acts 17:7).

**2. How the church began.** On the second missionary journey, Paul, Silas and Timothy came here from Philippi, where they had just "suffered and been shamefully treated" (2:2; see Acts 16:19–24). As usual, Paul went first to the synagogue, and "on three Sabbath days he reasoned with them from the Scriptures," explaining that the Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead (Acts 17:2–3). Some Jews believed, "as did a great many of the devout Greeks and not a few of the leading women" (Acts 17:4). But this letter suggests that most of the church had been Gentile idol-worshipers (1:9).
- **How long did he stay?** Acts mentions only three Sabbaths. But Paul "worked night and day" there (2:9), and the Philippian church sent him help "once and again" (Phil 4:16), so many commentators think he stayed in the city for some months, with the three Sabbaths being the time of debate in the synagogue. Either way, it was not long.
- **Forced out:** unbelieving Jews gathered "some wicked men of the rabble" and attacked Jason's house (Acts 17:5–9), and the brothers had to send Paul and Silas away by night. Paul later says they were "torn away from you" (2:17); many take his Greek word to carry the sense of being orphaned, wrenched apart.

**3. How the letter came to be written.** Paul went on to Berea, was pursued again, and reached Athens (Acts 17:10–15). "When we could bear it no longer," he sent Timothy back to Thessalonica to strengthen them (3:1–2). Later, when Paul was in Corinth, Timothy came back from Macedonia (Acts 18:5) with good news: their faith and love were standing firm, and they longed to see Paul (3:6). Overjoyed, Paul wrote this letter. (Acts says Silas and Timothy stayed in Berea and later joined Paul in Corinth; this letter says Timothy was sent back from Athens. Paul had asked them to come to him "as soon as possible" (Acts 17:15), so the simplest explanation is that Timothy first came to Paul in Athens and was then sent back to Thessalonica, a round trip Luke does not record. Since 3:1 says Paul was left "alone" at Athens, Silas too may have been sent elsewhere, perhaps to Philippi.)

**4. Date.** Paul stayed in Corinth a year and six months (Acts 18:11). An inscription found at Delphi in Greece lets us date the proconsulship of Gallio in Achaia (Acts 18:12) to about AD 51–52. So the letter was probably written around AD 50–51, only about twenty years after Jesus' death and resurrection. It is either Paul's earliest surviving letter or one of the earliest, and one of the earliest books of the New Testament. Many South Galatian scholars (such as F. F. Bruce and Richard Longenecker) date Galatians to AD 48–49, before this letter; North Galatian scholars (such as J. B. Lightfoot and Hans Dieter Betz) and some South Galatian scholars place Galatians later (see this study's Galatians introduction).

**5. Author.** The letter opens with "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy" (1:1). Most of it uses "we," though Paul sometimes says "I" (2:18; 3:5; 5:27). The early church received it unanimously as Paul's, and almost all scholars today agree.
- **The question of 2:14–16:** a few scholars (such as Birger Pearson) think the words about the Jews in 2:14–16 are too harsh to be Paul's and seem to conflict with Romans 9–11, and may have been added after the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70. But every surviving manuscript contains these verses, and most scholars (such as Jeffrey Weima and Gene Green) hold that Paul wrote them. How to understand the passage is left for chapter 2.

## Key Verse: 1:9–10

**1. "Turned to God from idols."** The Greek names God first: "to God from idols." This is one turning, not two steps, but the weight falls on God. The gospel does not begin by telling people to strain to throw things away; it shows them "the living and true God," and the idols lose their pull. For first-century Thessalonians the cost was high: family rites, trade-guild banquets and city festivals were all bound up with idol worship. Leaving idols often meant leaving one's whole network of relationships.

**2. "Serve" and "wait."** These two verbs describe the whole of the believer's life: serving the living and true God now, while waiting for his Son from heaven. The serving does not earn salvation, for the very next words say that Jesus is the one who delivers us; and the waiting is not idleness, but service in hope.

**3. "Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come."** God's wrath is real; it is his righteous judgment on sin (see 5:3, 9). But the stress here is that Jesus "delivers us." How? 5:10 says he "died for us." He bore the penalty we deserved, so those who trust him can wait for his coming in peace, without dread. The one coming from heaven is the one God "raised from the dead."

## The Shape of the Book

1 Thessalonians is a letter, and it can be roughly divided like this:
- **Greeting and thanksgiving** (1:1–10): Paul thanks God for their work of faith, labor of love and steadfastness of hope; they turned from idols to God and became an example to believers everywhere.
- **How Paul lived among them** (2:1–12): not to please people, with no greed; gentle like a mother, and like a father.
- **The word of God and affliction** (2:13–16): they received God's word, and suffered as the churches in Judea did.
- **Longing, sending Timothy, and prayer** (2:17–3:13): Paul wanted to return but Satan hindered him; Timothy brought good news; Paul prays that their hearts be established blameless in holiness at the Lord's coming.
- **Holiness and love** (4:1–12): abstain from sexual immorality, control one's own body in holiness and honor; love one another, live quietly and work.
- **Those who have fallen asleep** (4:13–18): believers who die are not without hope; the Lord will descend, the dead in Christ will rise first, and we will always be with the Lord.
- **The day of the Lord** (5:1–11): that day will come like a thief in the night; believers are children of light and must stay awake and sober.
- **Church life and closing** (5:12–28): respect those who labor among you, be at peace; rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances; "He who calls you is faithful; he will surely do it" (5:24).

The letter falls into two main parts: the first three chapters mostly look back and give thanks for what God has already done among them; the last two mostly exhort them how to keep going. The hinge is 4:1: "as you received from us how you ought to live and to please God, just as you are doing, that you do so more and more."

## How to Read This Book

**1. Watch for faith, love and hope.** Paul begins with "your work of faith and labor of love and steadfastness of hope in our Lord Jesus Christ" (1:3), and near the end says, "having put on the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet the hope of salvation" (5:8). As you read, notice how these three keep coming back.

**2. Watch for the Lord's coming.** Every chapter ends with the Lord's coming (1:10; 2:19; 3:13; 4:17; 5:23). Paul does not speak of it to satisfy curiosity or to draw up a timetable, but to comfort (4:18), to strengthen (3:13) and to call to holiness (5:23). As you read, ask: how does this hope change today?

**3. Watch for "you yourselves know."** Paul keeps saying "you know," "you yourselves know" (1:5; 2:1, 2, 5, 11; 3:3, 4; 4:2; 5:2). He is not giving them something new; he is calling them to remember what they already received. In affliction, believers often need not new teaching but to recall what they believe.

**4. Watch for family language.** Paul calls them "brothers" more than a dozen times, and says he was like a mother and a father to them, and that leaving them was like being orphaned. The church is not an institution but God's household. For many Chinese believers today who face pressure at home for their faith, or are even shut out by their families, this letter says: you have brothers and sisters in God's family.

**5. Read it with Acts 17 and 2 Thessalonians.** Acts 17:1–10 tells how the church began; 2 Thessalonians, probably written soon afterward, continues the discussion of the day of the Lord and of those who refuse to work.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "And we also thank God constantly for this, that when you received the word of God, which you heard from us, you accepted it not as the word of men but as what it really is, the word of God, which is at work in you believers" (2:13). What the Thessalonians heard came from a few outsiders who had just been driven out of another city and were working hard at a trade in theirs. Yet they heard in it the word of God. Do I remember the first time God's word spoke to me? Perhaps a single verse suddenly brought me to tears, or a sermon named exactly what was on my heart. But today, Bible reading may have become routine: I finish a chapter, close the book, and nothing remains, as if I had only read some old human words. Sometimes I hardly dare admit how long it has been since God's word moved me. Paul does not say this word works only when believers are fervent. He says it "really is" the word of God and that it "is at work in you believers." God's word does not depend on my feelings for its power. On my driest days it is still at work, like a seed in the soil, unseen but growing. Scripture is not cold print, because through it speaks the God who "has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:2), the Son who is "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14); and God's Spirit makes that word work in my heart. Today, before I open the Bible, let me be still for a moment and ask him to speak to me himself; and when I finish, I need not force myself to feel anything, but simply trust that this is God's word, and God's word never returns empty (Isa 55:11).
