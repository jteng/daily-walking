## 本章脉络

第二章有三段。**流泪写的信**（1-4节）：保罗解释他为什么没有再去哥林多，却写了一封信；那封信是「心里难过痛苦，多多地流泪」写的，为要叫他们「知道我格外地疼爱你们」。**赦免那人**（5-11节）：教会里曾有人叫保罗和众人忧愁，那人已经受了责罚；保罗劝他们「倒不如赦免他，安慰他」，「免得撒但趁着机会胜过我们」。**特罗亚的不安与基督里的夸胜**（12-17节）：保罗在特罗亚找不到提多，心里不安，往马其顿去；说到这里，他忽然发出感谢：「感谢神！常率领我们在基督里夸胜。」

这一章让我们看见一位牧者的心：他责备人，是因为爱；他要教会管教犯罪的人，也要教会在那人悔改以后接纳他。

## 深读 2:1-4 用眼泪写的信

**1. 不愿再忧愁地去。** 「我自己定了主意再到你们那里去，必须大家没有忧愁。」（2:1）原文的意思是不要再一次带着忧愁到他们那里去，可见保罗上一次去哥林多，就是一次叫人忧愁的探访（参13:2）。保罗不愿意重复那样的经历，所以先写信。

**2. 彼此的喜乐连在一起。** 「倘若我叫你们忧愁，除了我叫那忧愁的人以外，谁能叫我快乐呢？」（2:2）保罗和哥林多教会的喜乐是连在一起的。他不是一位高高在上、不受影响的领袖；教会忧愁，他也忧愁。

**3. 流泪的责备。** 「我先前心里难过痛苦，多多地流泪，写信给你们，不是叫你们忧愁，乃是叫你们知道我格外地疼爱你们。」（2:4）这封信说了严厉的话，但写信的人是流着泪写的。真正的责备不是发泄怒气，也不是要证明自己对，而是出于爱，为要挽回人。有些人以为爱就是不说难听的话；有些人以为说难听的话就是爱。保罗两样都不是：他说了重话，心里却在流泪。

> 要点：保罗严厉的话是流着泪写的。出于爱的责备，目的不是叫人难受，而是叫人回转；说这话的人，自己的心也在痛。

## 深读 2:5-11 赦免他，安慰他

**1. 那叫人忧愁的人是谁？** 「若有叫人忧愁的，他不但叫我忧愁，也是叫你们众人有几分忧愁。」（2:5）较早的许多解经家（今天也有少数学者，如菲利普·休斯）认为，这人就是哥林多前书5章那位收了继母的人。今天多数解经家认为是另一个人：他很可能在保罗那次痛苦的探访中当众顶撞、伤害了保罗，或者亏负了保罗的某位同工（参7:12「那亏负人的」与「那受人亏负的」）。无论是谁，原则都一样：一个犯了罪的人受了教会的管教，现在已经悔改了。

**2. 责罚也就够了。** 「这样的人受了众人的责罚也就够了。」（2:6）「众人」原文可译作多数人，也可能是指全会众，可见这是教会一起作的决定。有人认为还有少数人不同意（或主张更重，或主张免罚），但经文没有明说。管教是有目的的：叫人悔改，不是叫人永远抬不起头。目的达到了，管教就要停下来。

**3. 不要叫他忧愁太过。** 「倒不如赦免他，安慰他，免得他忧愁太过，甚至沉沦了。所以我劝你们，要向他显出坚定不移的爱心来。」（2:7-8）「沉沦」原文是被吞灭的意思，好像一个人被忧愁淹没。一个悔改的人，如果教会一直冷淡他、提防他、在背后议论他，他可能会被忧愁吞没，甚至离开信仰。「显出坚定不移的爱心」原文是法律上的用词，意思是正式确认。保罗要教会像当初公开管教他一样，也公开地、清楚地接纳他回来。

**4. 在基督面前赦免。** 「你们赦免谁，我也赦免谁。我若有所赦免的，是在基督面前为你们赦免的。」（2:10）如果这人得罪的正是保罗，那么保罗这个受伤的人，自己先站出来说「我赦免他」。他能这样做，是因为他站在基督面前：他自己就是一个被基督赦免的罪人（提前1:15）。我们能饶恕人，也是因为「神在基督里饶恕了你们」（弗4:32）。

**5. 撒但的诡计。** 「免得撒但趁着机会胜过我们，因我们并非不晓得他的诡计。」（2:11）撒但可以利用教会的放纵，叫教会容忍罪；也可以利用教会的严厉，叫悔改的人绝望，叫教会变得冷酷、分裂。两样都是他的诡计。

**6. 赦免不等于什么都恢复原状。** 这段经文讲的是一个已经悔改的人重新被接纳进入团契。它不是说一切罪都可以不了了之。如果有人犯的是虐待、性侵或其他罪行：
- 报警、交由司法处理是对的，政府是「神的用人」，为要「刑罚那作恶的」（罗13:4）；
- 受害人不必和不安全的人和好，也不必留在危险里；
- 赦免和接纳回到团契，不等于恢复他的职分，也不等于让他再接触受害人或儿童；
- 教会不可为了面子隐瞒这样的事，「家丑不可外扬」不是福音的原则。

这里是受伤最深的保罗自己先赦免，并劝教会结束对一个已经悔改之人的管教。他并没有要求受虐的人与加害者和好、重新信任他，或按别人的时间表「赶快原谅」。

> 要点：管教的目的是挽回，不是羞辱。当犯罪的人真心悔改，教会要赦免他、安慰他、公开确认对他的爱，因为我们自己也是在基督里蒙赦免的人。赦免他，却不等于让他回到能伤害人的位置上。

## 深读 2:12-13 开了的门，不安的心

**1. 一扇开了的门。** 「我从前为基督的福音到了特罗亚，主也给我开了门。」（2:12）特罗亚是亚细亚西北的港口，保罗多年前就是在这里看见「马其顿人」的异象（徒16:8-10）。现在主在那里给他开了传福音的门。

**2. 他却离开了。** 「那时，因为没有遇见兄弟提多，我心里不安，便辞别那里的人往马其顿去了。」（2:13）保罗在等提多带来哥林多的消息。他太挂念哥林多教会，以致无法安心留在特罗亚。这让我们看见使徒也是人：他会焦虑，会牵挂，会因为一个教会的光景而坐立不安（参11:28）。

**3. 故事停在这里。** 保罗讲到这里，就停下来，一直到7:5才接着说：「我们从前就是到了马其顿的时候……」中间这一大段（2:14–7:4），是保罗对新约职事的默想。他好像在说：讲到我的焦虑，我就想起神怎样在我的软弱中仍然得胜。

> 要点：使徒也会焦虑、牵挂、不安。神并不因为他的软弱就停止工作；保罗在最不安的时候，仍然能说：「感谢神！」

## 深读 2:14-17 在基督里夸胜，作基督的香气

**1. 凯旋的行列。** 「感谢神！常率领我们在基督里夸胜，并藉着我们在各处显扬那因认识基督而有的香气。」（2:14）「率领……夸胜」原文是罗马人凯旋游行的用词。打了胜仗的将军回到罗马，带着军队和俘虏游街，向全城显扬他的胜利。在当时，这个词一般是指得胜者带着被征服的俘虏游行，所以许多解经家认为，保罗是把自己比作俘虏：神在基督里征服了他。他曾经逼迫教会，现在却成了神的俘虏，到处显扬基督的得胜。有学者（如斯科特·哈夫曼）指出，凯旋行列中的俘虏常常是被带去处死的，这正与2:16「死的香气」和4:10-11「耶稣的死」相呼应。也有人认为保罗是说自己在得胜的行列中有份，和合本「夸胜」的译法比较接近这个意思。

**2. 香气。** 凯旋的行列中常常焚香，香气飘满街道。旧约的献祭也常称为「奉给耶和华为馨香之祭」（民15:3）。保罗说，神藉着我们「在各处显扬那因认识基督而有的香气」。基督自己「为我们舍了自己，当作馨香的供物和祭物，献与神」（弗5:2）；传扬他的人，也带着他的香气。

**3. 死的香气，活的香气。** 「因为我们在神面前，无论在得救的人身上或灭亡的人身上，都有基督馨香之气。在这等人，就作了死的香气叫他死；在那等人，就作了活的香气叫他活。」（2:15-16）同一个福音，对相信的人是生命，对拒绝的人却成了审判（参林前1:18）。这不是说传福音的人要为结果负责，而是说福音从来不是无关紧要的：人听了，不是走向生命，就是走向死亡。读到这里，我也要问自己：我听了这福音，是走向生命，还是走向死亡？

**4. 谁能当得起？** 「这事谁能当得起呢？」（2:16）面对这样严肃的职事，没有人凭自己当得起。保罗在3:5给了答案：「我们所能承担的，乃是出于神。」

**5. 不是为利混乱神的道。** 「我们不像那许多人，为利混乱神的道；乃是由于诚实，由于神，在神面前凭着基督讲道。」（2:17）「为利混乱」原文是小贩的用词，当时的小贩常常往酒里掺水、短斤缺两；希腊文旧约译本的以赛亚书1:22，「你的酒用水搀对」，就用了同一组词。有些人把神的道当作生意：怎么讲能吸引人、能多收奉献，就怎么讲；人爱听什么，就加什么，不爱听的就删掉。今天，把信仰包装成发财、健康、成功的秘诀，或者用讲道来经营个人的名气和收入，都是这种「为利混乱神的道」。保罗并不反对传道人从教会得供应（林前9:14）；他反对的是为了利益去改变神的道。这也问我们这些听道的人：我是不是只想听叫我顺利发达的道？

> 要点：神在基督里得胜，把我们这些被他征服的人带在他得胜的行列中，藉着我们散发基督的香气。传道的人凭自己当不起这职分，只能凭着诚实，在神面前凭着基督讲道，不为利益改变神的话。每一个信徒也都带着他的香气；我们没有人当得起，我们所能承担的，乃是出于神（3:5）。

## 默想

**默想：** 「倒不如赦免他，安慰他，免得他忧愁太过，甚至沉沦了。所以我劝你们，要向他显出坚定不移的爱心来。」（2:7-8）在我的教会里，是否有一位曾经跌倒、受过管教、后来真心悔改的弟兄或姊妹？我是否嘴上说已经原谅他，心里却一直和他保持距离，或者在别人面前提起他过去的事？（如果他曾经伤害过我或我的家人，保持安全的距离是对的；饶恕不等于要我回到危险里，也不等于要我对已经发生的罪行保持沉默。这里问的，是出于记恨或轻看的那种距离。）也许我自己就是那个跌倒过的人：我已经认了罪，可是每次走进教会，总觉得人人都记得，自己也不敢相信神真的赦免了我，好像快被忧愁吞没了。主耶稣讲过一位父亲：浪子「相离还远，他父亲看见，就动了慈心，跑去抱着他的颈项，连连与他亲嘴」（路15:20）。在十字架上，主耶稣已经担当了我的罪和我的羞辱；神在基督里赦免了我，就不再记念我的罪（来8:12）。如果我还没有被人接纳，我可以先在神那里得着安息；如果我是那个还在保持距离的人，我要留意：那个故事里还有一个站在门外、不肯进去的大儿子，「他父亲就出来劝他」（路15:28）。我自己也是这样被父亲劝进去的蒙赦免的人。今天，我能不能为那个跌倒后回来的人（或者为我自己）祷告，求神叫赦免不只停在嘴上，也成为真实的接纳？那位父亲没有等儿子把认罪的话说完，就吩咐人把上好的袍子拿来；神在基督里接纳我，也是这样。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 2 has three parts. **The tearful letter** (vv. 1–4): Paul explains why he wrote a letter instead of coming to Corinth again. He wrote it "out of much affliction and anguish of heart and with many tears," "to let you know the abundant love that I have for you." **Forgiving the offender** (vv. 5–11): someone in the church had caused pain to Paul and to them all, and had been punished. Paul urges them "to forgive and comfort him... so that we would not be outwitted by Satan." **From Troas to triumph** (vv. 12–17): Paul could not find Titus at Troas, was restless, and went on to Macedonia. At this point he suddenly bursts into thanks: "But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession."

This chapter shows a pastor's heart. He rebukes because he loves. He wants the church to discipline a sinner, and to welcome him once he repents.

## Going Deeper 2:1–4 A Letter Written with Tears

**1. Not another painful visit.** "For I made up my mind not to make another painful visit to you" (2:1). "Another painful visit" shows that Paul's last visit to Corinth had been painful (cf. 13:2). He did not want to repeat it, so he wrote first.

**2. Joy bound together.** "For if I cause you pain, who is there to make me glad but the one whom I have pained?" (2:2). Paul's joy and the Corinthians' joy are bound together. He is not a leader standing above them, untouched. When the church grieves, he grieves.

**3. Rebuke with tears.** "For I wrote to you out of much affliction and anguish of heart and with many tears, not to cause you pain but to let you know the abundant love that I have for you" (2:4). The letter said severe things, but its writer wept as he wrote. True rebuke is not venting anger or proving oneself right. It comes from love and aims to win people back. Some think love means never saying hard things; others think saying hard things is love. Paul is neither. He said hard words with tears in his heart.

> The point: Paul's severe words were written with tears. Rebuke that comes from love aims not to hurt but to turn people back, and the one who speaks it is hurting too.

## Going Deeper 2:5–11 Forgive and Comfort Him

**1. Who caused the pain?** "Now if anyone has caused pain, he has caused it not to me, but in some measure- not to put it too severely- to all of you" (2:5). Many older interpreters (and a few modern ones, such as Philip E. Hughes) identified this man with the man in 1 Corinthians 5 who had his father's wife. Most interpreters today think it was someone else: probably a man who, during the painful visit, publicly opposed and wounded Paul, or perhaps wronged one of his co-workers (cf. 7:12, "the one who did the wrong... the one who suffered the wrong"). Either way, the principle is the same: a man who sinned was disciplined by the church and has now repented.

**2. The punishment is enough.** "For such a one, this punishment by the majority is enough" (2:6). "The majority" (or "the many," perhaps meaning the whole assembly) shows that this was a decision of the church together. Some think a minority dissented, wanting either a harsher penalty or none; the text does not say. Discipline has a purpose: to bring a person to repentance, not to keep him ashamed forever. When the purpose is reached, the discipline should stop.

**3. Not overwhelmed by sorrow.** "So you should rather turn to forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. So I beg you to reaffirm your love for him" (2:7–8). "Overwhelmed" is literally "swallowed up," like someone drowning in grief. If the church keeps a repentant man at arm's length, watches him with suspicion and talks about him behind his back, he may be swallowed up by sorrow, even to the point of leaving the faith. "Reaffirm" is a legal term for formally ratifying something. Paul wants the church to welcome him back as publicly and clearly as it once disciplined him.

**4. Forgiving in the presence of Christ.** "Anyone whom you forgive, I also forgive. What I have forgiven, if I have forgiven anything, has been for your sake in the presence of Christ" (2:10). If the man had sinned against Paul himself, then Paul, the wounded one, is the first to say, "I forgive him." He can do this because he stands before Christ. He himself is a sinner Christ has forgiven (1 Tim 1:15). We too can forgive "as God in Christ forgave you" (Eph 4:32).

**5. Satan's designs.** "...so that we would not be outwitted by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his designs" (2:11). Satan can use a church's laxity to make it tolerate sin. He can also use its severity to drive a repentant person to despair and make the church cold and divided. Both are his designs.

**6. Forgiveness does not restore everything as it was.** This passage is about a repentant man being received back into fellowship. It does not mean every sin can simply be dropped. Where someone has committed abuse, sexual assault or another crime:
- reporting it to the police and the courts is right, for the governing authority is "the servant of God... who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (Rom 13:4);
- victims need not reconcile with someone unsafe, nor stay in danger;
- forgiveness and return to fellowship do not restore his office or give him access again to victims or children;
- the church must not hide such things to save face; "don't air the family's dirty laundry" (家丑不可外扬) is not a gospel principle.

Here Paul, the one most wronged, forgives first, and he urges the church to end its discipline of a man who has repented. He does not tell a victim of abuse to reconcile, to trust again, or to forgive on someone else's timetable.

> The point: The aim of discipline is restoration, not humiliation. When the sinner truly repents, the church should forgive him, comfort him and publicly reaffirm its love, because we too are forgiven in Christ. Forgiving him does not mean putting him back where he can harm others.

## Going Deeper 2:12–13 An Open Door and a Restless Heart

**1. An open door.** "When I came to Troas to preach the gospel of Christ, even though a door was opened for me in the Lord" (2:12). Troas was a port in the northwest of Asia. Years before, Paul had seen the vision of "a man of Macedonia" there (Acts 16:8–10). Now the Lord opened a door for the gospel there.

**2. He left anyway.** "My spirit was not at rest because I did not find my brother Titus there. So I took leave of them and went on to Macedonia" (2:13). Paul was waiting for Titus to bring news from Corinth. He was so anxious about the Corinthian church that he could not settle at Troas. Here we see that an apostle was human: he could be anxious, preoccupied, unable to rest because of a church's condition (cf. 11:28).

**3. The story breaks off.** Paul stops the story here and does not pick it up again until 7:5: "For even when we came into Macedonia..." The long section in between (2:14–7:4) is Paul's reflection on new covenant ministry. It is as if he says: speaking of my anxiety reminds me how God still triumphs in my weakness.

> The point: An apostle could be anxious, preoccupied and restless. God did not stop working because of his weakness, and at his most restless Paul could still say, "But thanks be to God!"

## Going Deeper 2:14–17 Led in Triumph, the Aroma of Christ

**1. The triumphal procession.** "But thanks be to God, who in Christ always leads us in triumphal procession, and through us spreads the fragrance of the knowledge of him everywhere" (2:14). "Leads in triumphal procession" is the language of the Roman triumph. A victorious general returned to Rome and paraded through the city with his army and his captives, displaying his victory to all. In that period the word usually meant the victor leading conquered captives, so many interpreters think Paul pictures himself as a captive conquered by God in Christ. He once persecuted the church; now God has taken him captive, and he displays Christ's victory everywhere. Some (such as Scott Hafemann) stress that captives in a triumph were often led to execution, which ties 2:14 to the "death" of 2:16 and 4:10–11. Others think Paul means he shares in the victor's procession; the CUV's rendering ("leads us to boast in triumph") leans that way.

**2. Fragrance.** Incense was often burned along the route of a triumph, filling the streets with its scent. In the Old Testament, sacrifices are often called "a pleasing aroma to the LORD" (Num 15:3). Paul says God "through us spreads the fragrance of the knowledge of him everywhere." Christ himself "gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2), and those who proclaim him carry his fragrance.

**3. A fragrance of death, a fragrance of life.** "For we are the aroma of Christ to God among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing, to one a fragrance from death to death, to the other a fragrance from life to life" (2:15–16). The same gospel is life to those who believe and judgment to those who reject it (cf. 1 Cor 1:18). This does not make the preacher responsible for the outcome. It means the gospel is never a matter of indifference: those who hear it move either toward life or toward death. As I read this, I must ask myself: having heard this gospel, am I moving toward life or toward death?

**4. Who is sufficient?** "Who is sufficient for these things?" (2:16). Before so solemn a ministry, no one is sufficient in himself. Paul gives the answer in 3:5: "our sufficiency is from God."

**5. Not peddlers of God's word.** "For we are not, like so many, peddlers of God's word, but as men of sincerity, as commissioned by God, in the sight of God we speak in Christ" (2:17). "Peddlers" translates a word for small traders, who were known for watering their wine and cheating on weights; the Greek Old Testament uses the same word group in Isaiah 1:22, "your best wine mixed with water." Some treat God's word as a business: they preach whatever draws people and brings in offerings, adding what people like and cutting what they don't. Today, packaging faith as the secret of wealth, health and success, or using preaching to build a personal brand and income, is this kind of peddling. Paul did not oppose preachers being supported by the church (1 Cor 9:14). He opposed changing God's word for gain. It also asks those of us who listen: do I only want to hear the message that makes me prosper?

> The point: God triumphs in Christ, leads us, whom he has conquered, in his triumphal procession, and through us spreads the fragrance of Christ. No preacher is sufficient for this; we can only speak in Christ with sincerity, before God, never changing his word for gain. Every believer carries this fragrance too; none of us is sufficient, and our sufficiency is from God (3:5).

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "So you should rather turn to forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. So I beg you to reaffirm your love for him" (2:7–8). Is there a brother or sister in my church who fell, was disciplined, and has since truly repented? Do I say I have forgiven him, yet keep my distance, or bring up his past in front of others? (If he once harmed me or my family, keeping a safe distance is right; forgiving him does not mean going back into danger, or keeping quiet about a crime. The question here is about distance that comes from resentment or contempt.) Perhaps I am the one who fell. I have confessed my sin, but every time I walk into church I feel everyone remembers, and I cannot quite believe God has really forgiven me. I feel close to being swallowed up by sorrow. Jesus told of a father whose son came home: "while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). On the cross the Lord Jesus has already borne my sin and my shame. God has forgiven me in Christ and remembers my sins no more (Heb 8:12). If people have not yet welcomed me back, I can first find rest with God. If I am the one keeping my distance, I should notice that the story also has an elder son who stood outside and would not go in, and "his father came out and entreated him" (Luke 15:28). I myself am a forgiven sinner the Father has pleaded with like that. Today, can I pray for the one who fell and came back (or for myself), asking God to let forgiveness move from my lips to a real welcome? The father did not wait for his son to finish his confession before calling for the best robe; that is how God in Christ has welcomed me.
