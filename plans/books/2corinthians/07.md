## 本章脉络

第七章接着第六章的劝勉，然后回到2:13中断的故事。**心地宽大收纳我们**（2-4节）：保罗再次请哥林多人向他敞开心，「你们常在我们心里，情愿与你们同生同死」。**提多带来的安慰**（5-7节）：保罗到了马其顿，「外有争战，内有惧怕」；「但那安慰丧气之人的神藉着提多来安慰了我们」。**两种忧愁**（8-13节上）：那封严厉的信叫哥林多人忧愁，但他们是「依着神的意思忧愁」，生出了悔改。**提多的喜乐**（13节下-16节）：哥林多人怎样接待提多，保罗就「能在凡事上为你们放心」。

第1节「我们既有这等应许，就当洁净自己」是上一章的总结，已在第六章讲过。这一章的中心是7:10：「依着神的意思忧愁，就生出没有后悔的懊悔来，以致得救；但世俗的忧愁是叫人死。」

## 深读 7:2-4 你们常在我们心里

**1. 收纳我们。** 「你们要心地宽大收纳我们。我们未曾亏负谁，未曾败坏谁，未曾占谁的便宜。」（7:2）这里接着6:13「你们也要照样用宽宏的心报答我」。保罗为自己辩护，大概是因为有人指控他在钱财上有问题。「占便宜」这个词在12:17-18又出现，那里保罗问：「提多占过你们的便宜吗？」

**2. 同生同死。** 「我说这话，不是要定你们的罪。我已经说过，你们常在我们心里，情愿与你们同生同死。」（7:3）这是至死忠诚的话，像以太对大卫说的「无论生死」（撒下15:21）：生也好，死也好，都不分离。一位使徒对一个曾经伤害他的教会这样说，可见他的爱是真实的。

**3. 在患难中快乐。** 「我们在一切患难中分外的快乐。」（7:4）保罗的快乐不是因为没有患难，而是因为神在患难中安慰他，也因为他看见哥林多人的回转。

> 要点：保罗的爱不是有条件的；即使受了伤害，他仍然说：「你们常在我们心里。」他的爱反映了基督的爱：主爱我们，在我们还作罪人的时候就为我们死（罗5:8）。

## 深读 7:5-7 安慰丧气之人的神

**1. 外有争战，内有惧怕。** 「我们从前就是到了马其顿的时候，身体也不得安宁，周围遭患难，外有争战，内有惧怕。」（7:5）保罗没有把自己说成一位从不害怕的英雄。他身体疲倦，外面有冲突，里面有惧怕：他担心哥林多教会会怎样回应那封信，担心提多的安全。

**2. 那安慰丧气之人的神。** 「但那安慰丧气之人的神藉着提多来安慰了我们。」（7:6）「丧气之人」原文是指卑微、低落、被压下去的人。以赛亚书说：「耶和华已经安慰他的百姓，也要怜恤他困苦之民。」（赛49:13）这节经文在七十士译本（旧约希腊文译本）里说，神安慰他百姓中卑微的人，用的正是保罗这里的词。保罗在6:2刚刚引过同一章（赛49:8）。神特别靠近这样的人。

**3. 神藉着人安慰人。** 「不但藉着他来，也藉着他从你们所得的安慰，安慰了我们；因他把你们的想念、哀恸，和向我的热心，都告诉了我，叫我更加欢喜。」（7:7）神的安慰常常藉着人而来：一位朋友的到来，一个好消息，一句真心的话。保罗在第一章说他要「用神所赐的安慰去安慰」人（1:4）；在这里，他自己也被人安慰了。在教会里，安慰是彼此传递的。

> 要点：神是「安慰丧气之人的神」。保罗不以「内有惧怕」为耻；神偏偏靠近被压下去的人，常常藉着一位弟兄的到来、一个好消息，把安慰带给他们。

## 深读 7:8-13上 两种忧愁

**1. 叫人忧愁的信。** 「我先前写信叫你们忧愁，我后来虽然懊悔，如今却不懊悔；因我知道，那信叫你们忧愁不过是暂时的。」（7:8）保罗写了那封信以后，也曾后悔，担心自己说得太重。这让我们看见一位牧者的挣扎：说真话是对的，但说完以后，心里也不好受。

**2. 不是因为你们忧愁而欢喜。** 「如今我欢喜，不是因你们忧愁，是因你们从忧愁中生出懊悔来。」（7:9）保罗不是喜欢看别人难过。他欢喜，是因为忧愁结出了果子：悔改。「懊悔」在这里就是悔改；这个词在新约里指整个人转离罪、归向神。

**3. 两种忧愁。** 「因为依着神的意思忧愁，就生出没有后悔的懊悔来，以致得救；但世俗的忧愁是叫人死。」（7:10）
- **依着神的意思忧愁：** 是因为得罪了神而忧伤，这忧伤把人带到神面前，生出悔改，以致得救。多数解经家认为「没有后悔」是形容这悔改：这样的悔改，人永远不会后悔。
- **世俗的忧愁：** 是因为罪的后果、因为被人发现、因为丢了面子而难过。这种忧愁只看自己，不看神，可能叫人自怨自艾、自我厌恶、自暴自弃，却不会把人带到神面前，最后「叫人死」。

新约中有两个人，都在主受难的那一夜犯了大罪。彼得三次不认主，「主转过身来看彼得……他就出去痛哭」（路22:61-62）；后来复活的主三次问他「你爱我吗」，把他挽回过来（约21:15-17）。犹大卖了主，后来也「后悔」，承认「我卖了无辜之人的血是有罪了」（太27:3-4），可是他没有回到主那里去。两个人都很痛苦，但一个人的痛苦把他带回到主面前，另一个人的痛苦却叫他离开了主。（马太在这里用的「后悔」，和保罗在7:8说自己「懊悔」是同一个词。可见分别不在用词，而在于这忧愁有没有把人带回主那里。）

**4. 悔改的果子。** 「你看，你们依着神的意思忧愁，从此就生出何等的殷勤、自诉、自恨、恐惧、想念、热心、责罚。」（7:11）真实的悔改不只是心里难过，也会结出行动：认真处理罪（殷勤），愿意澄清（自诉），对罪的愤恨（自恨；这里不是厌弃自己这个人，而是对那件罪的义愤），存敬畏的心（恐惧，可能是怕保罗带着刑杖来，参林前4:21，也是敬畏神），想念和热心（都是向着保罗，参7:7），以及按公义处理犯错的人（责罚，参2:6）。

**5. 信的目的。** 「我虽然从前写信给你们，却不是为那亏负人的，也不是为那受人亏负的，乃要在神面前把你们顾念我们的热心表明出来。」（7:12）保罗写信最终的目的，不是只为处理一个人的问题，而是要整个教会在神面前看清楚自己的心。

> 要点：世俗的忧愁只为后果和面子难过，最后叫人离开神；依着神的意思忧愁，却把人带到神面前，生出悔改，以致得救。真实的悔改不只是难过，也会结出行动的果子；而领我们走上这条路的，是神的恩慈，不是我们对自己的惩罚。

## 深读 7:13下-16 提多的喜乐

**1. 提多心里畅快。** 「并且在安慰之中，因你们众人使提多心里畅快欢喜，我们就更加欢喜了。」（7:13）提多大概带着那封严厉的信去了哥林多，心里很可能也很紧张。结果哥林多人接待了他，提多的心「畅快」了，原文是得了安息、重新得力的意思。

**2. 夸奖的话成了真的。** 「我若对提多夸奖了你们什么，也觉得没有惭愧；因我对提多夸奖你们的话成了真的，正如我对你们所说的话也都是真的。」（7:14）保罗在提多面前曾经称赞哥林多人。在他们最糟糕的时候，保罗仍然看见神在他们身上的工作。

**3. 恐惧战兢地接待他。** 「并且提多想起你们众人的顺服，是怎样恐惧战兢地接待他，他爱你们的心肠就越发热了。」（7:15）「恐惧战兢」不是害怕提多会伤害他们，而是在神面前认真、谦卑的态度（参腓2:12）。

**4. 放心。** 「我如今欢喜，能在凡事上为你们放心。」（7:16）第一至七章的主线到这里告一段落：关系恢复了。接下来，保罗可以放心地谈到下一个题目：为耶路撒冷圣徒的捐献。

> 要点：保罗在教会最糟糕的时候，仍然看见神在他们身上的工作，也敢在别人面前称赞他们。悔改带来关系的恢复，也带来彼此的喜乐。

## 默想

**默想：** 「因为依着神的意思忧愁，就生出没有后悔的懊悔来，以致得救；但世俗的忧愁是叫人死。」（7:10）当我犯了罪，我心里的难过是哪一种？我是否主要是因为被人发现、怕丢面子、怕承担后果而难过？我是否一遍又一遍地责备自己、讨厌自己，以为这样难过就是悔改，却始终不敢来到神面前？这样的忧愁只会叫我越陷越深。神给我的不是一条靠自我惩罚来赎罪的路，因为主耶稣已经在十字架上为我的罪受了刑罚。彼得三次不认主，复活的主却在海边为他预备了早饭，三次问他「你爱我吗」，把他挽回过来（约21:9-17）。神「丰富的恩慈」原是「领你悔改」的（罗2:4）。今天，我能不能停止在自责中打转，把那件事直接告诉神，具体地承认它，相信「我们若认自己的罪，神是信实的，是公义的，必要赦免我们的罪」（约一1:9），然后问他：主啊，我当怎样纠正、怎样修复？如果这件事伤害了别人，甚至触犯了法律，依着神的意思忧愁不会躲开后果，而会诚实地承担；但我不可以要求受伤的人马上原谅我，或者再与我亲近。彼得出去痛哭，可是眼泪不是他故事的结局；结局是岸上那堆炭火和主的呼唤。我回到主面前的时候，迎接我的不是一份罪状，而是那位已经替我受了刑罚的主。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 7 continues the appeal of chapter 6 and then picks up the story broken off at 2:13. **Make room for us** (vv. 2–4): Paul again asks the Corinthians to open their hearts: "you are in our hearts, to die together and to live together." **Comfort through Titus** (vv. 5–7): in Macedonia there was "fighting without and fear within," "but God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus." **Two kinds of grief** (vv. 8–13a): the severe letter grieved the Corinthians, but they "felt a godly grief" that led to repentance. **Titus's joy** (vv. 13b–16): because of how the Corinthians received Titus, Paul has "perfect confidence in you."

Verse 1, "Since we have these promises... let us cleanse ourselves," sums up the previous chapter and was treated there. The center of this chapter is 7:10: "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death."

## Going Deeper 7:2–4 You Are in Our Hearts

**1. Make room for us.** "Make room in your hearts for us. We have wronged no one, we have corrupted no one, we have taken advantage of no one" (7:2). This picks up 6:13, "widen your hearts also." Paul defends himself, probably because some had accused him of misconduct with money. The verb "take advantage" recurs in 12:17–18: "Did Titus take advantage of you?"

**2. To die together and to live together.** "I do not say this to condemn you, for I said before that you are in our hearts, to die together and to live together" (7:3). This is the language of loyalty to death (cf. Ittai to David, 2 Sam 15:21, "whether for death or for life"): in life or death, never parted. That an apostle says this to a church that had hurt him shows his love is real.

**3. Joy in affliction.** "In all our affliction, I am overflowing with joy" (7:4). Paul's joy is not the absence of affliction. It comes because God comforts him in it, and because he has seen the Corinthians turn back.

> The point: Paul's love was not conditional; even after being hurt he could say, "you are in our hearts." His love reflects Christ's, who loved us and died for us while we were still sinners (Rom 5:8).

## Going Deeper 7:5–7 God Who Comforts the Downcast

**1. Fighting without, fear within.** "For even when we came into Macedonia, our bodies had no rest, but we were afflicted at every turn- fighting without and fear within" (7:5). Paul does not present himself as a hero who is never afraid. His body was tired, there was conflict outside and fear inside: fear about how the Corinthians would answer the letter, fear for Titus's safety.

**2. God who comforts the downcast.** "But God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus" (7:6). "The downcast" means the lowly, the low in spirit, those pressed down. Isaiah says, "the LORD has comforted his people and will have compassion on his afflicted" (Isa 49:13). In the Greek version Isaiah 49:13 says God "comforted the lowly of his people," the very word Paul uses here; Paul has just quoted the same chapter (Isa 49:8) in 6:2. God draws especially near to such people.

**3. God comforts through people.** "...and not only by his coming but also by the comfort with which he was comforted by you, as he told us of your longing, your mourning, your zeal for me, so that I rejoiced still more" (7:7). God's comfort often comes through people: a friend's arrival, good news, a sincere word. In chapter 1 Paul said he would comfort others "with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God" (1:4); here he himself is comforted by others. In the church, comfort is passed back and forth.

> The point: God is the God "who comforts the downcast." Paul was not ashamed to admit "fear within"; God draws near precisely to those pressed down, often through a brother's arrival or a piece of good news.

## Going Deeper 7:8–13a Two Kinds of Grief

**1. A letter that grieved.** "For even if I made you grieve with my letter, I do not regret it- though I did regret it, for I see that that letter grieved you, though only for a while" (7:8). After writing the letter, Paul too had regrets, worried he had said too much. Here we see a pastor's struggle: speaking the truth was right, but afterwards it still hurt.

**2. Not glad that you grieved.** "As it is, I rejoice, not because you were grieved, but because you were grieved into repenting" (7:9). Paul did not enjoy seeing others hurt. He rejoiced because the grief bore fruit: repentance. In the New Testament the word means a whole-person turning from sin to God.

**3. Two kinds of grief.** "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (7:10).
- **Godly grief:** sorrow for having sinned against God. It brings a person to God, produces repentance and leads to salvation. Most take "without regret" with repentance: no one ever regrets that kind of repentance.
- **Worldly grief:** sorrow over sin's consequences, over being found out, over losing face. It looks only at oneself, not at God. It can lead to self-hatred, self-blame and giving up, but it does not bring a person to God, and in the end it "produces death."

The New Testament tells of two men who both sinned greatly on the night of the Lord's suffering. Peter denied the Lord three times, "and the Lord turned and looked at Peter... And he went out and wept bitterly" (Luke 22:61–62); later the risen Lord asked him three times, "Do you love me?" and restored him (John 21:15–17). Judas betrayed the Lord and afterwards also "changed his mind," confessing, "I have sinned by betraying innocent blood" (Matt 27:3–4), but he did not go back to the Lord. Both men were in anguish; one man's anguish brought him back to the Lord, the other's took him away from him. (Matthew's word for Judas's remorse is the same verb Paul uses of his own regret in 7:8, so the difference is not in the word but in whether the grief turned the man back to the Lord.)

**4. The fruit of repentance.** "For see what earnestness this godly grief has produced in you, but also what eagerness to clear yourselves, what indignation, what fear, what longing, what zeal, what punishment!" (7:11). Real repentance is not only feeling bad; it bears fruit in action: dealing with sin seriously (earnestness), willingness to set things straight (eagerness to clear yourselves), anger at sin (indignation; not loathing oneself, but being angry at the sin itself), fear (perhaps of Paul's coming "with a rod," and of God; cf. 1 Cor 4:21), longing and zeal (both directed toward Paul, cf. 7:7), and punishment, that is, justice carried out on the offender (cf. 2:6).

**5. The letter's purpose.** "So although I wrote to you, it was not for the sake of the one who did the wrong, nor for the sake of the one who suffered the wrong, but in order that your earnestness for us might be revealed to you in the sight of God" (7:12). Paul's ultimate aim was not only to deal with one man's problem but for the whole church to see its own heart clearly before God.

> The point: Worldly grief mourns only consequences and lost face, and in the end draws us away from God. Godly grief brings us to God and produces repentance that leads to salvation. Real repentance is more than sorrow; it bears fruit in action, and what leads us there is God's kindness, not our punishing ourselves.

## Going Deeper 7:13b–16 Titus's Joy

**1. Titus refreshed.** "And besides our own comfort, we rejoiced still more at the joy of Titus, because his spirit has been refreshed by you all" (7:13). Titus had probably carried the severe letter to Corinth, and was likely anxious. In the end the Corinthians received him, and his spirit was "refreshed," given rest and renewed strength.

**2. Boasting proved true.** "For whatever boasts I made to him about you, I was not put to shame. But just as everything we said to you was true, so also our boasting before Titus has proved true" (7:14). Paul had praised the Corinthians to Titus. Even at their worst, Paul could still see God's work in them.

**3. Received with fear and trembling.** "And his affection for you is even greater, as he remembers the obedience of you all, how you received him with fear and trembling" (7:15). "Fear and trembling" does not mean they were afraid Titus would harm them. It describes a serious and humble attitude before God (cf. Phil 2:12).

**4. Confidence.** "I rejoice, because I have perfect confidence in you" (7:16). This sentence closes the main thread of chapters 1–7: the relationship has been restored. Next Paul can turn with confidence to his next subject, the collection for the saints in Jerusalem.

> The point: At the church's worst, Paul could still see God's work in them and dared to praise them to others. Repentance brings restored relationships and shared joy.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (7:10). When I have sinned, which kind of grief do I feel? Am I sorry mainly because I was found out, because I am afraid of losing face or of facing the consequences? Do I blame and despise myself again and again, thinking that feeling bad is the same as repenting, yet never dare to come to God? That kind of grief only pulls me deeper. God has not given me a way of paying for sin by punishing myself, because the Lord Jesus has already borne the punishment for my sin on the cross. Peter denied the Lord three times, yet the risen Lord made him breakfast by the shore, asked him three times, "Do you love me?" and restored him (John 21:9–17). "God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance" (Rom 2:4). Today, can I stop going round in circles of self-blame, tell God plainly about that thing, confess it specifically, believe that "if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9), and then ask him: Lord, how should I put it right, and what needs repairing? If what I did has hurt someone, or even broken the law, godly grief does not dodge the consequences; it owns them honestly. But I may not demand that the person I hurt forgive me at once or let me close again. Peter went out and wept, but his tears were not the end of his story; the end was a charcoal fire on the shore and the Lord calling him. When I come back to the Lord, what meets me is not a charge sheet but the Lord who has already borne my punishment.
