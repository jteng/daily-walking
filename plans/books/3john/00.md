## 这卷书在讲什么

按希腊文的字数算，约翰三书是新约里最短的一卷书，比约翰二书还要短一些。它和约翰二书一样，是「作长老的」写的（1:1），却不是写给一间教会，而是写给一个人：「亲爱的该犹」。这是一封很私人的信，读起来像一位年老的牧者，写给一位忠心弟兄的便条。

信里出现了三个人：
- **该犹：** 他「按真理而行」（1:3），忠心地接待那些出外传道的弟兄，虽然他们原本是陌生人（1:5）。长老称赞他，又请他继续帮助他们往前行。
- **丢特腓：** 他「在教会中好为首」（1:9），不接待长老所差来的人，用恶言妄论长老，还禁止别人接待弟兄，甚至把接待弟兄的人赶出教会（1:10）。
- **低米丢：** 他「有众人给他作见证，又有真理给他作见证」（1:12）。许多人认为他就是带这封信去的人，长老为他写了一句推荐的话。

约翰二书说的是：不要接待那些否认基督的人（约二1:10）。约翰三书说的却是另一面：要接待那些为主的名出外的人。两封信放在一起，让我们看见真理和接待的关系：拒绝谁、接待谁，都要按真理来定。约翰说，我们接待忠心的传道人，就是「与他们一同为真理做工」（1:8）。

这封信还有一个特别的地方：全信没有直接提到「耶稣」或「基督」的名字。可是这名字在信的中心。和合本说那些弟兄是「为主的名出外」（1:7），原文只说「为那名」。当时的信徒不用人解释，都知道「那名」是谁的名：就是那「超乎万名之上的名」（腓2:9）。在旧约圣经里，单说「那名」，指的是耶和华自己的名（利24:11，和合本作「圣名」）；初期教会把这个称呼用在耶稣身上，正表明他们承认他就是主。

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 作者和时间。** 作者和约翰二书一样，自称「作长老的」（1:1）。约翰一书导读和约翰二书导读都讲过作者的问题；本研读采用传统的看法：作者是年老的使徒约翰。三封信大概都写于主后85-95年，长老当时可能住在以弗所。早期教会接受这封信，也和约翰二书一样比较慢：优西比乌把这两封信都列在有人质疑的书卷中（《教会史》第三卷第二十五章）。优西比乌说，有人不确定这两封信是福音书作者约翰写的，还是另一位同名的人写的；另外，它们很短，又是私人的信，教会也不常宣读、引用。三封信中，早期教父引用得最少的，就是约翰三书。

**2. 该犹是谁？** 该犹是罗马帝国中最常见的名字之一，新约里就有好几位该犹：哥林多的该犹，保罗曾为他施洗，他也接待保罗和全教会（罗16:23；林前1:14）；马其顿人该犹（徒19:29）；特庇人该犹（徒20:4）。这几位不一定就是约翰三书的该犹。四世纪的《使徒宪典》说，约翰立了一位该犹作别迦摩的监督，但这个记载很晚，不能确定。我们只知道，他是长老所爱的，很可能是长老带领信主的（「我的儿女们」，1:4），也是一位乐意接待人的信徒。

**3. 巡回传道的人。** 导读约翰二书时讲过，当时的传道人从一个城走到另一个城，住在信徒家中，靠信徒供给。这封信里说的弟兄，是长老所差、或至少是长老所认识和推荐的人。他们「为主的名出外，对于外邦人一无所取」（1:7），就是不向不信主的外邦人收取供给，免得人以为他们传福音是为了钱。亚伯兰也曾对所多玛王说，一根线、一根鞋带都不拿，「免得你说：『我使亚伯兰富足！』」（创14:23）当时有许多四处游走的哲学家和宗教人士，靠向听众讨钱为生；这些传道人却不这样做。所以信徒供给他们，就格外重要。

**4. 荐信的习惯。** 早期教会常用书信来推荐信徒，让别处的教会接待他们。保罗就这样推荐过非比：「请你们为主接待她，合乎圣徒的体统。」（罗16:2）亚波罗往亚该亚去的时候，弟兄们也「写信请门徒接待他」（徒18:27）；保罗也提到别人用的「荐信」（林后3:1）。许多学者认为，约翰三书就是这样一封信，为低米丢写的（1:12）。

**5. 「我曾略略地写信给教会」。** 长老说，他曾写信给该犹所在的教会，但丢特腓不接待他们（1:9）。这封信是哪一封？主要有两种看法：
- **约翰二书：** 有人认为就是约翰二书，主要是因为约翰二书也是写给一间教会的；也有少数人想到约翰一书。不过约翰二书的内容是警告假教师，和接待长老所差的弟兄关系不大。
- **一封已经失传的信：** 多数学者认为，这是一封我们今天看不到的信，可能是推荐那些弟兄的荐信，被丢特腓扣下来了。

**6. 丢特腓为什么这样做？** 信里只说他「好为首」（1:9），没有说他传假道理。学者有几种推测：
- **个人的野心：** 这是最直接的读法，也是多数释经者（如斯托得）的看法。丢特腓想在教会中掌权，不愿意别人的权柄在他之上。
- **教会治理的转变：** 德国学者阿道夫·冯·哈纳克认为，当时地方教会正开始由一位监督来治理，丢特腓代表的就是这种新兴的制度；长老代表的却是到处巡视的使徒权柄，于是双方起了冲突。
- **教义的冲突：** 另一位德国学者恩斯特·凯斯曼甚至提出，被看作异端的其实是长老，丢特腓是正统教会的领袖。不过这个看法很少人接受，因为信里看不出丢特腓在教义上和长老有什么分歧，而长老所说的「真理」正是约翰一书和二书所传的。
这几种看法也不一定互相排斥：个人的野心，也可能和教会治理的变化交织在一起。无论如何，约翰所责备的，是丢特腓的心：他好为首。这是教会在每个时代都会遇到的问题。

## 钥匙经文：1:4；1:8；1:11

**1. 「我的喜乐就没有比这个大的」。** 「我听见我的儿女们按真理而行，我的喜乐就没有比这个大的。」（1:4）长老最大的喜乐，不是教会人数增加，也不是自己受人敬重，而是他属灵的儿女「按真理而行」。这里的「儿女」，可能指长老带领信主的人，也可能泛指他所牧养的信徒。这句话让我们看见一位牧者的心：他的喜乐在别人的生命里。

**2. 「与他们一同为真理做工」。** 「所以我们应该接待这样的人，叫我们与他们一同为真理做工。」（1:8）不是每个信徒都要出外传道，但每个信徒都可以和传道的人「一同做工」。在家里接待他们，在路费和生活上供给他们，为他们祷告，都是真实的福音工作。「为真理做工」也可以理解为与真理一同做工：真理自己在做工，我们是和真理同工的人。在约翰的书信里，真理不是一个抽象的观念，而是关于基督的福音；基督自己就是真理（约14:6），圣灵也为他作见证，「圣灵就是真理」（约一5:6）。

**3. 「行善的属乎神」。** 「亲爱的兄弟啊，不要效法恶，只要效法善。行善的属乎神；行恶的未曾见过神。」（1:11）这是把约翰一书的教导，用在一件很具体的事上：该犹面前有两个榜样，一个是丢特腓，一个是低米丢。「未曾见过神」和约翰一书3:6的话很像。这不是说行善可以使人属神，而是说，一个人属不属神，会在他的行为上显出来。

## 全书结构

约翰三书可以这样分：
- **问安和喜乐**（1-4节）：长老写信给亲爱的该犹；愿他凡事兴盛、身体健壮，正如他的灵魂兴盛；听见儿女们按真理而行，喜乐没有比这个大的。
- **称赞该犹的接待**（5-8节）：该犹向作客旅的弟兄所行的是忠心的；要帮助他们往前行，配得过神；他们为主的名出外；我们应该接待这样的人。
- **责备丢特腓**（9-10节）：他好为首，不接待长老所差的人；用恶言妄论长老；禁止人接待弟兄，又把他们赶出教会。
- **效法善，推荐低米丢**（11-12节）：不要效法恶，只要效法善；低米丢有众人和真理给他作见证。
- **结语**（13-15节）：盼望当面谈论；愿你平安；按着姓名问众位朋友安。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 第2节不是成功神学的根据。** 「亲爱的兄弟啊，我愿你凡事兴盛，身体健壮，正如你的灵魂兴盛一样。」（1:2）有些传成功神学（健康财富福音）的人，常用这一节说：神的旨意是要每个信徒都发财、都健康。不过这节经文不能这样用：
- **这是书信的问安格式：** 当时的希腊文书信，开头常常有一句祝福收信人身体健康的话。长老用了这个常见的格式，却加上「正如你的灵魂兴盛一样」，把重点放在灵魂的兴盛上。
- **这是祝愿，不是应许：** 「兴盛」这个词原本和路途顺利有关，到新约时代一般就是顺利、亨通的意思（罗1:10，和合本译作「得平坦的道路」）。它可以包括生活上的顺利，保罗在林前16:2甚至用它说到各人的「进项」。可是这里是一句祝愿，不是神的应许；长老愿该犹一切顺利，并没有说神保证每个信徒都发财。
- **灵魂的兴盛是标准：** 长老说「正如你的灵魂兴盛一样」，是以该犹灵魂的兴盛为标准。该犹的灵魂怎样兴盛呢？就是他「按真理而行」（1:3）。
不过，我们也不要走到另一个极端，以为神不在乎我们的身体。长老为该犹的身体祷告，是对的。我们也可以为自己和别人的健康、工作、生活向神祈求（太6:11）。

**2. 和约翰二书一起读。** 约翰二书和三书都讲到「接待」：约翰二书说不要接待否认基督的人，约翰三书说要接待为主名出外的人。丢特腓的错，正好和约翰二书所警告的相反：他不接待那些忠心的弟兄。可见守护真理，不是对所有来的人都关门。教会要像该犹一样，看清楚来的人传的是什么，然后忠心地接待、供给那些传真理的人。

**3. 看三个人的画像。** 这封信像是三幅画：该犹、丢特腓、低米丢。读的时候，可以问自己：我在教会里，比较像哪一个？我所接待、帮助的，是谁？我在什么事上「好为首」？这样读，不是要我们只看别人的错，而是让神的话照亮自己的心。

**4. 留意「那名」。** 那些弟兄是「为主的名出外」（1:7）。在使徒行传里，使徒们「因被算是配为这名受辱」而欢喜（徒5:41）。一切的接待、供给和传道，都是为了那一位的名。这封信虽然没有写出「耶稣」这个名字，整封信却是为他的名而写的。

## 默想

**默想：** 「亲爱的兄弟啊，我愿你凡事兴盛，身体健壮，正如你的灵魂兴盛一样。」（1:2）有人拿这一节对一位病了多年的姊妹说：「神要你健康，你还在病中，是因为你的信心不够。」说这话的人，也许就是我。我不一定说得这样直，可是我也有一套现成的解释：谁家出了事，我就猜他是不是什么地方得罪了神；谁的生意垮了，我就想起他从前不太奉献。这样想，好像替神把世界解释清楚了，也让我觉得自己比较安全。

约伯的三个朋友也有一套解释。以利法说：「无辜的人有谁灭亡？正直的人在何处剪除？」（伯4:7）他们说了许多关于神的话，句句听起来都很属灵，约伯却说他们是「安慰人，反叫人愁烦」（伯16:2）。最后耶和华对以利法说：「我的怒气向你和你两个朋友发作，因为你们议论我不如我的仆人约伯说的是。」（伯42:7）原来，用一套整齐的道理去解释别人的苦难，在神面前不是小错。

可是故事没有停在神的怒气上。神吩咐他们献上燔祭，又说：「我的仆人约伯就为你们祈祷。我因悦纳他，就不按你们的愚妄办你们。」（伯42:8）那个被他们冤枉的受苦者，成了为他们代求的人。以赛亚书所说的那位受苦的义仆也是这样：「他却担当多人的罪，又为罪犯代求。」（赛53:12）这叫我想起另一位受苦的义人。以赛亚说：「他诚然担当我们的忧患，背负我们的痛苦；我们却以为他受责罚，被神击打苦待了。」（赛53:4）站在十字架下的人，也用同样的眼光看他：一定是他自己有罪，神才这样惩罚他。其实他受的刑罚不是为他自己，而是为我们：「他为我们的过犯受害，为我们的罪孽压伤」，耶和华使我们众人的罪孽都归在他身上（赛53:5-6）。马太说，耶稣医治病人，就应验了这句话（太8:17）；他的十字架和复活也保证了，我们的身体终必完全得医治。不过那一天是在复活的时候，不一定在今生。

所以，我可以把自己那套解释放下，承认我常常议论神「不如……说的是」，也承认我曾用这些话伤了软弱的人。那位被人以为「受责罚」的主，也为我这样的罪受了刑罚。下一次坐在病人旁边，我不必急着解释，只要陪着他，为他祷告，像长老一样，为他的身体和灵魂一同向神祈求。如果我自己就是那个病了很久、又被人这样议论的人，我也可以放心：神看重像约伯那样向他倾心吐意的人，并没有责备约伯向他诉苦。约伯的降卑（伯42:6），是因为见到了神，不是承认朋友们说得对。神最后没有给约伯一套解释，却把自己给了他：「我从前风闻有你，现在亲眼看见你。」（伯42:5）那位曾被人误会、被神击打的主，也最明白我的处境。

## What This Book Is About

Counted by Greek words, 3 John is the shortest book in the New Testament, a little shorter even than 2 John. Like 2 John, it is from "the elder" (1:1), but it is written not to a church but to one man: "the beloved Gaius." It is a very personal letter, and it reads like a note from an aged pastor to a faithful brother.

Three men appear in the letter:
- **Gaius:** He is "walking in the truth" (1:3). He faithfully receives the brothers who travel out to preach, though they were strangers to him (1:5). The elder commends him and asks him to keep helping them on their way.
- **Diotrephes:** He "likes to put himself first" (1:9). He does not acknowledge the elder's authority or welcome those the elder sends. He talks wicked nonsense against the elder, stops others from welcoming the brothers, and even puts those who do out of the church (1:10).
- **Demetrius:** He "has received a good testimony from everyone, and from the truth itself" (1:12). Many take him to be the one carrying the letter, and the elder adds a word of commendation for him.

Second John says: do not receive those who deny Christ (2 John 1:10). Third John shows the other side: receive those who have gone out for the sake of the name. Taken together, the two letters show how truth and hospitality belong together. Whom we refuse and whom we receive must both be decided by the truth. John says that when we support faithful preachers, we become "fellow workers for the truth" (1:8).

This letter has one more unusual feature: it never names "Jesus" or "Christ." Yet his name stands at the center of the letter. The brothers "have gone out for the sake of the name" (1:7). The Greek says only "the name." The first readers needed no explanation: everyone knew whose name it was, "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9). In the Old Testament, "the Name" on its own meant the LORD's own name (Lev 24:11). The early church gives this title to Jesus, and in doing so confesses him as Lord.

## Author and Setting

**1. Author and date.** As in 2 John, the writer calls himself "the elder" (1:1). The introductions to 1 and 2 John have discussed the question of authorship; this study takes the traditional view that the author is the aged apostle John. All three letters were probably written around AD 85–95, when the elder may have been living in Ephesus. As with 2 John, the early church was slow to accept this letter. Eusebius lists both among the books some disputed (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.25). Eusebius says some were unsure whether they were by John the evangelist or another John. They were also short and personal, and so seldom read aloud or quoted. Of the three letters, 3 John is the least quoted by the early fathers.

**2. Who was Gaius?** Gaius was one of the most common names in the Roman Empire, and the New Testament has several men named Gaius: Gaius of Corinth, whom Paul baptized and who hosted Paul and the whole church (Rom 16:23; 1 Cor 1:14); Gaius the Macedonian (Acts 19:29); and Gaius of Derbe (Acts 20:4). None of them need be the Gaius of 3 John. The fourth-century *Apostolic Constitutions* says John appointed a Gaius as bishop of Pergamum, but this is a late report and cannot be confirmed. We know only that the elder loved him, that the elder probably led him to faith ("my children," 1:4), and that he was glad to welcome people.

**3. Traveling preachers.** As the introduction to 2 John explained, preachers then traveled from city to city, staying in believers' homes and relying on them for support. The brothers in this letter were sent by the elder, or were at least known to him and commended by him. They "have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles" (1:7): they took no support from pagans, so that no one would think they preached the gospel for money. Abram, too, refused to take even a thread or a sandal strap from the king of Sodom, "lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich'" (Gen 14:23). Many wandering philosophers and religious figures in those days lived by asking their hearers for money; these preachers did not. That made believers' support for them all the more important.

**4. Letters of commendation.** The early church often used letters to commend believers, so that churches elsewhere would receive them. Paul commended Phoebe this way: "that you may welcome her in the Lord in a way worthy of the saints" (Rom 16:2). When Apollos crossed to Achaia, the brothers "wrote to the disciples to welcome him" (Acts 18:27), and Paul mentions "letters of recommendation" others used (2 Cor 3:1). Many take 3 John to be such a letter, written for Demetrius (1:12).

**5. "I have written something to the church."** The elder says he wrote to Gaius's church, but Diotrephes would not acknowledge them (1:9). Which letter was it? There are two views:
- **2 John:** Some think it was 2 John, mainly because 2 John is also addressed to a church; a few have suggested 1 John. But 2 John warns against false teachers, which has little to do with receiving the brothers the elder sent.
- **A lost letter:** Most scholars think it was a letter we no longer have, perhaps a letter commending those brothers, which Diotrephes suppressed.

**6. Why did Diotrephes act this way?** The letter says only that he "likes to put himself first" (1:9). It does not say he taught false doctrine. Scholars have offered several suggestions:
- **Personal ambition:** This is the most straightforward reading and the view of most interpreters (Stott, for example). Diotrephes wanted to hold power in the church and would not have anyone's authority over his.
- **A change in church government:** The German scholar Adolf von Harnack thought Diotrephes represented the rising pattern of a single bishop ruling a local church, which clashed with the traveling, apostolic authority the elder represented.
- **A doctrinal conflict:** Another German scholar, Ernst Käsemann, went so far as to suggest that the elder was the one regarded as a heretic, and that Diotrephes was the leader of the orthodox church. Few have accepted this, because the letter shows no doctrinal disagreement between Diotrephes and the elder, and the elder's "truth" is the very truth taught in 1 and 2 John.
These views need not exclude one another: personal ambition may have been tangled up with changes in church government. Whatever the case, what John rebukes is Diotrephes's heart: he loves to be first. The church meets that problem in every age.

## Key Verses: 1:4; 1:8; 1:11

**1. "I have no greater joy."** "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth." (1:4) The elder's greatest joy is not a growing church or his own name being honored. It is his spiritual children "walking in the truth." "Children" may mean those the elder led to faith, or more broadly the believers in his care. This sentence shows a pastor's heart: his joy is in other people's lives.

**2. "Fellow workers for the truth."** "Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth." (1:8) Not every believer is called to travel and preach, but every believer can be a "fellow worker" with those who do. Welcoming them into our homes, supporting them with travel money and daily needs, and praying for them are all real gospel work. "For the truth" can also be translated "with the truth": the truth itself is at work, and we are its fellow workers. In John's letters the truth is not an abstract idea but the gospel of Christ, who is himself the truth (John 14:6), to whom the Spirit bears witness, "because the Spirit is the truth" (1 John 5:6).

**3. "Whoever does good is from God."** "Beloved, do not imitate evil but imitate good. Whoever does good is from God; whoever does evil has not seen God." (1:11) This is 1 John applied to a concrete case. Gaius has two examples before him, Diotrephes and Demetrius. "Has not seen God" is very close to 1 John 3:6. John does not mean that doing good makes a person belong to God. He means that whether a person belongs to God shows in what he does.

## Structure of the Book

Third John can be divided this way:
- **Greeting and joy** (vv. 1–4): the elder writes to the beloved Gaius; he prays that all may go well with him and that he may be in good health, as it goes well with his soul; he has no greater joy than to hear that his children are walking in the truth.
- **Gaius's hospitality commended** (vv. 5–8): what Gaius does for the brothers, strangers as they are, is faithful; send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God; they have gone out for the sake of the name; we ought to support such people.
- **Diotrephes rebuked** (vv. 9–10): he likes to put himself first and does not acknowledge the elder; he talks wicked nonsense against him; he stops those who want to welcome the brothers and puts them out of the church.
- **Imitate good; Demetrius commended** (vv. 11–12): do not imitate evil but imitate good; Demetrius has a good testimony from everyone and from the truth itself.
- **Closing** (vv. 13–15): the hope of talking face to face; peace be to you; greet the friends, every one of them.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Verse 2 is no basis for a prosperity gospel.** "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul." (1:2) Some who preach a "health and wealth gospel" often use this verse to say that God wills every believer to be rich and healthy. But the verse cannot be used this way:
- **It is a standard letter greeting:** Greek letters of the time often began with a wish for the reader's good health. The elder uses this common form, but he adds "as it goes well with your soul," and puts the weight on the health of the soul.
- **It is a wish, not a promise:** The word was once tied to having a good journey, but by New Testament times it simply meant "to prosper, to go well" (Rom 1:10, Paul's hope to "succeed" in coming). It can include material things; in 1 Cor 16:2 it is used of income ("as he may prosper"). But here it is a wish, not a promise. The elder hopes all goes well for Gaius; he does not say God guarantees wealth to every believer.
- **The soul's health is the measure:** "As it goes well with your soul" makes Gaius's soul the measure. How was his soul doing well? He was "walking in the truth" (1:3).
But we should not swing to the other extreme and think God does not care about our bodies. The elder was right to pray for Gaius's body. We too can ask God for health, work, and daily needs for ourselves and others (Matt 6:11).

**2. Read it with 2 John.** Both 2 and 3 John are about hospitality. Second John says not to receive those who deny Christ; 3 John says to receive those who go out for the sake of the name. Diotrephes's fault is the opposite of what 2 John warns against: he refuses the faithful brothers. Guarding the truth, then, does not mean shutting the door on everyone who comes. Like Gaius, the church must see clearly what a visitor teaches, and then faithfully receive and support those who bring the truth.

**3. Look at the three portraits.** The letter is like three portraits: Gaius, Diotrephes, and Demetrius. As we read, we can ask: Which of them am I most like in my church? Whom do I welcome and help? Where do I "like to put myself first"? Reading this way is not about seeing only others' faults; it lets God's word shine on our own hearts.

**4. Notice "the name."** The brothers "have gone out for the sake of the name" (1:7). In Acts the apostles rejoiced "that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (Acts 5:41). All the welcoming, supporting, and preaching are for that one person's name. This letter never writes the name "Jesus," yet the whole letter is written for his name.

## Meditation

**Meditation:** "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul." (1:2) Someone once quoted this verse to a sister who had been ill for years and told her, "God wants you healthy. You are still ill because your faith is not strong enough." The one who said it may have been me. I may not put it so bluntly, but I too have ready explanations. When trouble comes to a family, I wonder where they offended God; when someone's business collapses, I remember that he never gave much. Thinking this way seems to explain the world on God's behalf, and it makes me feel safer.

Job's three friends had explanations too. Eliphaz said, "Remember: who that was innocent ever perished? Or where were the upright cut off?" (Job 4:7) They said a great deal about God, and every word sounded spiritual, but Job called them "miserable comforters" (Job 16:2). In the end the LORD said to Eliphaz, "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7). Explaining someone else's suffering with a tidy theory turns out to be no small fault before God.

But the story does not end with God's anger. God told them to offer a burnt offering, and said, "My servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept his prayer not to deal with you according to your folly" (Job 42:8). The sufferer they had wronged became the one who prayed for them. So it is with the suffering Servant in Isaiah: "he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors" (Isa 53:12). This reminds me of another righteous sufferer. Isaiah wrote, "Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows; yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted" (Isa 53:4). Those who stood near the cross looked at him the same way: he must have sinned, for God to punish him like this. In fact the punishment he bore was not for his own sins but for ours: "he was wounded for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities," and the LORD laid on him the iniquity of us all (Isa 53:5–6). Matthew says Jesus' healings fulfilled these words (Matt 8:17), and his cross and resurrection guarantee that our bodies will be fully healed. But that healing belongs to the resurrection, not necessarily to this life.

So I can lay down my ready explanations. I can admit that I have often not spoken of God "what is right," and that I have wounded weak people with my words. The Lord whom people "esteemed stricken" was punished for sins like mine too. Next time I sit beside someone who is ill, I need not hurry to explain. I can simply stay with them and pray, as the elder did, for their body and their soul together. And if I am the one who has been ill a long time and has been talked about this way, I can rest in this: God honors those who, like Job, pour out their hearts to him, and he never faulted Job for bringing his complaint to him. Job's humbling (Job 42:6) came from seeing God, not from conceding his friends were right. In the end God did not give Job an explanation; he gave him himself: "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5). And the Lord who was misjudged and smitten understands my place better than anyone.
