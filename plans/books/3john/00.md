## 这卷书在讲什么

约翰三书是新约里最短的书卷之一，按希腊文的字数算，比约翰二书还要短一些。它和约翰二书一样，是「作长老的」写的（1:1），却不是写给一间教会，而是写给一个人：「亲爱的该犹」。这是一封很私人的信，读起来像是一位年老的牧者写给一位忠心的弟兄的便条。

信里出现了三个人：
- **该犹：** 他「按真理而行」（1:3），忠心地接待那些出外传道的弟兄，虽然他们原本是陌生人（1:5）。长老称赞他，又请他继续帮助他们往前行。
- **丢特腓：** 他「在教会中好为首」（1:9），不接待长老所差来的人，用恶言妄论长老，还禁止别人接待弟兄，甚至把接待弟兄的人赶出教会（1:10）。
- **低米丢：** 他「有众人给他作见证，又有真理给他作见证」（1:12）。他可能就是带这封信去的人，长老为他写了一句推荐的话。

约翰二书说的是：不要接待那些否认基督的人（约二1:10）。约翰三书说的却是另一面：要接待那些为主的名出外的人。两封信放在一起，让我们看见真理和接待的关系：拒绝谁、接待谁，都要按真理来定。约翰说，我们接待忠心的传道人，就是「与他们一同为真理做工」（1:8）。

这封信还有一个特别的地方：全信没有直接提到「耶稣」或「基督」的名字。可是这名字在信的中心。和合本说那些弟兄是「为主的名出外」（1:7），原文只说「为那名」。对当时的信徒来说，不用解释，大家都知道「那名」是谁的名：就是那「超乎万名之上的名」（腓2:9）。

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 作者和时间。** 作者和约翰二书一样，自称「作长老的」（1:1）。约翰一书导读和约翰二书导读都讲过作者的问题；本研读采用传统的看法：作者是年老的使徒约翰。三封信大概都写于主后85-95年，长老当时可能住在以弗所。早期教会接受这封信，也和约翰二书一样比较慢：优西比乌把这两封信都列在有人质疑的书卷中（《教会史》第三卷第二十五章）。原因之一可能是它们太短，又是私人的信，在教会中不常被宣读引用。

**2. 该犹是谁？** 该犹是罗马帝国中最常见的名字之一，新约里就有好几位该犹：哥林多的该犹，保罗曾为他施洗，他也接待保罗和全教会（罗16:23；林前1:14）；马其顿人该犹（徒19:29）；特庇人该犹（徒20:4）。这些人和约翰三书的该犹不一定有关系。四世纪的《使徒宪典》说，约翰立了一位该犹作别迦摩的监督，但这个记载很晚，不能确定。我们只知道，他是长老所爱的，很可能是长老带领信主的（「我的儿女们」，1:4），也是一位乐意接待人的信徒。

**3. 巡回传道的人。** 导读约翰二书时讲过，当时的传道人从一个城走到另一个城，住在信徒家中，靠信徒供给。这封信里说的弟兄，是长老所差、或至少是长老所认识和推荐的人。他们「为主的名出外，对于外邦人一无所取」（1:7），就是不向不信主的外邦人收取供给，免得人以为他们传福音是为了钱。当时有许多四处游走的哲学家和宗教人士，靠向听众讨钱为生；这些传道人却不这样做。所以信徒供给他们，就格外重要。

**4. 荐信的习惯。** 早期教会常用书信来推荐信徒，让别处的教会接待他们。保罗就这样推荐过非比：「请你们为主接待她，合乎圣徒的体统。」（罗16:2）保罗也提到别人用的「荐信」（林后3:1）。约翰三书很可能就是这样一封信，为低米丢写的（1:12）。

**5. 「我曾略略地写信给教会」。** 长老说，他曾写信给该犹所在的教会，但丢特腓不接待他们（1:9）。这封信是哪一封？有几种看法：
- **约翰二书：** 有人认为就是约翰二书。不过约翰二书的内容是警告假教师，和接待长老所差的弟兄关系不大。
- **一封已经失传的信：** 多数学者认为，这是一封我们今天看不到的信，可能是推荐那些弟兄的荐信，被丢特腓扣下来了。

**6. 丢特腓为什么这样做？** 信里只说他「好为首」（1:9），没有说他传假道理。学者有几种推测：
- **个人的野心：** 这是最直接的读法，也是多数释经者（如斯托得）的看法。丢特腓想在教会中掌权，不愿意别人的权柄在他之上。
- **教会治理的转变：** 德国学者哈纳克认为，丢特腓代表了当时正在兴起的、由一位监督治理地方教会的制度，和长老所代表的、到处巡视的使徒权柄起了冲突。
- **教义的冲突：** 另一位德国学者凯泽曼甚至提出，被看作异端的其实是长老，丢特腓是正统教会的领袖。不过这个看法很少人接受，因为信里看不出丢特腓在教义上和长老有什么分歧，而长老所说的「真理」正是约翰一书和二书所传的。
无论如何，约翰所责备的，是丢特腓的心：他好为首。这是教会在每个时代都会遇到的问题。

## 钥匙经文：1:4；1:8；1:11

**1. 「我的喜乐就没有比这个大的」。** 「我听见我的儿女们按真理而行，我的喜乐就没有比这个大的。」（1:4）长老最大的喜乐，不是教会人数增加，也不是自己的名声被人敬重，而是他属灵的儿女「按真理而行」。这里的「儿女」，可能指长老带领信主的人，也可能泛指他所牧养的信徒。这句话让我们看见一位牧者的心：他的喜乐在别人的生命里。

**2. 「与他们一同为真理做工」。** 「所以我们应该接待这样的人，叫我们与他们一同为真理做工。」（1:8）不是每个信徒都要出外传道，但每个信徒都可以和传道的人「一同做工」。在家里接待他们，在路费和生活上供给他们，为他们祷告，都是真实的福音工作。这里的「为真理」，也可以译作与真理一同：真理自己在做工，我们是和真理同工的人。

**3. 「行善的属乎神」。** 「亲爱的兄弟啊，不要效法恶，只要效法善。行善的属乎神；行恶的未曾见过神。」（1:11）这是约翰一书的话，用在一件很具体的事上：该犹面前有两个榜样，一个是丢特腓，一个是低米丢。「未曾见过神」和约翰一书3:6的话很像。这不是说行善可以使人属神，而是说，一个人属不属神，会在他的行为上显出来。

## 全书结构

约翰三书可以这样分：
- **问安和喜乐**（1-4节）：长老写信给亲爱的该犹；愿他凡事兴盛、身体健壮，正如他的灵魂兴盛；听见儿女们按真理而行，喜乐没有比这个大的。
- **称赞该犹的接待**（5-8节）：该犹向作客旅的弟兄所行的是忠心的；要帮助他们往前行，配得过神；他们为主的名出外；我们应该接待这样的人。
- **责备丢特腓**（9-10节）：他好为首，不接待长老所差的人；用恶言妄论长老；禁止人接待弟兄，又把他们赶出教会。
- **效法善，推荐低米丢**（11-12节）：不要效法恶，只要效法善；低米丢有众人和真理给他作见证。
- **结语**（13-15节）：盼望当面谈论；愿你平安；按着姓名问众位朋友安。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 第2节不是成功神学的根据。** 「亲爱的兄弟啊，我愿你凡事兴盛，身体健壮，正如你的灵魂兴盛一样。」（1:2）有些传健康财富福音的人，常用这一节说：神的旨意是要每个信徒都发财、都健康。不过这节经文不能这样用：
- **这是书信的问安格式：** 当时的希腊文书信，开头常常有一句祝福收信人身体健康的话。长老用了这个常见的格式，却加上「正如你的灵魂兴盛一样」，把重点放在灵魂的兴盛上。
- **「兴盛」不是发财：** 这个词原文的意思是一路顺利，和合本在罗马书1:10译作「得平坦的道路」。它不是应许财富，而是愿望该犹一切顺利。
- **灵魂的兴盛是标准：** 长老说「正如你的灵魂兴盛一样」，是以该犹灵魂的兴盛为标准。该犹的灵魂怎样兴盛呢？就是他「按真理而行」（1:3）。
不过，我们也不要走到另一个极端，以为神不在乎我们的身体。长老为该犹的身体祷告，是对的。我们也可以为自己和别人的健康、工作、生活向神祈求（太6:11）。

**2. 和约翰二书一起读。** 约翰二书和三书都讲到「接待」：约翰二书说不要接待否认基督的人，约翰三书说要接待为主名出外的人。丢特腓的错，正好和约翰二书所警告的相反：他不接待那些忠心的弟兄。可见守护真理，不是对所有来的人都关门。教会要像该犹一样，看清楚来的人传的是什么，然后忠心地接待、供给那些传真理的人。

**3. 看三个人的画像。** 这封信像是三幅画：该犹、丢特腓、低米丢。读的时候，可以问自己：我在教会里，比较像哪一个？我所接待、帮助的，是谁？我在什么事上「好为首」？这样读，不是要我们只看别人的错，而是让神的话照亮自己的心。

**4. 留意「那名」。** 那些弟兄是「为主的名出外」（1:7）。在使徒行传里，使徒们「因被算是配为这名受辱」而欢喜（徒5:41）。一切的接待、供给和传道，都是为了那一位的名。这封信虽然没有写出「耶稣」这个名字，整封信却是为他的名而写的。

## 默想

**默想：** 「亲爱的兄弟啊，我愿你凡事兴盛，身体健壮，正如你的灵魂兴盛一样。」（1:2）有人常常拿这一节对生病的信徒说：「神要你健康，你还在病中，是因为你的信心不够。」有一位姊妹病了很多年，祷告了很多次，病还是没有好。有人这样对她说了以后，她连聚会都不太想去了：她不但身体痛，心里也觉得被神责备了。

可是这一节的重点，正好落在另一个地方：「正如你的灵魂兴盛一样。」长老为该犹的身体祷告，这是对的；但他先看见的，是该犹的灵魂兴盛，是他「按真理而行」（1:3）。新约里也有忠心的信徒病了，没有立刻得医治：保罗把病了的特罗非摩留在米利都（提后4:20），以巴弗提「病了，几乎要死」（腓2:27）。门徒看见生来瞎眼的人，问是谁犯了罪，耶稣说：「也不是这人犯了罪，也不是他父母犯了罪，是要在他身上显出神的作为来。」（约9:3）病不一定是因为信心不够，也不一定是神的惩罚。我可以诚实地为医治祈求，也可以诚实地向神诉说我的痛苦。

我的灵魂兴盛的根基，也不在于我的信心有多大。那位主「代替我们的软弱，担当我们的疾病」（太8:17）。他在十字架上担当了我们的罪，也担当了罪所带来的一切咒诅。今天我的身体还会软弱、会痛，我还在「心里叹息，等候……我们的身体得赎」（罗8:23）。但那一天一定会来，因为他已经从死里复活，身体也复活了。

如果我曾经对病中的人说过「你的信心不够」，我可以向主认罪，也向那个人道歉，然后去探望他，为他祷告，陪他一段路。如果我自己就是那个病了很久的人，我可以把第2节读成长老对我的祷告：他愿我身体健壮，也看见我的灵魂在主里兴盛。我的信心也许很软弱，但我所信的那一位并不软弱。他担当了我的疾病，他复活的身体，就是我将来得医治的凭据。

## What This Book Is About

Third John is one of the shortest books in the New Testament; counted by Greek words, it is a little shorter even than 2 John. Like 2 John, it is from "the elder" (1:1), but it is written not to a church but to one man: "the beloved Gaius." It is a very personal letter, and it reads like a note from an aged pastor to a faithful brother.

Three men appear in the letter:
- **Gaius:** He is "walking in the truth" (1:3). He faithfully receives the brothers who travel out to preach, though they were strangers to him (1:5). The elder commends him and asks him to keep helping them on their way.
- **Diotrephes:** He "likes to put himself first" (1:9). He does not acknowledge the elder's authority or welcome those the elder sends. He talks wicked nonsense against the elder, stops others from welcoming the brothers, and even puts those who do out of the church (1:10).
- **Demetrius:** He "has received a good testimony from everyone, and from the truth itself" (1:12). He may be the one carrying the letter, and the elder adds a word of commendation for him.

Second John says: do not receive those who deny Christ (2 John 1:10). Third John shows the other side: receive those who have gone out for the sake of the name. Taken together, the two letters show how truth and hospitality belong together. Whom we refuse and whom we receive must both be decided by the truth. John says that when we support faithful preachers, we become "fellow workers for the truth" (1:8).

This letter has one more unusual feature: it never names "Jesus" or "Christ." Yet his name stands at the center of the letter. The brothers "have gone out for the sake of the name" (1:7). The Greek says only "the name." The first readers needed no explanation: everyone knew whose name it was, "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9).

## Author and Setting

**1. Author and date.** As in 2 John, the writer calls himself "the elder" (1:1). The introductions to 1 and 2 John have discussed the question of authorship; this study takes the traditional view that the author is the aged apostle John. All three letters were probably written around AD 85–95, when the elder may have been living in Ephesus. As with 2 John, the early church was slow to accept this letter. Eusebius lists both among the books some disputed (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.25). One reason may be that they were short and personal, and so were not often read aloud or quoted in the churches.

**2. Who was Gaius?** Gaius was one of the most common names in the Roman Empire, and the New Testament has several men named Gaius: Gaius of Corinth, whom Paul baptized and who hosted Paul and the whole church (Rom 16:23; 1 Cor 1:14); Gaius the Macedonian (Acts 19:29); and Gaius of Derbe (Acts 20:4). None of them need be the Gaius of 3 John. The fourth-century *Apostolic Constitutions* says John appointed a Gaius as bishop of Pergamum, but this is a late report and cannot be confirmed. We know only that the elder loved him, that the elder probably led him to faith ("my children," 1:4), and that he was glad to welcome people.

**3. Traveling preachers.** As the introduction to 2 John explained, preachers then traveled from city to city, staying in believers' homes and relying on them for support. The brothers in this letter were sent by the elder, or were at least known to him and commended by him. They "have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles" (1:7): they took no support from pagans, so that no one would think they preached the gospel for money. Many wandering philosophers and religious figures in those days lived by asking their hearers for money; these preachers did not. That made believers' support for them all the more important.

**4. Letters of commendation.** The early church often used letters to commend believers, so that churches elsewhere would receive them. Paul commended Phoebe this way: "that you may welcome her in the Lord in a way worthy of the saints" (Rom 16:2). Paul also mentions "letters of recommendation" others used (2 Cor 3:1). Third John is very likely such a letter, written for Demetrius (1:12).

**5. "I have written something to the church."** The elder says he wrote to Gaius's church, but Diotrephes would not acknowledge them (1:9). Which letter was it? There are two views:
- **2 John:** Some think it was 2 John. But 2 John warns against false teachers, which has little to do with receiving the brothers the elder sent.
- **A lost letter:** Most scholars think it was a letter we no longer have, perhaps a letter commending those brothers, which Diotrephes suppressed.

**6. Why did Diotrephes act this way?** The letter says only that he "likes to put himself first" (1:9). It does not say he taught false doctrine. Scholars have offered several suggestions:
- **Personal ambition:** This is the most straightforward reading and the view of most interpreters (Stott, for example). Diotrephes wanted to hold power in the church and would not have anyone's authority over his.
- **A change in church government:** The German scholar Adolf von Harnack thought Diotrephes represented the rising pattern of a single bishop ruling a local church, which clashed with the traveling, apostolic authority the elder represented.
- **A doctrinal conflict:** Another German scholar, Ernst Käsemann, went so far as to suggest that the elder was the one regarded as a heretic, and that Diotrephes was the leader of the orthodox church. Few have accepted this, because the letter shows no doctrinal disagreement between Diotrephes and the elder, and the elder's "truth" is the very truth taught in 1 and 2 John.
Whatever the case, what John rebukes is Diotrephes's heart: he loves to be first. The church meets that problem in every age.

## Key Verses: 1:4; 1:8; 1:11

**1. "I have no greater joy."** "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth." (1:4) The elder's greatest joy is not a growing church or his own name being honored. It is his spiritual children "walking in the truth." "Children" may mean those the elder led to faith, or more broadly the believers in his care. This sentence shows a pastor's heart: his joy is in other people's lives.

**2. "Fellow workers for the truth."** "Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth." (1:8) Not every believer is called to travel and preach, but every believer can be a "fellow worker" with those who do. Welcoming them into our homes, supporting them with travel money and daily needs, and praying for them are all real gospel work. "For the truth" can also be translated "with the truth": the truth itself is at work, and we are its fellow workers.

**3. "Whoever does good is from God."** "Beloved, do not imitate evil but imitate good. Whoever does good is from God; whoever does evil has not seen God." (1:11) This is 1 John applied to a concrete case. Gaius has two examples before him, Diotrephes and Demetrius. "Has not seen God" is very close to 1 John 3:6. John does not mean that doing good makes a person belong to God. He means that whether a person belongs to God shows in what he does.

## Structure of the Book

Third John can be divided this way:
- **Greeting and joy** (vv. 1–4): the elder writes to the beloved Gaius; he prays that all may go well with him and that he may be in good health, as it goes well with his soul; he has no greater joy than to hear that his children are walking in the truth.
- **Gaius's hospitality commended** (vv. 5–8): what Gaius does for the brothers, strangers as they are, is faithful; send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God; they have gone out for the sake of the name; we ought to support such people.
- **Diotrephes rebuked** (vv. 9–10): he likes to put himself first and does not acknowledge the elder; he talks wicked nonsense against him; he stops those who want to welcome the brothers and puts them out of the church.
- **Imitate good; Demetrius commended** (vv. 11–12): do not imitate evil but imitate good; Demetrius has a good testimony from everyone and from the truth itself.
- **Closing** (vv. 13–15): the hope of talking face to face; peace be to you; greet the friends, every one of them.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Verse 2 is no basis for a prosperity gospel.** "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul." (1:2) Some who preach a "health and wealth gospel" often use this verse to say that God wills every believer to be rich and healthy. But the verse cannot be used this way:
- **It is a standard letter greeting:** Greek letters of the time often began with a wish for the reader's good health. The elder uses this common form, but he adds "as it goes well with your soul," and puts the weight on the health of the soul.
- **"Go well" does not mean getting rich:** The word means to have a good journey, to be helped along the way. In Romans 1:10 it is used of Paul hoping to "succeed" in coming to Rome. It is not a promise of wealth but a wish that all may go well for Gaius.
- **The soul's health is the measure:** "As it goes well with your soul" makes Gaius's soul the measure. How was his soul doing well? He was "walking in the truth" (1:3).
But we should not swing to the other extreme and think God does not care about our bodies. The elder was right to pray for Gaius's body. We too can ask God for health, work, and daily needs for ourselves and others (Matt 6:11).

**2. Read it with 2 John.** Both 2 and 3 John are about hospitality. Second John says not to receive those who deny Christ; 3 John says to receive those who go out for the sake of the name. Diotrephes's fault is the opposite of what 2 John warns against: he refuses the faithful brothers. Guarding the truth, then, does not mean shutting the door on everyone who comes. Like Gaius, the church must see clearly what a visitor teaches, and then faithfully receive and support those who bring the truth.

**3. Look at the three portraits.** The letter is like three portraits: Gaius, Diotrephes, and Demetrius. As we read, we can ask: Which of them am I most like in my church? Whom do I welcome and help? Where do I "like to put myself first"? Reading this way is not about seeing only others' faults; it lets God's word shine on our own hearts.

**4. Notice "the name."** The brothers "have gone out for the sake of the name" (1:7). In Acts the apostles rejoiced "that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name" (Acts 5:41). All the welcoming, supporting, and preaching are for that one person's name. This letter never writes the name "Jesus," yet the whole letter is written for his name.

## Meditation

**Meditation:** "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul." (1:2) Some people quote this verse to a sick believer and say, "God wants you healthy. You are still ill because your faith is not strong enough." One sister has been ill for many years. She has prayed many times, and she is still not well. After someone said this to her, she hardly wanted to go to church anymore. Her body was in pain, and now her heart felt condemned by God as well.

But the weight of this verse falls somewhere else: "as it goes well with your soul." The elder was right to pray for Gaius's body, but what he saw first was that Gaius's soul was doing well, that he was "walking in the truth" (1:3). In the New Testament, faithful believers also fell ill and were not healed at once. Paul left Trophimus ill at Miletus (2 Tim 4:20), and Epaphroditus "was ill, near to death" (Phil 2:27). When the disciples saw a man born blind and asked who had sinned, Jesus answered, "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3). Illness is not necessarily a sign of weak faith, nor necessarily God's punishment. I can honestly pray for healing, and I can honestly tell God about my pain.

Nor does the health of my soul rest on how great my faith is. The Lord "took our illnesses and bore our diseases" (Matt 8:17). On the cross he bore our sins, and with them all the curse that sin brought. Today my body still grows weak and still hurts; I still "groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for... the redemption of our bodies" (Rom 8:23). But that day will surely come, because he has risen from the dead, body and all.

If I have ever told a sick person, "Your faith is not strong enough," I can confess it to the Lord and apologize to that person, and then visit them, pray for them, and walk with them for a while. If I am the one who has been ill for a long time, I can read verse 2 as the elder's prayer for me: he wishes me good health, and he sees my soul doing well in the Lord. My faith may be weak, but the one I trust is not weak. He bore my diseases, and his risen body is the pledge that I will be healed.
