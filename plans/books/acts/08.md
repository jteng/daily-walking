## 本章脉络

司提反殉道那一天，「耶路撒冷的教会大遭逼迫」（8:1）。逼迫没有消灭福音，反而把福音推出了耶路撒冷，正如复活的主所说的：「在耶路撒冷、犹太全地，和撒马利亚，直到地极，作我的见证。」（1:8）第八章就是这个应许的第二步。

本章有三段：**逼迫与分散**（1-4节）；**腓利在撒马利亚**（5-25节），包括行邪术的西门，以及彼得、约翰来到，撒马利亚人受了圣灵；**腓利与衣索匹亚太监**（26-40节），一位从「地极」来的人，在旷野的路上听见了福音。

## 深读 8:1-4 逼迫成了差遣

**1. 除了使徒以外。** 「从这日起，耶路撒冷的教会大遭逼迫，除了使徒以外，门徒都分散在犹太和撒马利亚各处。」（8:1）留在耶路撒冷的是使徒，出去的却是普通的门徒。福音第一次大规模地传出去，不是靠领袖的策划，而是靠一群被赶出家门的平信徒。

**2. 虔诚人的哀哭。** 「有虔诚的人把司提反埋葬了，为他捶胸大哭。」（8:2）在逼迫中，教会没有假装坚强。为殉道者哀哭，并不是信心软弱；神的百姓可以「似乎忧愁，却是常常快乐的」（林后6:10）。

**3. 扫罗残害教会。** 「扫罗却残害教会，进各人的家，拉着男女下在监里。」（8:3）「残害」这个字在希腊文的旧约里用来形容林中的野猪糟踏葡萄园（诗80:13）。这是保罗后来常常提起的过去：「我从前逼迫神的教会。」（林前15:9）

**4. 分散的人往各处去传道。** 「那些分散的人往各处去传道。」（8:4）「分散」（*diaspeirō*）这个字的字根是「撒种」（*speirō*），「散居」（diaspora）一词就是从它来的。路加没有特别强调这个意象，但它很贴切：仇敌以为是把他们驱散，神却是在撒种。他们失去了家园，却没有失去要传的信息。

> 要点：神让逼迫成为差遣。被赶出耶路撒冷的门徒，没有只顾着逃命，而是走到哪里，就把福音带到哪里。

## 深读 8:5-25 撒玛利亚：福音跨越百年的仇恨

**1. 腓利下撒玛利亚城去。** 「腓利下撒马利亚城去，宣讲基督。」（8:5）这个腓利不是使徒腓利，而是第六章所选的七人之一（6:5）。犹太人和撒玛利亚人之间有好几百年的仇恨（约4:9）。腓利很可能是说希腊话的犹太信徒，属于那些寡妇曾「被忽略」的一群人，现在却成了第一个跨过这道墙的人。

**2. 大有欢喜。** 「在那城里，就大有欢喜。」（8:8）福音带来的，不只是神迹，更是喜乐。他们「信了腓利所传神国的福音和耶稣基督的名，连男带女就受了洗」（8:12）。

**3. 圣灵为什么延迟？** 撒玛利亚人受了洗，「圣灵还没有降在他们一个人身上」，直到彼得、约翰来为他们祷告、按手（8:15-17）。这是使徒行传中一个特别的情况。较好的理解是：这是救恩历史中一个独特的转折点，不是每个信徒都要经历的模式。撒玛利亚人和犹太人互相敌视几百年；神让耶路撒冷的使徒亲眼看见、亲手参与，确认撒玛利亚的信徒和耶路撒冷的信徒同属一个教会，领受同一位圣灵。路加一般的模式，是把领受圣灵与悔改、信靠基督连在一起（2:38；11:17；15:8-9）；保罗也说「人若没有基督的灵，就不是属基督的」（罗8:9）。有人把这段看作「第二次祝福」的模式，也有人认为撒玛利亚人的信心有缺陷；但路加说他们已经「领受了神的道」（8:14），而这种情形也没有被当作常规重复出现。

**4. 西门想买的东西。** 西门「向来在那城里行邪术，妄自尊大」，众人说他是「那称为神的大能者」（8:9-10）。他也「信了」，受了洗，却看见使徒按手便有圣灵赐下，「就拿钱给使徒，说：『把这权柄也给我。』」（8:18-19）彼得说：「你的银子和你一同灭亡吧！因你想神的恩赐是可以用钱买的。」（8:20）后来教会把买卖圣职的罪称为 simony，就是从「西门」这个名字来的。

**5. 「你的心不正。」** 「你在这道上无分无关；因为在神面前，你的心不正。」（8:21）西门的问题不只是用钱，而是他想把神的恩赐变成自己的能力，继续作那位「大能者」。彼得的话很严厉，却仍给他悔改的门：「你当懊悔你这罪恶，祈求主，或者你心里的意念可得赦免。」（8:22）

**6. 他真的信了吗？** 路加说西门「信了」（8:13），却也记录彼得说他「被罪恶捆绑」（8:23）。新约提醒我们，有一种「信」只是被神迹吸引（参约2:23-25），心却没有改变。西门最后怎样，路加没有告诉我们（8:24）。

> 要点：福音跨过了几百年的仇恨，也揭穿了人心里想利用神的意图。恩典是白白的，正因为是白白的，就不能买，也不能拿来抬高自己。

## 深读 8:26-40 旷野路上的太监

**1. 从热闹的城到旷野的路。** 「有主的一个使者对腓利说：『起来！向南走，往那从耶路撒冷下迦萨的路上去。』那路是旷野。」（8:26）腓利正在撒玛利亚大有果效，神却叫他到旷野去，只为了一个人。

**2. 一个从地极来的人。** 这人是「衣索匹亚人，是个有大权的太监，在衣索匹亚女王甘大基的手下总管银库，他上耶路撒冷礼拜去了」（8:27）。这里的「衣索匹亚」指的是埃及以南的古实地区（今天苏丹一带），在当时的人看来，就是「地的尽头」。他有权、有钱，却千里迢迢去敬拜，回程还在读以赛亚书。

**3. 被拦在门外的人。** 按摩西律法，「被阉割的，不可入耶和华的会」（申23:1）。他去了耶路撒冷，很可能只能站在外院。但以赛亚书曾应许：「太监也不要说：我是枯树。」神要使持守他约的太监「在我殿中，在我墙内，有记念，有名号，比有儿女的更美」（赛56:3-5）。他正在读的书卷，几章之后，就有对他这样的人的应许。

**4. 「没有人指教我，怎能明白呢？」** 腓利问他：「你所念的，你明白吗？」他说：「没有人指教我，怎能明白呢？」（8:30-31）他念的是以赛亚书53章：「他像羊被牵到宰杀之地，又像羊羔在剪毛的人手下无声；他也是这样不开口。」（8:32；赛53:7）他问：「先知说这话是指着谁？」（8:34）

**5. 从这经上起，传讲耶稣。** 「腓利就开口从这经上起，对他传讲耶稣。」（8:35）这是读旧约的钥匙：那位不开口、被夺去生命的受苦者，就是耶稣。他「卑微的时候，人不按公义审判他」（8:33），这个太监也知道被羞辱、被拒绝是什么滋味。这段经文接着说，他从活人之地被剪除，「是因我百姓的罪过」（赛53:8）：他是替我们被剪除的。而这位「谁能述说他的世代」的受苦者，却「必看见后裔」（赛53:10）。藉着他，这个没有后裔的太监，要从神那里得着「永远的名，不能剪除」（赛56:5）。

**6. 「我受洗有什么妨碍呢？」** 「看哪，这里有水，我受洗有什么妨碍呢？」（8:36）律法曾拦阻他，现在却没有任何事拦阻他。（有些较晚的抄本在这里加上第37节，记载太监承认「我信耶稣基督是神的儿子」；最早的抄本没有这一节，所以英文ESV把它放在注脚，本应用所用的和合本经文也在这里空出37节。）

**7. 欢欢喜喜地走路。** 「从水里上来，主的灵把腓利提了去，太监也不再见他了，就欢欢喜喜地走路。」（8:39）他再也没有见过腓利，却没有失去喜乐，因为他得着的不是腓利，而是基督。

> 要点：神为一个人，把传道人从热闹的城里带到旷野的路上。被拦在门外的人，在基督里被接纳了；他所读的经文，原来就是在讲那位为他被羞辱、被剪除的主。

## 默想

**默想：** 司提反的死似乎是一个结束，神却让它成为福音向外传的开始。在我生命中，有没有一件事让我觉得被「赶散」了：搬家、失业、关系破裂、教会的分裂？神是否也可能在那里撒种？分散的门徒没有讲台，也没有头衔，只是走到哪里，就把耶稣带到哪里。西门想买神的能力，把神当作可以使用的力量，而不是要敬拜的主。我祷告的时候，是在寻求神自己，还是只想得着他手里的东西？衣索匹亚太监带着一个被拦在门外的身份去敬拜，最后却发现，经文所讲的那位受苦者，正是为他被羞辱的主。我心里有没有某个觉得「我不配进去」的地方？耶稣为我被拒绝，好使我被接纳；我可以像那位太监一样，「欢欢喜喜地走路」。

## The Shape of the Chapter

On the day Stephen died, "there arose... a great persecution against the church in Jerusalem" (8:1). Persecution didn't destroy the gospel; it pushed it out of Jerusalem, just as the risen Lord had said: "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (1:8). Chapter 8 is the second step of that promise.

The chapter has three parts: **persecution and scattering** (vv. 1–4); **Philip in Samaria** (vv. 5–25), including Simon the magician, and Peter and John coming so that the Samaritans receive the Spirit; and **Philip and the Ethiopian eunuch** (vv. 26–40), a man from "the end of the earth" who hears the gospel on a desert road.

## Going Deeper: 8:1–4 Persecution Becomes Sending

**1. Except the apostles.** "And there arose on that day a great persecution against the church in Jerusalem, and they were all scattered throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except the apostles" (8:1). The apostles stayed in Jerusalem; it was ordinary disciples who went out. The gospel's first large-scale spread came not through leaders' planning but through ordinary believers driven from their homes.

**2. Devout men lamented.** "Devout men buried Stephen and made great lamentation over him" (8:2). Under persecution the church didn't pretend to be strong. Grieving a martyr isn't weak faith; God's people can be "sorrowful, yet always rejoicing" (2 Cor 6:10).

**3. Saul ravaged the church.** "But Saul was ravaging the church, and entering house after house, he dragged off men and women and committed them to prison" (8:3). In the Greek Old Testament the word for "ravage" describes a wild boar ravaging a vineyard (Ps 80:13). It is the past Paul would often recall: "I persecuted the church of God" (1 Cor 15:9).

**4. The scattered went about preaching.** "Now those who were scattered went about preaching the word" (8:4). "Scattered" (*diaspeirō*) is built on the verb for sowing seed (*speirō*); it is the word behind "diaspora." Luke doesn't press the image, but it fits: the enemy thought he was scattering them; God was sowing them. They had lost their homes, but not their message.

> The point: God turned persecution into sending. The disciples driven out of Jerusalem didn't only run for their lives; wherever they went, they took the gospel with them.

## Going Deeper: 8:5–25 Samaria: The Gospel Crosses Centuries of Hostility

**1. Philip went down to Samaria.** "Philip went down to the city of Samaria and proclaimed to them the Christ" (8:5). This isn't the apostle Philip but one of the Seven chosen in chapter 6 (6:5). Jews and Samaritans had been hostile to one another for centuries (John 4:9). Philip, probably a Greek-speaking Jewish believer, from the group whose widows had been "overlooked," became the first to cross this wall.

**2. Much joy.** "So there was much joy in that city" (8:8). The gospel brought not only miracles but joy. They "believed Philip as he preached good news about the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ," and "were baptized, both men and women" (8:12).

**3. Why was the Spirit delayed?** The Samaritans were baptized, but the Spirit "had not yet fallen on any of them" until Peter and John came, prayed and laid hands on them (8:15–17). This is an unusual case in Acts. The better reading is that it was a unique turning point in salvation history, not a pattern every believer must follow. Samaritans and Jews had been enemies for centuries; God had the Jerusalem apostles see it with their own eyes and take part with their own hands, confirming that the Samaritan believers belonged to the same church as the believers in Jerusalem and had received the same Spirit. Luke's normal pattern ties the gift of the Spirit to repentance and faith in Christ (2:38; 11:17; 15:8–9), and Paul says that anyone who does not have the Spirit of Christ does not belong to him (Rom 8:9). Some read this passage as a pattern of a later "second blessing," and others as a sign that the Samaritans' faith was defective; but Luke says they had "received the word of God" (8:14), and nothing like this is repeated as a norm.

**4. What Simon wanted to buy.** Simon "had previously practiced magic in the city... saying that he himself was somebody great," and people called him "the power of God that is called Great" (8:9–10). He too "believed" and was baptized, but when he saw the Spirit given through the apostles' hands, "he offered them money, saying, 'Give me this power also'" (8:18–19). Peter said, "May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money!" (8:20). The later church called the sin of buying church office "simony," after Simon's name.

**5. "Your heart is not right."** "You have neither part nor lot in this matter, for your heart is not right before God" (8:21). Simon's problem wasn't only money; he wanted to turn God's gift into his own power and stay "somebody great." Peter's words are severe, yet they still leave the door to repentance open: "Repent, therefore, of this wickedness of yours, and pray to the Lord that, if possible, the intent of your heart may be forgiven you" (8:22).

**6. Did he truly believe?** Luke says Simon "believed" (8:13), yet records Peter saying he is "in the bond of iniquity" (8:23). The New Testament warns that there is a kind of "belief" that is only drawn by signs (cf. John 2:23–25) while the heart is unchanged. Luke doesn't tell us how Simon ended (8:24).

> The point: The gospel crossed centuries of hostility, and it also exposed the human desire to use God. Grace is free, and because it is free, it can't be bought, or used to make ourselves great.

## Going Deeper: 8:26–40 The Eunuch on the Desert Road

**1. From a busy city to a desert road.** "Now an angel of the Lord said to Philip, 'Rise and go toward the south to the road that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza.' This is a desert place" (8:26). Philip's ministry in Samaria was flourishing, yet God sent him into the desert for one man.

**2. A man from the end of the earth.** He was "an Ethiopian, a eunuch, a court official of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, who was in charge of all her treasure. He had come to Jerusalem to worship" (8:27). "Ethiopia" here means the ancient region of Cush south of Egypt (roughly today's Sudan), which people then thought of as the ends of the earth. He had power and wealth, yet traveled far to worship, and was reading Isaiah on the way home.

**3. A man kept outside.** Under the law of Moses, one who had been castrated "shall not enter the assembly of the LORD" (Deut 23:1). In Jerusalem he could likely go no further than the outer court. But Isaiah had promised: "Let not the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree.'" To eunuchs who hold fast God's covenant he would give "in my house and within my walls a monument and a name better than sons and daughters" (Isa 56:3–5). Only a few chapters on from where he was reading was a promise for someone like him.

**4. "How can I, unless someone guides me?"** Philip asked, "Do you understand what you are reading?" He said, "How can I, unless someone guides me?" (8:30–31). He was reading Isaiah 53: "Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter and like a lamb before its shearer is silent, so he opens not his mouth" (8:32; Isa 53:7). He asked, "About whom... does the prophet say this?" (8:34).

**5. Beginning with this Scripture, he told him about Jesus.** "Then Philip opened his mouth, and beginning with this Scripture he told him the good news about Jesus" (8:35). This is the key to reading the Old Testament: the silent sufferer whose life was taken away is Jesus. "In his humiliation justice was denied him" (8:33), and this eunuch knew what humiliation and rejection felt like. The passage goes on to say he was "stricken for the transgression of my people" (Isa 53:8): he was cut off in our place. Yet this one whose "generation" no one could describe would "see his offspring" (Isa 53:10). Through him, the eunuch with no descendants would receive from God "an everlasting name that shall not be cut off" (Isa 56:5).

**6. "What prevents me from being baptized?"** "See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" (8:36). The law had kept him out; now nothing stood in his way. (Some later manuscripts add a verse 37, in which the eunuch confesses, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God." The earliest manuscripts lack it, so the ESV puts it in a footnote and the Chinese Union text used here leaves verse 37 blank.)

**7. He went on his way rejoicing.** "And when they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord carried Philip away, and the eunuch saw him no more, and went on his way rejoicing" (8:39). He never saw Philip again, but he didn't lose his joy, because what he had gained wasn't Philip but Christ.

> The point: For the sake of one man, God took his preacher from a busy city to a desert road. A man kept outside was welcomed in Christ; the Scripture he was reading turned out to be about the Lord who was humiliated and cut off for him.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Stephen's death seemed like an ending, but God made it the beginning of the gospel's spread. Is there something in my life that has made me feel "scattered": a move, a lost job, a broken relationship, a church split? Might God be sowing there too? The scattered disciples had no pulpit and no title; wherever they went, they simply took Jesus with them. Simon wanted to buy God's power, treating God as a force to be used rather than a Lord to be worshiped. When I pray, am I seeking God himself, or only what is in his hand? The Ethiopian eunuch went to worship as a man kept outside, and found that the sufferer in the Scripture he was reading was the Lord humiliated for him. Is there a place in my heart that says, "I'm not fit to come in"? Jesus was rejected for me so that I could be welcomed; like the eunuch, I can go on my way rejoicing.
