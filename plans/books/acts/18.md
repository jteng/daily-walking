## 本章脉络

第十八章的事主要发生在哥林多。这是一座富裕、繁忙的港口大城，素有道德败坏之名（这名声有一部分来自被罗马毁灭以前的旧哥林多）；哥林多前书也显出当地在性道德上的问题。**亚居拉和百基拉**（1-4节）：保罗与这对织帐棚的夫妇同住做工。**转向外邦人**（5-11节）：犹太人抗拒，保罗转到会堂隔壁的一个家里传道；主在夜间异象中对他说：「不要怕，只管讲。」**迦流的判决**（12-17节）：罗马方伯拒绝审理犹太人对保罗的控告。**回程与再出发**（18-23节）：保罗结束第二次旅程，又开始第三次。**亚波罗**（24-28节）：一位满有恩赐的传道人，在百基拉、亚居拉的教导下更加明白神的道。

保罗在哥林多住了一年零六个月，在此以前，他从未在一个城住这么久。他后来写给这个教会的两封信（哥林多前后书），让我们看见这段事工的另一面。

## 深读 18:1-4 织帐棚的使徒

**1. 从雅典到哥林多。** 「这事以后，保罗离了雅典，来到哥林多。」（18:1）他后来回想：「我在你们那里，又软弱，又惧怕又甚战兢。」（林前2:3）保罗不是一位从不害怕的超人；他是带着软弱来到这座大城的。

**2. 亚居拉和百基拉。** 他「遇见一个犹太人，名叫亚居拉，他生在本都；因为克劳第命犹太人都离开罗马，新近带着妻百基拉，从意大利来」（18:2）。罗马史家苏埃托尼乌斯也记载，克劳第因犹太人「受克列斯督煽动」不断争闹，把他们赶出罗马。许多学者认为「克列斯督」就是指基督；这事一般根据较晚的史家，定在主后49年。一道驱逐令，竟成了神把这对夫妇带到保罗身边的方法。

**3. 同住做工。** 「他们本是制造帐棚为业。保罗因与他们同业，就和他们同住做工。」（18:3）保罗常常用双手做工养活自己，「免得叫你们一人受累」（帖前2:9）。他的工作坊很可能也成了他的讲台：在制作帐棚的时候，他可以和顾客、同工谈论福音。华人教会常说的「带职事奉」，也有人称为「织帐棚」的事奉，就是一边工作、一边传福音。

## 深读 18:5-11 「不要怕，只管讲」

**1. 为道迫切。** 「西拉和提摩太从马其顿来的时候，保罗为道迫切，向犹太人证明耶稣是基督。」（18:5）他们带来马其顿教会的消息和供给（参林后11:9），保罗就能更专心传道。

**2. 抖着衣裳。** 犹太人「抗拒、毁谤」，保罗就抖着衣裳说：「你们的罪归到你们自己头上，与我无干。从今以后，我要往外邦人那里去。」（18:6）这呼应以西结书中守望者的职分（结33:1-9）：守望者若已吹角警告，人却不听，罪就归到他们自己头上。

**3. 会堂隔壁。** 保罗搬到「提多‧犹士都」家里，「他的家靠近会堂」（18:7）。会堂的门关了，隔壁的门却开了。更令人惊讶的是，「管会堂的基利司布和全家都信了主，还有许多哥林多人听了，就相信受洗」（18:8）。

**4. 主在夜间说话。** 「夜间，主在异象中对保罗说：『不要怕，只管讲，不要闭口，有我与你同在，必没有人下手害你，因为在这城里我有许多的百姓。』」（18:9-10）主知道保罗害怕。他给保罗的，不是没有危险的保证，而是他同在的应许。「我有许多的百姓」：在保罗看来，哥林多满是拜偶像、放荡的人；主却已经看见了那些他要呼召归向自己的人。这是传福音最大的盼望：神在城里早有他的百姓。

**5. 一年零六个月。** 「保罗在那里住了一年零六个月，将神的道教训他们。」（18:11）主的应许没有叫保罗离开，反而叫他留下来。

> 要点：保罗害怕，主就对他说话。福音工作的勇气，不是来自没有危险，而是来自主的同在，和他「在这城里我有许多的百姓」的应许。

## 深读 18:12-17 迦流的判决

**1. 告上公堂。** 「到迦流作亚该亚方伯的时候，犹太人同心起来攻击保罗，拉他到公堂。」（18:12）在希腊德尔斐出土的一块碑文提到迦流任亚该亚方伯，时间约在主后51-52年。这是确定保罗生平年代最重要的依据之一，也显示路加记载的历史细节是可靠的。

**2. 「你们自己去办吧！」** 保罗还没开口，迦流就说：如果是冤枉或奸恶的事，「我理当耐性听你们。但所争论的，若是关乎言语、名目，和你们的律法，你们自己去办吧！这样的事我不愿意审问」（18:14-15），就把他们撵出公堂。在罗马官员看来，基督徒和犹太人的争论只是犹太教内部的事务。这个判决虽不是全帝国的法律先例，实际上却让福音在亚该亚有一段时间可以自由传讲，也可能影响其他官员的看法。

**3. 应许的应验。** 众人揪住管会堂的所提尼，在堂前打他，迦流却不管（18:17）。主曾应许「必没有人下手害你」，果然应验了。值得留意的是，哥林多前书开头提到「兄弟所提尼」（林前1:1），可能就是这位管会堂的，后来也信了主。

## 深读 18:18-23 回程与再出发

**1. 许愿剪头发。** 保罗离开哥林多前，「因为许过愿，就在坚革哩剪了头发」（18:18）。这很可能是类似拿细耳人的许愿（民6章）。保罗是信了基督的犹太人，仍可以自由地遵守某些犹太习俗，只是不把它们当作得救的条件。

**2. 「神若许我」。** 在以弗所，人请他多住些日子，他没有答应，说：「神若许我，我还要回到你们这里。」（18:21）保罗有计划，但他把计划放在神的旨意之下（参雅4:15）。

**3. 第三次旅程开始。** 他回到耶路撒冷问安，再回到安提阿，「住了些日子，又离开那里，挨次经过加拉太和弗吕家地方，坚固众门徒」（18:22-23）。路加用一两节就交代了两千多公里的路程。第二次旅程结束了，第三次旅程随即开始。

## 深读 18:24-28 亚波罗

**1. 有恩赐，心里火热。** 「有一个犹太人，名叫亚波罗，来到以弗所。他生在亚历山大，是有学问的，最能讲解圣经。这人已经在主的道上受了教训，心里火热，将耶稣的事详细讲论教训人；只是他单晓得约翰的洗礼。」（18:24-25）亚历山大是当时的学术中心。亚波罗口才好、熟悉圣经、热心，所讲的也正确，只是还不完全。约翰的洗礼是悔改的洗礼，指向那要来的主（19:4）。但对五旬节以后的事（奉耶稣名的洗礼和圣灵的浇灌），他可能还不完全明白。与19:1-7那几个门徒不同，路加没有说亚波罗重新受洗。

**2. 私下、温和的教导。** 「他在会堂里放胆讲道；百基拉、亚居拉听见，就接他来，将神的道给他讲解更加详细。」（18:26）他们没有当众纠正他，也没有批评他，而是「接他来」，很可能就是请他到家里，私下更详细地讲解。路加在这里把百基拉的名字放在前面，也许表示她在教导上特别有分，或她在教会中较有名望（参18:18；罗16:3；提后4:19）；无论如何，是夫妇二人一同教导。一对织帐棚的平信徒夫妇，教导了一位有学问的传道人。

**3. 谦卑的学习者。** 亚波罗可贵的地方，不只是口才，更是他肯受指正。他后来到亚该亚，「多帮助那蒙恩信主的人」，「引圣经证明耶稣是基督」（18:27-28）。保罗后来说：「我栽种了，亚波罗浇灌了，惟有神叫他生长。」（林前3:6）

> 要点：百基拉和亚居拉用温柔和私下的方式帮助亚波罗，亚波罗用谦卑的心领受。一个健康的教会，既有愿意教导的人，也有愿意被教导的人。

## 默想

**默想：** 保罗来到哥林多时，「又软弱，又惧怕又甚战兢」。主没有责备他的害怕，而是在夜间对他说：「不要怕……有我与你同在……在这城里我有许多的百姓。」这应许没有叫保罗离开，反而叫他留了一年零六个月。我现在是否也在一个很想离开的地方：一个人数很少、看不见果效的小教会，一份让我疲惫的工作，一个在信仰上只有我一人的家？我常以为，要看得见成果，才有力气留下来；保罗留下来的理由，却是主看见了他还看不见的人。我能不能把自己的软弱如实告诉主，然后在他放我的地方，再站一天？保罗一边织帐棚，一边传福音；把帐棚做得结实，本身就是对主的忠心。我今天手里的工作，也可以为它本身献给主，不只是传福音的「平台」。最后，亚波罗学问好、口才好，却愿意被一对织帐棚的夫妇纠正。当别人指出我在圣经或信仰上的不足时，我的第一反应是防卫，还是感谢？我常常防卫，是因为我把自己的价值放在「我是对的」上面。可是亚波罗所帮助的，是「那蒙恩信主的人」：我站立得住，是靠恩典，不是靠我懂得最多。复活的主也曾在以马忤斯的路上，耐心地把「凡经上所指着自己的话都给他们讲解明白了」（路24:27）。他这样温柔地对待糊涂的我，我也可以安心领受别人的指正，也这样温柔地「接」别人来。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 18 takes place mostly in Corinth, a wealthy, bustling port city with a reputation for immorality (partly inherited from the older Greek city destroyed by Rome), and 1 Corinthians shows real sexual-ethical struggles there. **Aquila and Priscilla** (vv. 1–4): Paul stays and works with this tentmaking couple. **Turning to the Gentiles** (vv. 5–11): the Jews resist, and Paul moves his preaching to a house next door to the synagogue; in a night vision the Lord tells him, "Do not be afraid, but go on speaking." **Gallio's ruling** (vv. 12–17): the Roman proconsul refuses to hear the Jews' case against Paul. **The return and a new start** (vv. 18–23): Paul ends his second journey and begins his third. **Apollos** (vv. 24–28): a gifted preacher comes to understand the way of God more accurately through Priscilla and Aquila.

Paul stayed in Corinth a year and six months, his longest stay in one city on his journeys so far. The two letters he later wrote to this church (1 and 2 Corinthians) show us another side of this ministry.

## Going Deeper: 18:1–4 The Tentmaking Apostle

**1. From Athens to Corinth.** "After this Paul left Athens and went to Corinth" (18:1). He later recalled, "I was with you in weakness and in fear and much trembling" (1 Cor 2:3). Paul wasn't a superman who never felt afraid; he came to this great city in weakness.

**2. Aquila and Priscilla.** "He found a Jew named Aquila, a native of Pontus, recently come from Italy with his wife Priscilla, because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to leave Rome" (18:2). The Roman historian Suetonius also records that Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome because of constant disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" (many scholars think this refers to Christ); the date is usually set at AD 49, following a later historian. An expulsion order became God's way of bringing this couple to Paul.

**3. Living and working together.** "And because he was of the same trade he stayed with them and worked, for they were tentmakers by trade" (18:3). Paul often supported himself with his own hands, "that we might not be a burden to any of you" (1 Thess 2:9). His workshop very likely became his pulpit: while making tents, he could talk about the gospel with customers and co-workers. People still call ministry combined with ordinary work "tentmaking."

## Going Deeper: 18:5–11 "Do Not Be Afraid, but Go On Speaking"

**1. Occupied with the word.** "When Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, Paul was occupied with the word, testifying to the Jews that the Christ was Jesus" (18:5). They brought news and support from the Macedonian churches (cf. 2 Cor 11:9), so Paul could devote himself more fully to preaching.

**2. Shaking out his garments.** When the Jews "opposed and reviled him," Paul shook out his garments and said, "Your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent. From now on I will go to the Gentiles" (18:6). This echoes the watchman's duty in Ezekiel (Ezek 33:1–9): if the watchman sounds the warning and people don't listen, their blood is on their own heads.

**3. Next door to the synagogue.** Paul moved to the house of Titius Justus, whose "house was next door to the synagogue" (18:7). The synagogue's door had closed; the one next door had opened. More surprising still, "Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believed in the Lord, together with his entire household. And many of the Corinthians hearing Paul believed and were baptized" (18:8).

**4. The Lord speaks at night.** "And the Lord said to Paul one night in a vision, 'Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many in this city who are my people'" (18:9–10). The Lord knew Paul was afraid. He gave him not a guarantee of no danger but the promise of his presence. "I have many in this city who are my people": to Paul, Corinth looked full of idol-worshipers and libertines; the Lord already saw those he would call to himself. This is evangelism's greatest hope: God already has his people in the city.

**5. A year and six months.** "And he stayed a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them" (18:11). The Lord's promise didn't send Paul away; it kept him there.

> The point: Paul was afraid, and the Lord spoke to him. Courage for gospel work comes not from the absence of danger but from the Lord's presence, and his promise, "I have many in this city who are my people."

## Going Deeper: 18:12–17 Gallio's Ruling

**1. Brought before the tribunal.** "But when Gallio was proconsul of Achaia, the Jews made a united attack on Paul and brought him before the tribunal" (18:12). An inscription found at Delphi in Greece mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia, around AD 51–52. It is one of the most important anchors for dating Paul's life, and it shows that Luke's historical details are reliable.

**2. "See to it yourselves."** Before Paul could open his mouth, Gallio said that if it were a matter of wrongdoing or crime, "I would have reason to accept your complaint. But since it is a matter of questions about words and names and your own law, see to it yourselves. I refuse to be a judge of these things" (18:14–15), and he drove them from the tribunal. To a Roman official, the dispute between Christians and Jews was an internal Jewish matter. Though not binding law across the empire, this ruling in practice gave the gospel freedom in Achaia for a time, and may have shaped how other officials saw the matter.

**3. The promise kept.** The crowd seized Sosthenes, the ruler of the synagogue, and beat him in front of the tribunal, and Gallio paid no attention (18:17). The Lord had promised, "no one will attack you to harm you," and so it was. Interestingly, 1 Corinthians opens with "our brother Sosthenes" (1 Cor 1:1), who may be this same synagogue ruler, later come to faith.

## Going Deeper: 18:18–23 The Return and a New Start

**1. A vow and a haircut.** Before leaving Corinth, "at Cenchreae he had cut his hair, for he was under a vow" (18:18). This was probably a vow like the Nazirite vow (Num 6). As a Jewish Christian, Paul remained free to keep certain Jewish customs, so long as they weren't treated as conditions of salvation.

**2. "If God wills."** In Ephesus people asked him to stay longer, but he declined, saying, "I will return to you if God wills" (18:21). Paul made plans, but he placed them under God's will (cf. Jas 4:15).

**3. The third journey begins.** He went up to greet the church in Jerusalem and then down to Antioch; "after spending some time there, he departed and went from one place to the next through the region of Galatia and Phrygia, strengthening all the disciples" (18:22–23). Luke covers well over a thousand miles in a verse or two. The second journey ends, and the third begins at once.

## Going Deeper: 18:24–28 Apollos

**1. Gifted and fervent.** "Now a Jew named Apollos, a native of Alexandria, came to Ephesus. He was an eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures. He had been instructed in the way of the Lord. And being fervent in spirit, he spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus, though he knew only the baptism of John" (18:24–25). Alexandria was a center of learning. Apollos was eloquent, knew the Scriptures, was zealous, and taught correctly, though incompletely. John's was a baptism of repentance pointing to the one coming after him (19:4). Apollos knew and taught Jesus accurately but may not yet have grasped what followed Pentecost (baptism in Jesus' name, the outpoured Spirit). Unlike the disciples in 19:1–7, Luke does not say Apollos was rebaptized.

**2. Private, gentle teaching.** "He began to speak boldly in the synagogue, but when Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (18:26). They didn't correct him publicly or criticize him; they "took him" (that is, took him aside), very likely into their home, and explained things more fully in private. Luke names Priscilla first here, perhaps indicating her particular role in the teaching, or her greater prominence (cf. 18:18; Rom 16:3; 2 Tim 4:19); in any case, the couple taught together. A tentmaking lay couple taught a learned preacher.

**3. A humble learner.** Apollos's greatness lay not only in his eloquence but in his willingness to be corrected. When he went on to Achaia, "he greatly helped those who through grace had believed," "showing by the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus" (18:27–28). Paul later wrote, "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Cor 3:6).

> The point: Priscilla and Aquila helped Apollos gently and privately, and Apollos received it humbly. A healthy church has people willing to teach and people willing to be taught.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** When Paul came to Corinth he was "in weakness and in fear and much trembling." The Lord didn't scold him for being afraid; he spoke to him at night: "Do not be afraid… I am with you… I have many in this city who are my people." That promise didn't send Paul away; it kept him there a year and six months. Am I in a place I'd like to leave: a small church where nothing seems to grow, a job that wears me out, a family where I'm the only believer? I often assume I can only stay if I see results; but Paul stayed because the Lord saw people Paul couldn't yet see. Can I tell the Lord honestly how weak I feel, and then stand one more day where he has put me? Paul made tents as well as preaching; a tent that held in the wind was itself faithfulness to the Lord. The work in my hands today can be offered to him for its own sake, not only as a "platform" for evangelism. Finally, Apollos, learned and eloquent, let a tentmaking couple correct him. When someone points out gaps in my understanding of Scripture or the faith, is my first reaction defensiveness or gratitude? I often get defensive because I've put my worth in being right. But Apollos helped "those who through grace had believed": I stand by grace, not by knowing the most. The risen Lord himself walked the Emmaus road with two confused disciples and "interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27). Since he has been that gentle with my confusion, I can receive correction in peace, and gently "take aside" others too.
