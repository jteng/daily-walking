## 本章脉络

第二十三章的场景在耶路撒冷和凯撒利亚之间快速转换。**在公会前**（1-10节）：保罗被大祭司下令掌嘴，说出一句严厉的话，随即又照经上的话认错；接着他宣告自己是为「盼望死人复活」受审，公会中的法利赛人和撒都该人就争吵起来。**夜间的应许**（11节）：主站在保罗旁边，说：「放心吧！」**四十人的阴谋**（12-22节）：四十多个犹太人起誓不杀保罗就不吃不喝，保罗的外甥听见了，及时报信。**夜间护送**（23-35节）：千夫长派了将近五百名兵丁，连夜把保罗送到凯撒利亚的巡抚腓力斯那里。

这一章的中心是第11节。前后都是人的争吵、阴谋和调兵遣将，中间却是主亲自站在保罗旁边的那一夜。

## 深读 23:1-5 粉饰的墙

**1. 凭着良心。** 「保罗定睛看着公会的人，说：『弟兄们，我在神面前行事为人都是凭着良心，直到今日。』」（23:1）这不是说保罗从来没有犯罪（他自称是罪人中的罪魁，提前1:15），而是说他一生都是按着自己所知道的，真诚地在神面前行事（参提后1:3；他逼迫教会也是「在不信不明白的时候」，提前1:13）。但保罗也清楚，良心无亏并不等于在神面前称义：「我虽不觉得自己有错，却也不能因此得以称义；但判断我的乃是主。」（林前4:4）

**2. 「你这粉饰的墙」。** 「大祭司亚拿尼亚就吩咐旁边站着的人打他的嘴。保罗对他说：『你这粉饰的墙，神要打你！你坐堂为的是按律法审问我，你竟违背律法，吩咐人打我吗？』」（23:2-3）这个亚拿尼亚（不是第9章那位门徒）是一位以贪婪和残暴出名的大祭司：据约瑟夫记载，他的仆人强夺祭司应得的十分之一，使贫穷的祭司饿死；主后66年他被奋锐党人杀害。「粉饰的墙」是外表刷白、里面朽坏的墙（参结13:10-16；太23:27）。保罗的话指出了真相：一个按律法审判的人，自己却违背律法。

**3. 保罗认错。** 旁边的人说：「你辱骂神的大祭司吗？」保罗说：「弟兄们，我不晓得他是大祭司；经上记着说：『不可毁谤你百姓的官长。』」（23:4-5；出22:28）保罗为什么不认识大祭司？可能因为他多年不在耶路撒冷，或这次聚会不是正式的公会审判，大祭司没有穿上礼服；也有人认为保罗视力不好；也可能是会中人多，保罗没看清是谁下的令；还有人认为保罗这话带着讽刺。但最自然的读法是：保罗一知道，就立刻按照圣经承认自己说错了话。

**4. 与耶稣的对照。** 耶稣受审时也被差役打了一掌，他却只说：「我若说的不是，你可以指证那不是；我若说的是，你为什么打我呢？」（约18:23）保罗是一个会说错话、也会立刻认错的人；只有耶稣，被打的时候没有一句不当的话。

> 要点：保罗的话说中了真相，却也说过了头。他在被不公地掌嘴时说了严厉的话，一知道错了，就立刻照着神的话认错。属神的人不是从不失言，而是在神的话面前肯低头。

## 深读 23:6-10 为盼望死人复活

**1. 法利赛人和撒都该人。** 「保罗看出大众一半是撒都该人，一半是法利赛人，就在公会中大声说：『弟兄们，我是法利赛人，也是法利赛人的子孙。我现在受审问，是为盼望死人复活。』」（23:6）撒都该人不信复活，也不信天使和鬼魂；法利赛人却信（23:8）。保罗的话一出，公会立刻分成两党争吵起来。

**2. 是策略，也是核心。** 路加说保罗「看出」（23:6）会中的组成，可见这是有意的一步；可是他所说的并不是借口，而是他信仰的核心：耶稣已经从死里复活，这正是神所应许的「死人复活」的初熟果子（林前15:20）。保罗后来在腓力斯面前，也说同样的话（24:15，21）。他被审，归根到底就是因为复活。

**3. 被救出来。** 争吵越来越激烈，千夫长「恐怕保罗被他们扯碎了，就吩咐兵丁下去，把他从众人当中抢出来」（23:10）。犹太人的最高议会，竟然不能冷静地审理一个案件；罗马人第三次把保罗从犹太人手中救出来（参21:32；22:24）。

## 深读 23:11 「放心吧！」

「当夜，主站在保罗旁边，说：『放心吧！你怎样在耶路撒冷为我作见证，也必怎样在罗马为我作见证。』」（23:11）

**1. 最低落的一夜。** 这是保罗一生中可能最低落的时刻之一：他在圣殿被打，在营楼被捆，在公会被掌嘴，前面的路完全看不清。保罗也许问过自己：坚持上耶路撒冷，是不是错了？

**2. 主站在旁边。** 就在这一夜，「主站在保罗旁边」。主没有先解释这一切为什么会发生，也没有立刻救他出监，而是亲自来到他身边。司提反殉道时，看见主「站在神的右边」（7:56）；如今，这位主站在保罗身旁。

**3. 一个必然的应许。** 「你怎样在耶路撒冷为我作见证，也必怎样在罗马为我作见证。」主没有应许保罗一路顺利，却应许他的工作不会在这里结束。保罗早就想往罗马去（19:21；罗1:10-15）；现在，主亲自确认了这件事。在保罗到达罗马之前，还有两年的监禁、几次审判、一场船难；可是从这一夜起，他知道终点在主手里。

> 要点：在最黑暗的夜里，主没有先改变环境，而是亲自站在保罗旁边，告诉他：你的路还没有走完。主的同在和应许，比任何解释更能叫人放心。

## 深读 23:12-35 四十人的誓言与五百人的护送

**1. 不吃不喝的誓言。** 「到了天亮，犹太人同谋起誓，说：『若不先杀保罗就不吃不喝。』这样同心起誓的有四十多人。」（23:12-13）他们和祭司长、长老串通，要假称再审问保罗，在路上埋伏杀他（23:14-15）。

**2. 一个外甥。** 「保罗的外甥听见他们设下埋伏，就来到营楼里告诉保罗。」（23:16）这是新约里唯一一次明确提到保罗的姐妹和她的儿子。我们不知道这位少年人怎样听见的，路加也没有说神迹；可是神就藉着一个普通的少年人，使一场周密的阴谋落空。前一夜主应许「必怎样在罗马为我作见证」，第二天，他就用最平凡的方法保守这个应许。

**3. 将近五百人的护送。** 千夫长听了，就派「步兵二百，马兵七十，长枪手二百」，在夜里九点（「亥初」）出发，把保罗送到凯撒利亚（23:23）。四十多个立誓的人，面对的是将近五百名罗马兵丁。神可以用一个少年人，也可以用一支罗马军队，来成就他的计划。

**4. 千夫长的信。** 千夫长克劳第‧吕西亚写信给巡抚腓力斯，说：「这人被犹太人拿住，将要杀害，我得知他是罗马人，就带兵丁下去救他出来。」（23:27）其实他是先救了保罗，后来才知道他是罗马人，还差点鞭打了他（22:24-29）。路加照实记下这封信，让读者看见：人总想把自己写得体面一些。可是信中也说出了一个重要的结论：「并没有什么该死该绑的罪名。」（23:29）

**5. 那四十个人呢？** 路加没有告诉我们，那四十多个起誓不吃不喝的人后来怎样了。可以肯定的是，他们没有杀成保罗。人的誓言再坚决，也拦不住神的旨意。

> 要点：主在夜间的应许，第二天就藉着一个少年人和一支军队来成就。神很少用我们想象的方式行事，却从不让他的应许落空。

## 默想

**默想：** 保罗在公会里被掌嘴，就脱口骂大祭司是「粉饰的墙」；一知道对方是大祭司，他立刻照圣经认错。我最近有没有在怒气中说过一句过头的话，对配偶、孩子、同事，或在网上？我是坚持「我说的也没错」，还是愿意像保罗一样，在神的话面前低头认错？我能这样认错，是因为那位被打也没有一句恶言的主，已经为我所有过头的话付上了代价。在保罗最低落的那一夜，「主站在保罗旁边，说：『放心吧！』」我今天有没有一件看不清前路的事：一个还没有结果的诊断、一份不知会不会保住的工作、一个让我牵挂的孩子？主不一定先给我解释，却应许他与我同在，并且我的路在他手中。最后，神用保罗的外甥这样一个普通的少年人，救了保罗的性命。我是否只在神迹奇事中寻找神的作为，却忽略了他每天藉着平凡的人和事保守我？今天，我能不能为一个曾经在关键时刻帮助过我的人感谢神？

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 23 moves quickly between Jerusalem and Caesarea. **Before the council** (vv. 1–10): ordered struck on the mouth by the high priest, Paul speaks a harsh word, then at once acknowledges his fault according to Scripture; he declares that he is on trial for "the hope and the resurrection of the dead," and the Pharisees and Sadducees on the council fall to arguing. **A promise by night** (v. 11): the Lord stands by Paul and says, "Take courage." **The plot of the forty** (vv. 12–22): more than forty Jews swear not to eat or drink until they have killed Paul; Paul's nephew overhears and warns him in time. **A night escort** (vv. 23–35): the tribune sends nearly five hundred soldiers to take Paul by night to Felix the governor in Caesarea.

At the center of the chapter is verse 11. Before and after it are human quarrels, plots and troop movements; in the middle is the night the Lord himself stood by Paul.

## Going Deeper: 23:1–5 A Whitewashed Wall

**1. A good conscience.** "And looking intently at the council, Paul said, 'Brothers, I have lived my life before God in all good conscience up to this day'" (23:1). This doesn't mean Paul never sinned (he called himself the foremost of sinners, 1 Tim 1:15), but that throughout his life he had acted sincerely before God according to what he knew (cf. 2 Tim 1:3; even his persecuting was done "ignorantly in unbelief," 1 Tim 1:13). Yet Paul knew a clear conscience is not the same as being justified: "I am not aware of anything against myself, but I am not thereby acquitted. It is the Lord who judges me" (1 Cor 4:4).

**2. "You whitewashed wall!"** "And the high priest Ananias commanded those who stood by him to strike him on the mouth. Then Paul said to him, 'God is going to strike you, you whitewashed wall! Are you sitting to judge me according to the law, and yet contrary to the law you order me to be struck?'" (23:2–3). This Ananias (not the disciple of ch. 9) was a high priest notorious for greed and violence: Josephus says his servants seized the tithes owed to the priests, so that poorer priests starved, and he was killed by Jewish insurgents in AD 66. A "whitewashed wall" looks clean outside but is rotten within (cf. Ezek 13:10–16; Matt 23:27). Paul's words exposed the truth: a man judging by the law was himself breaking it.

**3. Paul acknowledges his fault.** Those standing by said, "Would you revile God's high priest?" Paul said, "I did not know, brothers, that he was the high priest, for it is written, 'You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people'" (23:4–5; Ex 22:28). Why didn't Paul recognize the high priest? Perhaps because he had been away from Jerusalem for years, or because this wasn't a formal session and the high priest wasn't in his robes; some suggest Paul's eyesight was poor; or he may not have seen who gave the order in a crowded room; some even read his reply as ironic. But the most natural reading is that as soon as Paul knew, he admitted by Scripture that he had spoken wrongly.

**4. A contrast with Jesus.** When Jesus was struck at his trial, he said only, "If what I said is wrong, bear witness about the wrong; but if what I said is right, why do you strike me?" (John 18:23). Paul was a man who could speak wrongly and quickly own it; only Jesus, when struck, spoke not one unfitting word.

> The point: Paul's words hit the truth, but went too far. Struck unjustly, he spoke harshly, and as soon as he knew he was wrong, he owned it according to God's word. God's people aren't those who never misspeak, but those who bow before God's word.

## Going Deeper: 23:6–10 On Trial for the Resurrection

**1. Pharisees and Sadducees.** "Now when Paul perceived that one part were Sadducees and the other Pharisees, he cried out in the council, 'Brothers, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees. It is with respect to the hope and the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial'" (23:6). The Sadducees denied the resurrection, angels and spirits; the Pharisees affirmed them (23:8). At Paul's words the council split into two parties and began to quarrel.

**2. Strategy, and the heart of the matter.** Luke says Paul "perceived" (23:6) how the council was made up, so this was a deliberate move; but what he said was no pretext; it was the core of his faith: Jesus has risen from the dead, the firstfruits of the promised "resurrection of the dead" (1 Cor 15:20). Paul would say the same before Felix (24:15, 21). At bottom, he was on trial because of the resurrection.

**3. Rescued.** The quarrel grew so violent that the tribune, "afraid that Paul would be torn to pieces by them, commanded the soldiers to go down and take him away from among them by force" (23:10). The highest Jewish council couldn't calmly hear a case, and for the third time the Romans rescued Paul from Jewish hands (cf. 21:32; 22:24).

## Going Deeper: 23:11 "Take Courage"

"The following night the Lord stood by him and said, 'Take courage, for as you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome'" (23:11).

**1. The lowest night.** This may have been one of the lowest moments of Paul's life: beaten in the temple, bound in the barracks, struck before the council, with no clear way ahead. Paul may well have asked himself whether insisting on Jerusalem had been a mistake.

**2. The Lord stood by him.** That very night, "the Lord stood by him." The Lord didn't first explain why all this had happened, or immediately free him; he came and stood beside him. When Stephen was martyred, he saw the Lord "standing at the right hand of God" (7:56); now that Lord stood beside Paul.

**3. A certain promise.** "As you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome." The Lord didn't promise Paul an easy road, but he promised that his work would not end here. Paul had long wanted to go to Rome (19:21; Rom 1:10–15); now the Lord himself confirmed it. Before Paul reached Rome there would be two years in custody, several trials and a shipwreck; but from that night on, he knew the destination was in the Lord's hands.

> The point: On the darkest night, the Lord didn't change the circumstances first; he came and stood by Paul and told him his road wasn't finished. The Lord's presence and promise give more courage than any explanation.

## Going Deeper: 23:12–35 Forty Oaths and Five Hundred Soldiers

**1. An oath not to eat or drink.** "When it was day, the Jews made a plot and bound themselves by an oath neither to eat nor drink till they had killed Paul. There were more than forty who made this conspiracy" (23:12–13). They conspired with the chief priests and elders to request another hearing and ambush Paul on the way (23:14–15).

**2. A nephew.** "Now the son of Paul's sister heard of their ambush, so he went and entered the barracks and told Paul" (23:16). This is the only explicit reference in the New Testament to Paul's sister and her son. We don't know how the young man heard, and Luke reports no miracle; yet God used an ordinary young man to bring a carefully laid plot to nothing. The night before, the Lord promised Paul would testify in Rome; the next day, he kept that promise by the most ordinary means.

**3. An escort of nearly five hundred.** The tribune sent "two hundred soldiers, with seventy horsemen and two hundred spearmen," setting out at nine at night, to take Paul to Caesarea (23:23). More than forty sworn men now faced nearly five hundred Roman soldiers. God can use a young man, and he can use a detachment of Roman troops, to accomplish his plan.

**4. The tribune's letter.** Claudius Lysias wrote to Felix: "This man was seized by the Jews and was about to be killed by them when I came upon them with the soldiers and rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman citizen" (23:27). In fact he rescued Paul first and learned of his citizenship later, after nearly flogging him (22:24–29). Luke records the letter as it was, letting readers see how people like to present themselves well. Yet the letter also states an important conclusion: "charged with nothing deserving death or imprisonment" (23:29).

**5. And the forty?** Luke doesn't tell us what became of the forty men who swore not to eat or drink. What is certain is that they did not kill Paul. However firm human oaths may be, they cannot stop God's will.

> The point: The Lord's promise by night was kept the next day through a young man and an army. God rarely works the way we imagine, but he never lets his promises fail.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Struck before the council, Paul blurted out that the high priest was a "whitewashed wall"; as soon as he learned who it was, he owned his fault by Scripture. Have I recently said something too harsh in anger, to my spouse, my children, a coworker, or online? Do I insist "but I wasn't wrong," or am I willing, like Paul, to bow before God's word and admit it? I can admit it because the Lord who was struck without a single evil word has already paid for all my excessive words. On Paul's lowest night, "the Lord stood by him and said, 'Take courage.'" Is there something in my life today with no clear way ahead: a diagnosis with no answer yet, a job that may not last, a child I worry about? The Lord may not explain first, but he promises to be with me and that my road is in his hands. Finally, God used Paul's nephew, an ordinary young man, to save Paul's life. Do I look for God's work only in miracles, and miss how he keeps me every day through ordinary people and events? Today, could I thank God for someone who helped me at a critical moment?
