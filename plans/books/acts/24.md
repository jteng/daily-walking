## 本章脉络

第二十四章是保罗在罗马巡抚腓力斯面前的受审。**控告**（1-9节）：大祭司带着律师帖土罗下到凯撒利亚，帖土罗先恭维巡抚，然后控告保罗是「瘟疫」、「拿撒勒教党里的一个头目」，又想污秽圣殿。**保罗的申辩**（10-21节）：保罗逐条回应，并且承认他所事奉的，就是他们称为「异端」的「这道」。**腓力斯的拖延**（22-27节）：腓力斯和他的妻子土西拉听保罗讲论信基督耶稣的道，听到「公义、节制，和将来的审判」，就「甚觉恐惧」，却叫保罗暂且离去；他一面盼望保罗送他银钱，一面为讨犹太人喜欢，把保罗留在监里整整两年。

这一章是一场两种人的对比：一位被锁着、却心里自由的囚犯，和一位手握大权、心里却被恐惧和贪婪捆绑的巡抚。

## 深读 24:1-9 帖土罗的控告

**1. 一位律师。** 「过了五天，大祭司亚拿尼亚同几个长老，和一个辩士帖土罗下来，向巡抚控告保罗。」（24:1）帖土罗可能是一位熟悉罗马法律程序的律师。犹太领袖为了对付一个囚犯，专程从耶路撒冷下来，还请了专业的辩士。

**2. 恭维的开场。** 「腓力斯大人，我们因你得以大享太平，并且这一国的弊病，因着你的先见得以更正了；我们随时随地满心感谢不尽。」（24:2-3）这是当时法庭上常用的恭维话，可是与事实相去甚远：罗马史家塔西佗说，腓力斯是以奴隶的心肠，行君王的权柄，他在任时犹太地的动乱反而越来越多。

**3. 三项罪名。** 帖土罗控告保罗：一，他「如同瘟疫一般，是鼓动普天下众犹太人生乱的」（煽动叛乱，这在罗马法中是重罪）；二，他是「拿撒勒教党里的一个头目」；三，他「连圣殿他也想要污秽」（24:5-6）。（有些抄本在第6-8节多了几句话，现代译本多放在注脚。）

## 深读 24:10-21 保罗的申辩

**1. 不恭维，也不失礼。** 「我知道你在这国里断事多年，所以我乐意为自己分诉。」（24:10）保罗的开场只说了一句真实的话：腓力斯确实在这里断事多年，熟悉犹太人的事。他没有恭维，也没有失礼。

**2. 逐条回应。** 保罗说，他上耶路撒冷才十二天，没有在殿里、会堂里或城里与人辩论，也没有聚众生事，「他们现在所告我的事并不能对你证实了」（24:11-13）。他们拿不出证据。

**3. 「他们所称为异端的道」。** 「但有一件事，我向你承认，就是他们所称为异端的道，我正按着那道事奉我祖宗的神，又信合乎律法的和先知书上一切所记载的。」（24:14）保罗不否认自己属于「这道」，但他指出，这不是一个新宗教，而是旧约所应许的成全。他所事奉的，是「我祖宗的神」；他所信的，正是律法和先知所写的。

**4. 复活的盼望。** 「并且靠着神，盼望死人，无论善恶，都要复活，就是他们自己也有这个盼望。」（24:15）保罗又回到复活。这里他说「无论善恶，都要复活」：复活不只是给义人的盼望，也是所有人都要面对的事实（参约5:28-29）。有复活，就有审判。

**5. 无亏的良心。** 「我因此自己勉励，对神对人，常存无亏的良心。」（24:16）因为有复活和审判，保罗就认真地活：不只在神面前，也在人面前，保持无亏的良心。

**6. 捐项。** 「过了几年，我带着周济本国的捐项和供献的物上去。」（24:17）这是使徒行传中唯一直接提到保罗为耶路撒冷筹集的捐项（参罗15:25-28）。保罗是带着爱心和礼物来的，却被当作污秽圣殿的人捉拿。

> 要点：保罗的申辩不靠恭维，也不靠诡辩，只靠真实。他承认自己属于「这道」，宣告复活的盼望，并且因为将来的审判，在神和人面前都存着无亏的良心。

## 深读 24:22-27 腓力斯的拖延

**1. 详细晓得这道。** 「腓力斯本是详细晓得这道，就支吾他们说：『且等千夫长吕西亚下来，我要审断你们的事。』」（24:22）腓力斯知道保罗无罪，却不愿意得罪犹太领袖，就用拖延来逃避作决定。他吩咐百夫长看守保罗，却「宽待他」，也准他的亲友来供给他（24:23）。

**2. 腓力斯和土西拉。** 「过了几天，腓力斯和他夫人－犹太的女子土西拉－一同来到，就叫了保罗来，听他讲论信基督耶稣的道。」（24:24）土西拉是第十二章那位希律亚基帕一世的女儿。据历史记载，她原是另一个王的妻子，是腓力斯引诱她离开丈夫，嫁给了他。

**3. 「公义、节制，和将来的审判」。** 「保罗讲论公义、节制，和将来的审判。腓力斯甚觉恐惧。」（24:25）保罗讲的这三件事，正好刺中腓力斯：一个不公义的官，一个夺人之妻、不知节制的人，一个将来要站在神面前受审的人。保罗没有因为对方是掌握他生死的巡抚，就只讲他爱听的；他也没有只讲道德，而是讲「信基督耶稣的道」：在那将来的审判以前，人可以藉着基督得着公义。

**4. 「你暂且去吧」。** 腓力斯说：「你暂且去吧，等我得便再叫你来。」（24:25）他害怕了，却没有悔改。他后来「屡次叫他来，和他谈论」，路加却说，那是因为他「指望保罗送他银钱」（24:26）。他听了许多次福音，却从来没有回应。

**5. 两年。** 「过了两年，波求‧非斯都接了腓力斯的任；腓力斯要讨犹太人的喜欢，就留保罗在监里。」（24:27）保罗在凯撒利亚的监里度过了整整两年。在人看来，这是白白浪费的两年；可是神没有浪费它。许多学者认为，路加很可能就是在这段时间，在犹太地搜集资料、访问见证人，为写路加福音作准备（路1:1-4）。

> 要点：腓力斯听见了福音，也被真理打动，却因为贪财、怕得罪人，一次又一次地把神的呼召推开。保罗被锁在监里，心却是自由的；腓力斯坐在审判座上，心却被捆绑。

## 默想

**默想：** 帖土罗一开口，就满口恭维腓力斯；保罗却只说了一句真实的话，然后就讲事实。在我的工作场所和人际关系中，我是否也习惯说「场面话」，在上司或有权势的人面前，说一些自己都不相信的好话？我能不能学习保罗，既有礼貌，又只说真实的话？保罗说，因为将来有复活和审判，他「对神对人，常存无亏的良心」。我是否只在乎神怎样看我，却不在意自己在人面前留下的见证：欠人的钱、答应了没做的事、一次没有道歉的冲突？基督的血已经洁净了我的良心（来9:14），所以我不需要假装无亏，也可以诚实地去处理那些亏欠。最后，保罗在凯撒利亚的监里等了两年，看似一事无成。我生命中有没有一段「被关起来」的日子：长期的病、照顾年老父母的岁月、一个看不到出路的等待？那位在保罗的两年里仍在工作的神，也没有浪费我的这段日子。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 24 is Paul's trial before Felix, the Roman governor. **The accusation** (vv. 1–9): the high priest comes down to Caesarea with a lawyer named Tertullus, who flatters the governor and then accuses Paul of being "a plague," "a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes," and of trying to profane the temple. **Paul's defense** (vv. 10–21): Paul answers point by point and confesses that he worships according to "the Way, which they call a sect." **Felix's delay** (vv. 22–27): Felix and his wife Drusilla hear Paul speak about faith in Christ Jesus; when Paul reasons about "righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment," Felix is "alarmed" but sends him away. Hoping for a bribe and wanting to please the Jews, Felix leaves Paul in prison for two full years.

The chapter sets two men side by side: a prisoner in chains who is free in heart, and a governor with great power whose heart is bound by fear and greed.

## Going Deeper: 24:1–9 Tertullus's Accusation

**1. A lawyer.** "And after five days the high priest Ananias came down with some elders and a spokesman, one Tertullus. They laid before the governor their case against Paul" (24:1). Tertullus was probably an advocate familiar with Roman legal procedure. To deal with a single prisoner, the Jewish leaders came down from Jerusalem and hired a professional speaker.

**2. A flattering opening.** "Since through you we enjoy much peace, and since by your foresight, most excellent Felix, reforms are being made for this nation, in every way and everywhere we accept this with all gratitude" (24:2–3). This was the customary flattery of the courts, and far from the facts: the Roman historian Tacitus said Felix "exercised the power of a king with the mind of a slave," and unrest in Judea grew worse under his rule.

**3. Three charges.** Tertullus accused Paul of being "a plague, one who stirs up riots among all the Jews throughout the world" (sedition, a serious crime under Roman law); of being "a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes"; and of trying "to profane the temple" (24:5–6). (Some manuscripts add more in vv. 6–8; modern translations usually put it in a footnote.)

## Going Deeper: 24:10–21 Paul's Defense

**1. No flattery, no rudeness.** "Knowing that for many years you have been a judge over this nation, I cheerfully make my defense" (24:10). Paul's opening says only something true: Felix had indeed judged there for years and knew Jewish affairs. He neither flattered nor was discourteous.

**2. Point by point.** Paul said he had gone up to Jerusalem only twelve days earlier, had not disputed or stirred up a crowd in the temple, synagogues or city, "neither can they prove to you what they now bring up against me" (24:11–13). They had no evidence.

**3. "The Way, which they call a sect."** "But this I confess to you, that according to the Way, which they call a sect, I worship the God of our fathers, believing everything laid down by the Law and written in the Prophets" (24:14). Paul doesn't deny belonging to "the Way," but he points out that it isn't a new religion; it is the fulfillment the Old Testament promised. He worships "the God of our fathers" and believes what the Law and Prophets wrote.

**4. The hope of resurrection.** "Having a hope in God, which these men themselves accept, that there will be a resurrection of both the just and the unjust" (24:15). Paul returns again to the resurrection. "Both the just and the unjust": resurrection is not only a hope for the righteous but a fact everyone must face (cf. John 5:28–29). Where there is resurrection, there is judgment.

**5. A clear conscience.** "So I always take pains to have a clear conscience toward both God and man" (24:16). Because resurrection and judgment are coming, Paul lives carefully, keeping a clear conscience not only before God but before people.

**6. The collection.** "Now after several years I came to bring alms to my nation and to present offerings" (24:17). This is the only direct mention in Acts of the gift Paul gathered for Jerusalem (cf. Rom 15:25–28). Paul came with love and a gift, yet was seized as a defiler of the temple.

> The point: Paul's defense relied on neither flattery nor clever argument, only on truth. He confessed belonging to "the Way," proclaimed the hope of resurrection, and, because judgment is coming, kept a clear conscience before God and people.

## Going Deeper: 24:22–27 Felix Delays

**1. Accurate knowledge of the Way.** "But Felix, having a rather accurate knowledge of the Way, put them off, saying, 'When Lysias the tribune comes down, I will decide your case'" (24:22). Felix knew Paul was innocent, but didn't want to offend the Jewish leaders, so he avoided a decision by delay. He ordered the centurion to keep Paul in custody but "let him have some liberty," and allowed his friends to attend to his needs (24:23).

**2. Felix and Drusilla.** "After some days Felix came with his wife Drusilla, who was Jewish, and he sent for Paul and heard him speak about faith in Christ Jesus" (24:24). Drusilla was the daughter of Herod Agrippa I of chapter 12. According to historical sources, she had been married to another king, and Felix persuaded her to leave her husband and marry him.

**3. "Righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment."** "And as he reasoned about righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment, Felix was alarmed" (24:25). These three topics struck Felix exactly: an unjust ruler, a man who took another's wife without restraint, a man who would one day stand before God's judgment. Paul didn't tell the governor who held his life only what he wanted to hear; nor did he speak only of morals, but of "faith in Christ Jesus": before that coming judgment, a person can receive righteousness through Christ.

**4. "Go away for the present."** Felix said, "Go away for the present. When I get an opportunity I will summon you" (24:25). He was afraid, but did not repent. He "sent for him often and conversed with him," but Luke says it was because "he hoped that money would be given him by Paul" (24:26). He heard the gospel many times and never responded.

**5. Two years.** "When two years had elapsed, Felix was succeeded by Porcius Festus. And desiring to do the Jews a favor, Felix left Paul in prison" (24:27). Paul spent two full years in prison in Caesarea. To human eyes, two wasted years; but God did not waste them. Many scholars think Luke was very likely gathering material and interviewing witnesses in Judea during this time, in preparation for writing his Gospel (Luke 1:1–4).

> The point: Felix heard the gospel and was moved by its truth, but out of greed and fear of offending others he pushed God's call away again and again. Paul was chained in prison but free in heart; Felix sat on the judgment seat, but his heart was bound.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Tertullus opened with lavish flattery of Felix; Paul said one true sentence and then stated the facts. At work and in my relationships, am I in the habit of saying the expected polite things, flattering bosses or powerful people with words I don't believe myself? Can I learn from Paul to be courteous and still say only what is true? Paul said that because resurrection and judgment are coming, he took pains "to have a clear conscience toward both God and man." Do I care only how God sees me, while neglecting the witness I leave before people: money I owe, something I promised and never did, a conflict I never apologized for? Christ's blood has already cleansed my conscience (Heb 9:14), so I needn't pretend to be blameless; I can honestly go and deal with those debts. Finally, Paul waited two years in a Caesarean prison, seemingly accomplishing nothing. Is there a season in my life when I feel "locked up": a long illness, years caring for aging parents, a wait with no way out in sight? The God who was still at work in Paul's two years is not wasting this season of mine either.
