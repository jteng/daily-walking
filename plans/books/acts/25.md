## 本章脉络

第二十五章，新巡抚非斯都上任，保罗的案子重新开始。**耶路撒冷的请求**（1-5节）：祭司长和犹太人的首领求非斯都把保罗提到耶路撒冷，想在路上埋伏杀他。**在凯撒利亚受审**（6-12节）：犹太人拿不出证据，非斯都却想讨犹太人的喜欢，问保罗是否愿意上耶路撒冷受审；保罗说：「我要上告于凯撒。」**亚基帕王来访**（13-22节）：非斯都向亚基帕王说明案情，说争论的焦点是「一个人名叫耶稣，是已经死了，保罗却说他是活着的」。**大张威势的公厅**（23-27节）：亚基帕和百妮基「大张威势而来」，保罗被带进来，非斯都承认自己找不到可以向皇帝奏明的罪状。

两年过去了，换了一位巡抚，犹太领袖要杀保罗的心却没有变。可是神的计划也没有变：主曾说，保罗「必怎样在罗马为我作见证」（23:11）。这一章，正是这个应许向前推进的一步。

## 深读 25:1-12 上告于凯撒

**1. 两年后的阴谋。** 非斯都到任才三天，就上耶路撒冷去。「祭司长和犹太人的首领向他控告保罗，又央告他，求他的情，将保罗提到耶路撒冷来，他们要在路上埋伏杀害他。」（25:2-3）这和两年前那四十个人的阴谋一模一样（23:12-15）。仇恨没有因为时间而淡去。

**2. 不能证实的罪名。** 在凯撒利亚，「那些从耶路撒冷下来的犹太人周围站着，将许多重大的事控告他，都是不能证实的」（25:7）。保罗分诉说：「无论犹太人的律法，或是圣殿，或是凯撒，我都没有干犯。」（25:8）三个方面，正好对应他被控告的三类罪名：宗教、圣殿、政治。

**3. 非斯都的妥协。** 「但非斯都要讨犹太人的喜欢，就问保罗说：『你愿意上耶路撒冷去，在那里听我审断这事吗？』」（25:9）新巡抚想要和当地领袖搞好关系。保罗却很清楚：去耶路撒冷，就是走进埋伏。

**4. 「就是死，我也不辞」。** 「保罗说：『我站在凯撒的堂前，这就是我应当受审的地方。我向犹太人并没有行过什么不义的事，这也是你明明知道的。我若行了不义的事，犯了什么该死的罪，就是死，我也不辞。他们所告我的事若都不实，就没有人可以把我交给他们。我要上告于凯撒。』」（25:10-11）保罗不是为了逃避审判，他说，如果他真的犯了该死的罪，他愿意接受死刑。他所拒绝的，是在没有罪的情况下，被不公义地交在仇敌手里。

**5. 往凯撒那里去。** 「非斯都和议会商量了，就说：『你既上告于凯撒，可以往凯撒那里去。』」（25:12）罗马公民有权向皇帝上诉。当时的皇帝是尼禄，他在位初期还算温和，后来才大肆逼迫基督徒。保罗上告，不只是自保，也是走在神的应许上：主说他要在罗马作见证，如今罗马政府要亲自护送他去。

> 要点：保罗不逃避公义的审判，却也不接受不公义的出卖。他合法地上告于凯撒，而神就藉着罗马的法律，把他带往主所应许的罗马。

## 深读 25:13-22 「一个人名叫耶稣」

**1. 亚基帕和百妮基。** 「过了些日子，亚基帕王和百妮基氏来到凯撒利亚，问非斯都安。」（25:13）这位亚基帕是希律亚基帕二世，第十二章那位被虫所咬而死的希律的儿子。百妮基是他的妹妹，也是土西拉的姐姐（24:24）。亚基帕熟悉犹太人的律法和习俗，罗马人也让他管理圣殿的事务。

**2. 罗马的条例。** 非斯都对亚基帕说，祭司长和长老曾求他定保罗的罪，他却回答：「无论什么人，被告还没有和原告对质，未得机会分诉所告他的事，就先定他的罪，这不是罗马人的条例。」（25:16）路加让我们看见，罗马的法律在这里比犹太领袖更公正。

**3. 事情的核心。** 非斯都说，他原以为是什么严重的恶事，结果只是「有几样辩论，为他们自己敬鬼神的事，又为一个人名叫耶稣，是已经死了，保罗却说他是活着的」（25:19）。这是一个局外人对整件事的总结，却无意中说中了福音的核心：耶稣是死了，还是活着？如果耶稣仍在坟墓里，保罗的信仰就是一场误会；如果他活着，一切都改变了。保罗后来写道：「若基督没有复活，我们所传的便是枉然，你们所信的也是枉然。」（林前15:14）

**4. 「我心里作难」。** 非斯都说：「这些事当怎样究问，我心里作难。」（25:20）一位罗马巡抚，懂得罗马法律，却不知道该怎样处理「一个人是否从死里复活」这样的问题。这正是福音给世上的权势和智慧所带来的难题。

**5. 「我自己也愿听」。** 「亚基帕对非斯都说：『我自己也愿听这人辩论。』」（25:22）这让人想起他的家族：他的曾祖父大希律想见初生的耶稣，为要杀他（太2:8）；他的叔祖父希律安提帕想见耶稣，为要看他行神迹（路23:8）。现在，亚基帕想听保罗讲论这位耶稣。

> 要点：非斯都以为这只是一场宗教争论，却无意中说出了问题的核心：那位名叫耶稣的人，是死了，还是活着？整个福音，都取决于这个问题的答案。

## 深读 25:23-27 大张威势

**1. 两种荣耀。** 「第二天，亚基帕和百妮基大张威势而来，同着众千夫长和城里的尊贵人进了公厅。非斯都吩咐一声，就有人将保罗带进来。」（25:23）一边是王、王后、军官和城中的权贵，穿着华服，前呼后拥；另一边是一个戴着锁链的囚犯。在人看来，荣耀全在那一边。可是几十年后，亚基帕的王国已经消失，非斯都的名字只在历史的注脚中出现；保罗所写的书信，今天仍在全世界被人诵读。

**2. 非斯都的难处。** 非斯都承认：「我查明他没有犯什么该死的罪」，可是保罗已经上告于皇帝，他必须写一份报告，「论到这人，我没有确实的事可以奏明主上」（25:25-26）。他希望亚基帕能帮他找到一些可以写的罪状，因为「解送囚犯，不指明他的罪案是不合理的」（25:27）。

**3. 一再被宣告无罪。** 这已经是使徒行传中第二位罗马官员承认保罗无罪：千夫长吕西亚（23:29）、非斯都（25:25）；下一章亚基帕王也要这样说（26:31-32）。路加写这些，或许也是为了让罗马世界的读者知道：基督徒不是叛乱分子。

> 要点：在世人眼中，荣耀属于大张威势的王和巡抚；可是在神的计划里，那个戴着锁链的囚犯，才是这一幕真正的主角。

## 默想

**默想：** 非斯都用一句话总结了整个案子：「一个人名叫耶稣，是已经死了，保罗却说他是活着的。」对我来说，耶稣的复活是信仰里一个可有可无的教义，还是一切的根基？如果耶稣仍在坟墓里，我今天的生活会有什么不同？正因为他活着，我的罪真的被赦免了，我的盼望也不会落空（林前15:17）。保罗说：「我若行了不义的事……就是死，我也不辞。」他不怕面对公正的审判，因为他问心无愧。我在工作或家庭里犯了错的时候，是想办法掩饰、推卸，还是愿意承担应有的后果？我能这样坦然，不是因为我从不犯错，而是因为最可怕的审判，基督已经替我承受了。最后，亚基帕和百妮基「大张威势而来」，保罗却戴着锁链被带进来。我心里最羡慕的，是哪一种荣耀？在人看来体面风光的那一边，还是那位为了我甘愿被锁、被钉的主所在的那一边？

## The Shape of the Chapter

In chapter 25 a new governor, Festus, takes office and Paul's case starts again. **The request from Jerusalem** (vv. 1–5): the chief priests and Jewish leaders ask Festus to bring Paul to Jerusalem, planning to ambush him on the way. **Trial at Caesarea** (vv. 6–12): the Jews can't prove their charges, but Festus, wanting to please them, asks whether Paul will go to Jerusalem for trial; Paul says, "I appeal to Caesar." **King Agrippa's visit** (vv. 13–22): Festus explains the case to King Agrippa, saying the dispute is "about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." **The audience hall** (vv. 23–27): Agrippa and Bernice arrive "with great pomp," Paul is brought in, and Festus admits he has found nothing definite to write to the emperor.

Two years have passed and there is a new governor, but the Jewish leaders' resolve to kill Paul hasn't changed. Nor has God's plan: the Lord had said Paul "must testify also in Rome" (23:11). This chapter moves that promise forward.

## Going Deeper: 25:1–12 Appealing to Caesar

**1. A plot two years on.** Just three days after arriving, Festus went up to Jerusalem. "And the chief priests and the principal men of the Jews laid out their case against Paul, and they urged him, asking as a favor against Paul that he summon him to Jerusalem—because they were planning an ambush to kill him on the way" (25:2–3). It was the same plan as the forty men two years earlier (23:12–15). Time had not cooled their hatred.

**2. Charges they could not prove.** At Caesarea, "the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem stood around him, bringing many and serious charges against him that they could not prove" (25:7). Paul argued, "Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar have I committed any offense" (25:8). The three answer the three kinds of charge against him: religious, temple, political.

**3. Festus compromises.** "But Festus, wishing to do the Jews a favor, said to Paul, 'Do you wish to go up to Jerusalem and there be tried on these charges before me?'" (25:9). The new governor wanted good relations with the local leaders. Paul knew well that going to Jerusalem meant walking into an ambush.

**4. "I do not seek to escape death."** "But Paul said, 'I am standing before Caesar's tribunal, where I ought to be tried. To the Jews I have done no wrong, as you yourselves know very well. If then I am a wrongdoer and have committed anything for which I deserve to die, I do not seek to escape death. But if there is nothing to their charges against me, no one can give me up to them. I appeal to Caesar'" (25:10–11). Paul wasn't trying to escape judgment; if he had truly committed a capital crime, he said, he would accept death. What he refused was to be handed over unjustly to his enemies when he was innocent.

**5. To Caesar you shall go.** "Then Festus, when he had conferred with his council, answered, 'To Caesar you have appealed; to Caesar you shall go'" (25:12). Roman citizens had the right to appeal to the emperor. The emperor then was Nero, still relatively moderate in his early years; only later did he brutally persecute Christians. Paul's appeal was not only self-protection; it was a step along God's promise: the Lord had said he would testify in Rome, and now the Roman government itself would escort him there.

> The point: Paul did not run from just judgment, but he would not accept unjust betrayal. He lawfully appealed to Caesar, and God used Roman law to carry him to the Rome the Lord had promised.

## Going Deeper: 25:13–22 "A Certain Jesus"

**1. Agrippa and Bernice.** "Now when some days had passed, Agrippa the king and Bernice arrived at Caesarea and greeted Festus" (25:13). This was Herod Agrippa II, son of the Herod eaten by worms in chapter 12. Bernice was his sister, and the sister of Drusilla (24:24). Agrippa knew Jewish law and customs well, and the Romans had given him oversight of temple affairs.

**2. Roman custom.** Festus told Agrippa that the chief priests and elders had asked him to condemn Paul, but he had answered "that it was not the custom of the Romans to give up anyone before the accused met the accusers face to face and had opportunity to make his defense concerning the charge laid against him" (25:16). Luke shows Roman law here as fairer than the Jewish leaders.

**3. The heart of the matter.** Festus said he had expected some serious crime, but instead "they had certain points of dispute with him about their own religion and about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19). It is an outsider's summary of the whole affair, and it unwittingly names the heart of the gospel: is Jesus dead, or alive? If Jesus is still in the tomb, Paul's faith is a mistake; if he is alive, everything changes. Paul would write, "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Cor 15:14).

**4. "Being at a loss."** Festus said, "Being at a loss how to investigate these questions..." (25:20). A Roman governor who knew Roman law did not know how to handle a question like whether a man had risen from the dead. That is exactly the problem the gospel poses to the world's power and wisdom.

**5. "I would like to hear the man myself."** "Then Agrippa said to Festus, 'I would like to hear the man myself'" (25:22). It recalls his family: his great-grandfather Herod the Great wanted to find the newborn Jesus in order to kill him (Matt 2:8); his great-uncle Herod Antipas wanted to see Jesus in hope of a miracle (Luke 23:8). Now Agrippa wanted to hear Paul speak about this Jesus.

> The point: Festus thought this was merely a religious dispute, but unwittingly named its heart: is the man called Jesus dead or alive? The whole gospel turns on the answer.

## Going Deeper: 25:23–27 Great Pomp

**1. Two kinds of glory.** "So on the next day Agrippa and Bernice came with great pomp, and they entered the audience hall with the military tribunes and the prominent men of the city. Then, at the command of Festus, Paul was brought in" (25:23). On one side, a king, a queen, officers and the city's elite, in splendid dress with their retinues; on the other, a prisoner in chains. To human eyes, all the glory was on one side. Yet within a few decades Agrippa's kingdom was gone and Festus survived only as a footnote in history; Paul's letters are still read aloud all over the world today.

**2. Festus's problem.** Festus admitted, "I found that he had done nothing deserving death," yet since Paul had appealed to the emperor he had to send a report: "I have nothing definite to write to my lord about him" (25:25–26). He hoped Agrippa could help him find something to write, for "it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to indicate the charges against him" (25:27).

**3. Declared innocent again and again.** This makes the second Roman official in Acts to acknowledge Paul's innocence, after the tribune Lysias (23:29); in the next chapter Agrippa will say the same (26:31–32). Luke may record this partly so that readers in the Roman world would know that Christians were not rebels.

> The point: In the world's eyes, glory belonged to the king and governor arriving with great pomp; but in God's plan, the prisoner in chains was the real center of the scene.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Festus summed up the whole case in one sentence: "a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." For me, is Jesus' resurrection an optional doctrine, or the foundation of everything? If Jesus were still in the tomb, how would my life today be different? Because he lives, my sins are truly forgiven and my hope will not be put to shame (1 Cor 15:17). Paul said, "If then I am a wrongdoer... I do not seek to escape death." He didn't fear fair judgment, because his conscience was clear. When I've done wrong at work or at home, do I look for ways to cover it up or shift the blame, or am I willing to accept the consequences I deserve? I can face it openly not because I never fail, but because Christ has already borne the most terrible judgment for me. Finally, Agrippa and Bernice came "with great pomp," and Paul was brought in in chains. Which glory do I most envy in my heart? The impressive, respectable side, or the side where the Lord stands, who let himself be bound and crucified for me?
