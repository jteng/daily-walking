## 本章脉络

第二十五章，新巡抚非斯都上任，保罗的案子重新开始。**耶路撒冷的请求**（1-5节）：祭司长和犹太人的首领求非斯都把保罗提到耶路撒冷，想在路上埋伏杀他。**在凯撒利亚受审**（6-12节）：犹太人拿不出证据，非斯都却想讨犹太人的喜欢，问保罗是否愿意上耶路撒冷受审；保罗说：「我要上告于凯撒。」**亚基帕王来访**（13-22节）：非斯都向亚基帕王说明案情，说争论的焦点是「一个人名叫耶稣，是已经死了，保罗却说他是活着的」。**大张威势**（23-27节）：亚基帕和百妮基「大张威势而来」，保罗被带进来，非斯都承认自己找不到可以向皇帝奏明的罪状。

两年过去了，换了一位巡抚，犹太领袖要杀保罗的心却没有变。可是神的计划也没有变：主曾说，保罗「必怎样在罗马为我作见证」（23:11）。这一章所记的，正是这应许向前推进的一步。

## 深读 25:1-12 上告于凯撒

**1. 两年后的阴谋。** 非斯都到任才三天，就上耶路撒冷去。「祭司长和犹太人的首领向他控告保罗，又央告他，求他的情，将保罗提到耶路撒冷来，他们要在路上埋伏杀害他。」（25:2-3）这和两年前那四十个人的阴谋如出一辙（23:12-15）。仇恨没有因为时间而淡去。

**2. 不能证实的罪名。** 在凯撒利亚，「那些从耶路撒冷下来的犹太人周围站着，将许多重大的事控告他，都是不能证实的」（25:7）。保罗分诉说：「无论犹太人的律法，或是圣殿，或是凯撒，我都没有干犯。」（25:8）律法、圣殿、凯撒，正好对应他被控告的三类罪名。

**3. 非斯都的妥协。** 「但非斯都要讨犹太人的喜欢，就问保罗说：『你愿意上耶路撒冷去，在那里听我审断这事吗？』」（25:9）新巡抚想要和当地领袖搞好关系。保罗却很清楚：去耶路撒冷，就是走进埋伏。

**4. 「就是死，我也不辞」。** 「保罗说：『我站在凯撒的堂前，这就是我应当受审的地方。我向犹太人并没有行过什么不义的事，这也是你明明知道的。我若行了不义的事，犯了什么该死的罪，就是死，我也不辞。他们所告我的事若都不实，就没有人可以把我交给他们。我要上告于凯撒。』」（25:10-11）保罗上告，不是要逃避审判。他说得很清楚：若真犯了该死的罪，他甘愿受死。他所拒绝的，是自己没有罪，却被冤枉地交在仇敌手里。

**5. 往凯撒那里去。** 「非斯都和议会商量了，就说：『你既上告于凯撒，可以往凯撒那里去。』」（25:12）罗马公民有权向皇帝上诉；非斯都与议会商量后，接受了这上诉。当时的皇帝是尼禄，他在位初期还算温和，后来才大肆逼迫基督徒。保罗上告，不只是自保，也是顺着神的应许往前走：主说他要在罗马作见证，如今罗马政府要派兵护送他去。

> 要点：保罗不逃避公义的审判，却也不接受不公义的出卖。他合法地上告于凯撒，而神就藉着罗马的法律，把他带往主所应许的罗马。

## 深读 25:13-22 「一个人名叫耶稣」

**1. 亚基帕和百妮基。** 「过了些日子，亚基帕王和百妮基氏来到凯撒利亚，问非斯都安。」（25:13）这位亚基帕是希律亚基帕二世，他的父亲就是第十二章被虫咬死的那位希律王。百妮基是他的妹妹，也是土西拉的姐姐（24:24）。亚基帕熟悉犹太人的律法和习俗，罗马人还让他有权任命大祭司，并管理圣殿的库房。

**2. 罗马的条例。** 非斯都对亚基帕说，祭司长和长老曾求他定保罗的罪，他却回答：「无论什么人，被告还没有和原告对质，未得机会分诉所告他的事，就先定他的罪，这不是罗马人的条例。」（25:16）路加让我们看见，罗马的法律在这里比犹太领袖更公正。

**3. 事情的核心。** 非斯都说，他原以为是什么严重的恶事，结果只是「有几样辩论，为他们自己敬鬼神的事，又为一个人名叫耶稣，是已经死了，保罗却说他是活着的」（25:19）。这是一个局外人对整件事的总结，却无意中说中了福音的核心：耶稣是死了，还是活着？如果耶稣仍在坟墓里，保罗的信仰就全落了空；如果他活着，一切都改变了。保罗早在写给哥林多教会的信中就说过：「若基督没有复活，我们所传的便是枉然，你们所信的也是枉然。」（林前15:14）

**4. 「我心里作难」。** 非斯都说：「这些事当怎样究问，我心里作难。」（25:20）一位罗马巡抚，懂得罗马法律，却不知道该怎样处理「一个人是否从死里复活」这样的问题。世上的权势和智慧，碰到复活这件事，正是这样束手无策。

**5. 「我自己也愿听」。** 「亚基帕对非斯都说：『我自己也愿听这人辩论。』」（25:22）这让人想起他的家族：他的曾祖父大希律想见初生的耶稣，为要杀他（太2:8，13，16）；他的叔祖父希律安提帕想见耶稣，为要看他行神迹（路23:8）。现在，亚基帕想听保罗讲论这位耶稣。

> 要点：非斯都以为这只是一场宗教争论，却无意中说出了问题的核心：那位名叫耶稣的人，是死了，还是活着？整个福音，都取决于这个问题的答案。

## 深读 25:23-27 大张威势

**1. 两种荣耀。** 「第二天，亚基帕和百妮基大张威势而来，同着众千夫长和城里的尊贵人进了公厅。非斯都吩咐一声，就有人将保罗带进来。」（25:23）一边是亚基帕王、百妮基、众千夫长和城中的权贵，穿着华服，前呼后拥；另一边是一个戴着锁链的囚犯。在人看来，荣耀全在那一边。可是亚基帕死后（约在一世纪末），他的王国并入了罗马行省；非斯都的名字，只在史书里留下一笔；保罗的书信，今天却仍在全世界被诵读。这不是说保罗最后比他们更出名，而是「惟有我们神的话必永远立定」（赛40:8）。

**2. 非斯都的难处。** 非斯都承认：「我查明他没有犯什么该死的罪」，可是保罗已经上告于皇帝，他必须写一份报告，「论到这人，我没有确实的事可以奏明主上」（25:25-26）。他希望亚基帕能帮他找到一些可以写的罪状，因为「解送囚犯，不指明他的罪案是不合理的」（25:27）。

**3. 一再被宣告无罪。** 这已经不是第一次有官员承认保罗无罪了：哥林多的方伯迦流不肯审理（18:14-15），千夫长吕西亚查不出该死该绑的罪（23:29），如今巡抚非斯都也这样说（25:25）；下一章亚基帕王也要说同样的话（26:31-32）。路加写这些，或许也是为了让罗马世界的读者知道：基督徒不是叛乱分子。

> 要点：在世人眼中，荣耀属于大张威势的王和巡抚；可是在神的计划里，这一幕真正的中心，是那个戴锁链的囚犯所传的、已经复活的耶稣，神正藉着这一切成就他自己的话。

## 默想

**默想：** 非斯都用一句话总结了整个案子：「一个人名叫耶稣，是已经死了，保罗却说他是活着的。」对我来说，耶稣的复活是我偶尔才想起的一条教义，还是我每天站立的根基？如果耶稣仍在坟墓里，我今天的生活会有什么不同？正因为他活着，我的罪真的被赦免了，我的盼望也不会落空（林前15:17-20）。非斯都「要讨犹太人的喜欢」，就想把一个无罪的人交出去。我在什么时候最容易这样做：开会时同事替我的错背了锅，我却没有出声；家里起了争执，我跟着说话最响的那一方，让最弱的那个人受委屈？耶稣也曾被一位巡抚交出去：彼拉多「要叫众人喜悦」，就把他交给人钉十字架（可15:15）。他没有上告，而是甘心「被交给人，是为我们的过犯」（罗4:25），也包括我每一次拿别人的公道去换有权势者的好脸色。神既然已经在他里面接纳了我，我就不必再去讨那份脸色。最后，亚基帕和百妮基「大张威势而来」，保罗却戴着锁链被带进来。老实说，我心里羡慕的是哪一边？刷到老同学升职、换房的消息时，我心里涌起的是什么？那位为我甘愿被锁、被钉的主，如今已经得着荣耀；「基督是我们的生命，他显现的时候，你们也要与他一同显现在荣耀里」（西3:4）。这份荣耀，我能不能今天就先为它欢喜？

## The Shape of the Chapter

In chapter 25 a new governor, Festus, takes office and Paul's case starts again. **The request from Jerusalem** (vv. 1–5): the chief priests and Jewish leaders ask Festus to bring Paul to Jerusalem, planning to ambush him on the way. **Trial at Caesarea** (vv. 6–12): the Jews can't prove their charges, but Festus, wanting to please them, asks whether Paul will go to Jerusalem for trial; Paul says, "I appeal to Caesar." **King Agrippa's visit** (vv. 13–22): Festus explains the case to King Agrippa, saying the dispute is "about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." **The audience hall** (vv. 23–27): Agrippa and Bernice arrive "with great pomp," Paul is brought in, and Festus admits he has found nothing definite to write to the emperor.

Two years have passed and there is a new governor, but the Jewish leaders' resolve to kill Paul hasn't changed. Nor has God's plan: the Lord had said Paul "must testify also in Rome" (23:11). This chapter moves that promise forward.

## Going Deeper: 25:1–12 Appealing to Caesar

**1. A plot two years on.** Just three days after arriving, Festus went up to Jerusalem. "And the chief priests and the principal men of the Jews laid out their case against Paul, and they urged him, asking as a favor against Paul that he summon him to Jerusalem—because they were planning an ambush to kill him on the way" (25:2–3). It was the same kind of plan as the forty men two years earlier (23:12–15). Time had not cooled their hatred.

**2. Charges they could not prove.** At Caesarea, "the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem stood around him, bringing many and serious charges against him that they could not prove" (25:7). Paul argued, "Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar have I committed any offense" (25:8). The three answer the three kinds of charge against him: religious, temple, political.

**3. Festus compromises.** "But Festus, wishing to do the Jews a favor, said to Paul, 'Do you wish to go up to Jerusalem and there be tried on these charges before me?'" (25:9). The new governor wanted good relations with the local leaders. Paul knew well that going to Jerusalem meant walking into an ambush.

**4. "I do not seek to escape death."** "But Paul said, 'I am standing before Caesar's tribunal, where I ought to be tried. To the Jews I have done no wrong, as you yourselves know very well. If then I am a wrongdoer and have committed anything for which I deserve to die, I do not seek to escape death. But if there is nothing to their charges against me, no one can give me up to them. I appeal to Caesar'" (25:10–11). Paul wasn't trying to escape judgment; if he had truly committed a capital crime, he said, he would accept death. What he refused was to be handed over unjustly to his enemies when he was innocent.

**5. To Caesar you shall go.** "Then Festus, when he had conferred with his council, answered, 'To Caesar you have appealed; to Caesar you shall go'" (25:12). Roman citizens had the right to appeal to the emperor; after conferring with his council, Festus accepted the appeal. The emperor then was Nero, still relatively moderate in his early years; only later did he brutally persecute Christians. Paul's appeal was not only self-protection; it was a step along God's promise: the Lord had said he would testify in Rome, and now the Roman government itself would escort him there.

> The point: Paul did not run from just judgment, but he would not accept unjust betrayal. He lawfully appealed to Caesar, and God used Roman law to carry him to the Rome the Lord had promised.

## Going Deeper: 25:13–22 "A Certain Jesus"

**1. Agrippa and Bernice.** "Now when some days had passed, Agrippa the king and Bernice arrived at Caesarea and greeted Festus" (25:13). This was Herod Agrippa II, son of the Herod eaten by worms in chapter 12. Bernice was his sister, and the sister of Drusilla (24:24). Agrippa knew Jewish law and customs well, and the Romans had given him the right to appoint the high priest and charge of the temple treasury.

**2. Roman custom.** Festus told Agrippa that the chief priests and elders had asked him to condemn Paul, but he had answered "that it was not the custom of the Romans to give up anyone before the accused met the accusers face to face and had opportunity to make his defense concerning the charge laid against him" (25:16). Luke shows Roman law here as fairer than the Jewish leaders.

**3. The heart of the matter.** Festus said he had expected some serious crime, but instead "they had certain points of dispute with him about their own religion and about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19). It is an outsider's summary of the whole affair, and it unwittingly names the heart of the gospel: is Jesus dead, or alive? If Jesus is still in the tomb, Paul's faith is a mistake; if he is alive, everything changes. Paul had already written, "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Cor 15:14).

**4. "Being at a loss."** Festus said, "Being at a loss how to investigate these questions..." (25:20). A Roman governor who knew Roman law did not know how to handle a question like whether a man had risen from the dead. That is exactly the problem the gospel poses to the world's power and wisdom.

**5. "I would like to hear the man myself."** "Then Agrippa said to Festus, 'I would like to hear the man myself'" (25:22). It recalls his family: his great-grandfather Herod the Great wanted to find the newborn Jesus in order to kill him (Matt 2:8, 13, 16); his great-uncle Herod Antipas wanted to see Jesus in hope of a miracle (Luke 23:8). Now Agrippa wanted to hear Paul speak about this Jesus.

> The point: Festus thought this was merely a religious dispute, but unwittingly named its heart: is the man called Jesus dead or alive? The whole gospel turns on the answer.

## Going Deeper: 25:23–27 Great Pomp

**1. Two kinds of glory.** "So on the next day Agrippa and Bernice came with great pomp, and they entered the audience hall with the military tribunes and the prominent men of the city. Then, at the command of Festus, Paul was brought in" (25:23). On one side, the king, Bernice, officers and the city's elite, in splendid dress with their retinues; on the other, a prisoner in chains. To human eyes, all the glory was on one side. Yet by the end of the century, after Agrippa's death, his kingdom had been absorbed into a Roman province and Festus survived only as a footnote in history; Paul's letters are still read aloud all over the world today. This is not that Paul ended up more famous than they were, but that "the word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:8).

**2. Festus's problem.** Festus admitted, "I found that he had done nothing deserving death," yet since Paul had appealed to the emperor he had to send a report: "I have nothing definite to write to my lord about him" (25:25–26). He hoped Agrippa could help him find something to write, for "it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to indicate the charges against him" (25:27).

**3. Declared innocent again and again.** This is not the first time an official has found Paul innocent: the proconsul Gallio refused to hear the case (18:14–15), the tribune Lysias found nothing deserving death or imprisonment (23:29), and now Governor Festus says the same (25:25); in the next chapter King Agrippa will agree (26:31–32). Luke may record this partly so that readers in the Roman world would know that Christians were not rebels.

> The point: In the world's eyes, glory belonged to the king and governor arriving with great pomp; but in God's plan the real center of the scene was the risen Jesus whom the chained prisoner proclaimed, and God was keeping his own word through it all.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Festus summed up the whole case in one sentence: "a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." For me, is Jesus' resurrection a doctrine I remember now and then, or the ground I stand on every day? If Jesus were still in the tomb, how would my life today be different? Because he lives, my sins are truly forgiven and my hope will not be put to shame (1 Cor 15:17–20). Festus, "wishing to do the Jews a favor," was ready to hand over an innocent man. Where am I most tempted to do the same: staying quiet in a meeting while a colleague takes the blame for my mistake, or siding with the loudest voice in a family quarrel and letting the weakest person bear it? Jesus too was handed over by a governor: Pilate, "wishing to satisfy the crowd," delivered him to be crucified (Mark 15:15). He did not appeal; he let himself be "delivered up for our trespasses" (Rom 4:25), including every time I have traded someone else's fair treatment for a powerful person's approval. Since God has already accepted me in him, I no longer need to buy that approval. Finally, Agrippa and Bernice came "with great pomp," and Paul was brought in in chains. Honestly, which side do I envy? What stirs in me when I scroll past an old classmate's promotion or new home? The Lord who let himself be bound and crucified for me now reigns in glory, and "when Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory" (Col 3:4). Can I begin to rejoice in that glory today?
