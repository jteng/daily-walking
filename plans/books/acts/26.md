## 本章脉络

第二十六章是保罗在亚基帕王面前的申辩，也是使徒行传第三次记述保罗归主的经过，而且是最详细的一次。**开场**（1-3节）：亚基帕熟悉犹太人的规矩，保罗能在他面前分诉，觉得是万幸。**从前的我**（4-11节）：他从小是最严紧的法利赛人，曾经逼迫圣徒，「他们被杀，我也出名定案」。**大马士革路上的呼召**（12-18节）：主不但拦住他，更差派他到外邦人那里去，「要叫他们的眼睛得开，从黑暗中归向光明」。**顺服与传讲**（19-23节）：他「没有违背那从天上来的异象」。**回应**（24-32节）：非斯都说他癫狂了，亚基帕说「你想少微一劝，便叫我作基督徒啊」，保罗却说，他愿意所有听他的人都像他一样，「只是不要像我有这些锁链」。

和第二十二章相比，这一次保罗不只讲自己怎样被主拦住，更着重讲他所领受的使命。他与其说是为自己辩护，不如说是在向这位王传福音。

## 深读 26:1-11 盼望与逼迫

**1. 实为万幸。** 「亚基帕王啊，犹太人所告我的一切事，今日得在你面前分诉，实为万幸；更可幸的，是你熟悉犹太人的规矩和他们的辩论。」（26:2-3）保罗的开场是真诚的：亚基帕确实了解犹太人的信仰，能明白保罗所说的。

**2. 因这指望受审。** 「现在我站在这里受审，是因为指望神向我们祖宗所应许的；这应许，我们十二个支派，昼夜切切地事奉神，都指望得着。」（26:6-7）保罗再次强调，他所信的并没有背叛以色列的信仰，反倒正是以色列盼望的应验。

**3. 「神叫死人复活」。** 「神叫死人复活，你们为什么看作不可信的呢？」（26:8）这是整篇申辩的核心问题。既然神是创造天地的神，叫死人复活又有什么不可信的呢？

**4. 出名定案。** 保罗毫不隐瞒自己的过去：「从前我自己以为应当多方攻击拿撒勒人耶稣的名」；他把许多圣徒囚在监里，「他们被杀，我也出名定案」；在各会堂里「屡次用刑强逼他们说亵渎的话，又分外恼恨他们」（26:9-11）。「出名定案」原文直译是「投下我的石子」，就是表示赞成处死。这可能指正式的投票，也可能只是比喻，表示他完全赞同（参8:1；22:20）；无论是哪一种，保罗都没有替自己开脱；三次讲述中，这一次把他从前的罪讲得最重。

## 深读 26:12-18 「你用脚踢刺是难的」

**1. 比日头还亮。** 「王啊，我在路上，晌午的时候，看见从天发光，比日头还亮，四面照着我并与我同行的人。」（26:13）

**2. 踢刺的比喻。** 主用希伯来话对他说：「扫罗！扫罗！为什么逼迫我？你用脚踢刺是难的！」（26:14）「刺」是赶牛用的尖棍；牛若用脚踢它，只会伤了自己。这是当时希腊人常用的俗语，三次讲述中只有这一次记下。有人认为，这暗示扫罗在路上遇见主以前，良心已经受了刺激（例如在司提反殉道时）；但许多解经家认为，这俗语的重点只是：抵挡神，只会伤害自己。保罗自己也说，他从前是「不信不明白的时候而做的」（提前1:13；参腓3:6）。无论哪一种，重点都是：扫罗所抵挡的，其实是主自己。

**3. 作执事，作见证。** 「你起来站着，我特意向你显现，要派你作执事，作见证，将你所看见的事和我将要指示你的事证明出来。」（26:16）这里的「执事」不是教会的职分，而是「仆人」的意思。在第九章和第二十二章，这使命是藉着亚拿尼亚传达的（9:15；22:14-15）；在这里，保罗在王面前，把它简要地说成主亲口对他说的话。这并不矛盾：使命的源头始终是主自己，亚拿尼亚只是传话的人（参加1:1，12）。「起来站着」令人想起神呼召以西结的话（结2:1）：保罗蒙召，正像旧约的先知一样。

**4. 从黑暗中归向光明。** 「我差你到他们那里去，要叫他们的眼睛得开，从黑暗中归向光明，从撒但权下归向神；又因信我，得蒙赦罪，和一切成圣的人同得基业。」（26:18）这是新约中对福音果效最完整的描述之一：眼睛得开（明白真理）、从黑暗归向光明、从撒但权下归向神（得释放）、得蒙赦罪、同得基业。这一切都是「因信我」而得的，就是因信耶稣。这也呼应以赛亚书中耶和华的仆人「开瞎子的眼，领被囚的出牢狱」的使命（赛42:6-7）。这位仆人就是基督；26:23说，是复活的基督自己「把光明的道传给百姓和外邦人」，保罗是蒙他差遣，在这工作上有分（参13:47）。曾经瞎了眼的扫罗，被差去开别人的眼睛。

> 要点：主拦住保罗，不只是为了救他，也是为了差他。他被差去开人的眼睛，使人从黑暗归向光明，从撒但权下归向神，因信耶稣得蒙赦罪。

## 深读 26:19-23 没有违背那异象

**1. 顺服。** 「亚基帕王啊，我故此没有违背那从天上来的异象。」（26:19）从大马士革到这一天，大约已经过了二十五年。保罗经历了鞭打、石头、船难、监禁，却一直没有违背那一次所领受的呼召。

**2. 悔改与行为。** 他到处劝勉人「应当悔改归向神，行事与悔改的心相称」（26:20）。真悔改会在生活中结出果子（参路3:8）。这不是说人靠行为得救，而是说真正归向神的人，生命一定会改变。悔改本身也是神所赐的（11:18）；悔改不只是改过自新，而是归向神。

**3. 蒙神的帮助。** 「然而我蒙神的帮助，直到今日还站得住，对着尊贵、卑贱、老幼作见证。」（26:22）保罗把他能站到今天，归功于神的帮助。他所讲的，「并不外乎众先知和摩西所说将来必成的事，就是基督必须受害，并且因从死里复活，要首先把光明的道传给百姓和外邦人」（26:22-23）。

## 深读 26:24-32 「你癫狂了吧」

**1. 非斯都的反应。** 「保罗这样分诉，非斯都大声说：『保罗，你癫狂了吧。你的学问太大，反叫你癫狂了！』」（26:24）在一位罗马官员听来，一个人从死里复活、又向外邦人传光明，简直是疯话。保罗早在写给哥林多教会的信中就说过，他所传钉十字架的基督「在外邦人为愚拙」（林前1:23）。

**2. 「真实明白话」。** 「保罗说：『非斯都大人，我不是癫狂，我说的乃是真实明白话。』」（26:25）保罗没有生气，只是平静地说：这是真实的，也是合理的。他又说：「这些事……都不是在背地里做的。」（26:26）耶稣的死和复活，是在耶路撒冷公开发生的历史事件，不是哪个秘密教派私下流传的说法。

**3. 「你信先知吗？」** 保罗转向亚基帕：「亚基帕王啊，你信先知吗？我知道你是信的。」（26:27）这一问使亚基帕进退两难：若说不信，就会失去犹太人的支持；若说信，就得承认先知所预言的，正是保罗所讲的耶稣。

**4. 「少微一劝」。** 「亚基帕对保罗说：『你想少微一劝，便叫我作基督徒啊！』」（26:28）这句话的语气不容易确定：可能是讽刺（「你想这么快就劝我作基督徒吗？」），也可能是半认真的回避。无论如何，亚基帕没有接受。「基督徒」这个名字，在新约里只出现三次（11:26；26:28；彼前4:16），这里是从一位君王口中说出来的。

**5. 「只是不要像我有这些锁链」。** 「保罗说：『无论是少劝是多劝，我向神所求的，不但你一个人，就是今天一切听我的，都要像我一样，只是不要像我有这些锁链。』」（26:29）一个戴着锁链的囚犯，对着王、王后、巡抚和满堂的权贵说：我愿你们都像我一样。他没有羡慕他们的荣华，反而为他们祷告，希望他们得着他所得着的基督。

**6. 再一次被宣告无罪。** 他们退到里面，彼此说：「这人并没有犯什么该死该绑的罪。」亚基帕对非斯都说：「这人若没有上告于凯撒，就可以释放了。」（26:31-32）可是正因为他上告了凯撒，他才能往罗马去，完成主所说的话。神甚至藉着人的上诉程序，成就他的应许（23:11）。

> 要点：在世人看来，保罗是疯子，是囚犯；在保罗眼中，那些坐在高位上的人才是真正需要被释放的。他心里所想的，不是怎样脱身，而是愿这些人都像他一样认识基督。

## 默想

**默想：** 保罗说：「我故此没有违背那从天上来的异象。」从大马士革路到亚基帕面前，大约过了二十五年，其间有鞭打、石头、船难和监禁，他却没有转身。托住他的，不是一份要靠自己保温的感动，而是那位亲自向他显现、对他说「我就是你所逼迫的耶稣」的主。我回头看自己信主的这些年，是不是常拿起初的热情来量自己，热情一淡，就以为信心也没了？保罗说：「我蒙神的帮助，直到今日还站得住。」我今天还站得住，靠的也不是当年那一刻有多火热，而是那位找到我、到今天还没有放手的主。非斯都大声说：「保罗，你癫狂了吧！」我在同事、同学或亲戚面前提到信仰时，是否也曾被人看作「迷信」「读书读傻了」？我不必赢下每一场争论；耶稣的复活「不是在背地里做的」，真理不靠我的口才站立，所以我可以不生气、不退缩，平静地说这是「真实明白话」。最后，戴着锁链的保罗对满堂权贵说，他向神所求的，是众人「都要像我一样，只是不要像我有这些锁链」。他敢这样说，是因为他知道自己得着了什么：眼睛得开，从黑暗归向光明，从撒但权下归向神，罪得赦免，与圣徒同得基业（26:18）。我的信仰在我自己眼中，是这样的福气，还是一副担子？如果连我自己都过得像在服刑，我又怎会盼望别人「像我一样」？今天，我能不能从这几样恩典里挑出一样，慢慢地为它感谢主？

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 26 is Paul's defense before King Agrippa, and the third and fullest account in Acts of his conversion. **Opening** (vv. 1–3): Paul counts himself fortunate to make his case before Agrippa, who knows Jewish customs. **Who I was** (vv. 4–11): from his youth a Pharisee of the strictest party, he persecuted the saints, and "when they were put to death I cast my vote against them." **The call on the Damascus road** (vv. 12–18): the Lord not only stopped him but sent him to the Gentiles, "to open their eyes, so that they may turn from darkness to light." **Obedience and preaching** (vv. 19–23): he "was not disobedient to the heavenly vision." **Responses** (vv. 24–32): Festus says he is out of his mind; Agrippa says, "In a short time would you persuade me to be a Christian?"; and Paul says he wishes everyone listening might become as he is, "except for these chains."

Compared with chapter 22, this telling is less about how one man was stopped and more about the commission he received. This is less a defense than a gospel appeal to the king.

## Going Deeper: 26:1–11 Hope and Persecution

**1. Fortunate.** "I consider myself fortunate that it is before you, King Agrippa, I am going to make my defense today against all the accusations of the Jews, especially because you are familiar with all the customs and controversies of the Jews" (26:2–3). Paul's opening is sincere: Agrippa really did understand the Jewish faith and could follow what Paul would say.

**2. On trial for this hope.** "And now I stand here on trial because of my hope in the promise made by God to our fathers, to which our twelve tribes hope to attain, as they earnestly worship night and day" (26:6–7). Paul stresses again that what he believes is not a betrayal of Israel's faith but the fulfillment of Israel's hope.

**3. "That God raises the dead."** "Why is it thought incredible by any of you that God raises the dead?" (26:8). This is the central question of the whole defense. If God is the God who made heaven and earth, what is incredible about his raising the dead?

**4. Casting his vote.** Paul hides nothing of his past: "I myself was convinced that I ought to do many things in opposing the name of Jesus of Nazareth"; he locked up many saints, "and when they were put to death I cast my vote against them"; "I punished them often in all the synagogues and tried to make them blaspheme, and in raging fury against them I persecuted them" (26:9–11). "Cast my vote" is literally "cast my pebble," an expression for approving a death sentence. It may mean a formal vote, or simply wholehearted approval (cf. 8:1; 22:20); either way, Paul does not soften his guilt. This telling paints his former sin most darkly of all.

## Going Deeper: 26:12–18 "Hard to Kick Against the Goads"

**1. Brighter than the sun.** "At midday, O king, I saw on the way a light from heaven, brighter than the sun, that shone around me and those who journeyed with me" (26:13).

**2. The goads.** The Lord spoke to him in Hebrew: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (26:14). A goad was a pointed stick for driving oxen; an ox that kicked against it only hurt itself. It was a common Greek proverb for the futility of resisting a god. The saying appears only in this telling. Some take it to hint that Saul's conscience had already been pricked before Damascus (perhaps at Stephen's death); but many commentators read the proverb simply as the futility of resisting God, which harms only the resister, and Paul himself says he acted "ignorantly in unbelief" (1 Tim 1:13; cf. Phil 3:6). Either way, the point is that Saul was in fact fighting the Lord himself.

**3. Servant and witness.** "But rise and stand upon your feet, for I have appeared to you for this purpose, to appoint you as a servant and witness to the things in which you have seen me and to those in which I will appear to you" (26:16). In chapters 9 and 22 the commission came through Ananias (9:15; 22:14–15); here, before the king, Paul telescopes it into the Lord's direct words. There is no contradiction: the source was always the Lord himself, and Ananias was his messenger (cf. Gal 1:1, 12). "Stand upon your feet" recalls God's call to Ezekiel (Ezek 2:1): Paul is commissioned like an Old Testament prophet.

**4. From darkness to light.** "To open their eyes, so that they may turn from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins and a place among those who are sanctified by faith in me" (26:18). This is one of the New Testament's fullest descriptions of what the gospel does: eyes opened (understanding the truth), turning from darkness to light, from Satan's power to God (liberation), forgiveness of sins, a share in the inheritance. All of it is "by faith in me," faith in Jesus. It echoes the mission of the Servant of the LORD in Isaiah, "to open the eyes that are blind, to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon" (Isa 42:6–7). That Servant is Christ; 26:23 says the risen Christ himself "would proclaim light," and Paul shares in that mission through him (cf. 13:47). Saul, once blinded, was sent to open others' eyes.

> The point: The Lord stopped Paul not only to save him but to send him. He was sent to open eyes, so that people would turn from darkness to light and from Satan's power to God, and receive forgiveness through faith in Jesus.

## Going Deeper: 26:19–23 Not Disobedient to the Vision

**1. Obedience.** "Therefore, O King Agrippa, I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision" (26:19). About twenty-five years had passed since Damascus. Paul had been flogged, stoned, shipwrecked and imprisoned, yet had never turned from the call he received that day.

**2. Repentance and deeds.** Everywhere he declared "that they should repent and turn to God, performing deeds in keeping with their repentance" (26:20). True repentance bears fruit in life (cf. Luke 3:8). This doesn't mean people are saved by deeds, but that those who truly turn to God will surely be changed. Repentance itself is God's gift (11:18).

**3. Help from God.** "To this day I have had the help that comes from God, and so I stand here testifying both to small and great" (26:22). Paul credits his standing to this day to God's help. What he preached was "nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would come to pass: that the Christ must suffer and that, by being the first to rise from the dead, he would proclaim light both to our people and to the Gentiles" (26:22–23).

## Going Deeper: 26:24–32 "You Are Out of Your Mind"

**1. Festus's reaction.** "And as he was saying these things in his defense, Festus said with a loud voice, 'Paul, you are out of your mind; your great learning is driving you out of your mind'" (26:24). To a Roman official, a man rising from the dead and bringing light to the Gentiles sounded like madness. Paul had already written that Christ crucified is "folly to Gentiles" (1 Cor 1:23).

**2. "True and rational words."** "But Paul said, 'I am not out of my mind, most excellent Festus, but I am speaking true and rational words'" (26:25). Paul didn't lose his temper; he calmly said this was both true and reasonable. He added, "this has not been done in a corner" (26:26). Jesus' death and resurrection were public historical events in Jerusalem, not the private lore of a secret cult.

**3. "Do you believe the prophets?"** Paul turned to Agrippa: "King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe" (26:27). The question put Agrippa in a bind: say no, and lose Jewish support; say yes, and admit that what the prophets foretold is the Jesus Paul preached.

**4. "In a short time."** "And Agrippa said to Paul, 'In a short time would you persuade me to be a Christian?'" (26:28). His tone is hard to pin down: perhaps sarcastic ("Do you think you can make me a Christian so quickly?"), perhaps a half-serious dodge. Either way, Agrippa did not accept. The name "Christian" appears only three times in the New Testament (11:26; 26:28; 1 Pet 4:16), and here it comes from a king's mouth.

**5. "Except for these chains."** "And Paul said, 'Whether short or long, I would to God that not only you but also all who hear me this day might become such as I am—except for these chains'" (26:29). A prisoner in chains told a king, a queen, a governor and a hall full of dignitaries: I wish you were all like me. He didn't envy their splendor; he prayed that they would receive the Christ he had received.

**6. Declared innocent again.** When they withdrew, they said to one another, "This man is doing nothing to deserve death or imprisonment." And Agrippa said to Festus, "This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar" (26:31–32). Yet precisely because he had appealed to Caesar, he would go to Rome, as the Lord had said. God was using even a legal procedure to keep his promise (23:11).

> The point: To the world, Paul was a madman and a prisoner; in Paul's eyes, those in the seats of power were the ones who needed to be set free. What filled his mind was not how to get out, but the wish that they might all come to know Christ as he did.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Paul said, "I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision." Some twenty-five years lay between the Damascus road and Agrippa's hall, years of floggings, stones, shipwreck and prison, and he never turned back. What held him was not a feeling he had to keep warm by himself but the Lord who appeared to him and said, "I am Jesus whom you are persecuting." Looking back over my years of faith, do I measure myself by how fervent I was at the start, and conclude that when the fervor fades, faith must have faded too? Paul said, "To this day I have had the help that comes from God." If I am still standing today, it is not because of how bright that first moment was, but because the Lord who found me has not let go. Festus shouted, "Paul, you are out of your mind!" When I mention my faith to coworkers, classmates or relatives, have I been taken for "superstitious" or someone who "studied too much"? I don't have to win every argument: Jesus' resurrection "has not been done in a corner," and the truth doesn't stand on my eloquence, so I can neither bristle nor back down, but calmly say these are "true and rational words." Finally, Paul in chains told a hall of dignitaries, "I would to God that... all who hear me this day might become such as I am—except for these chains." He could say it because he knew what he had been given: eyes opened, darkness turned to light, freedom from Satan's power, sins forgiven, a place among those sanctified by faith (26:18). In my own eyes, is my faith that kind of blessing, or a burden? If I live it like a sentence to be served, how could I wish anyone to "become such as I am"? Today, can I pick one of these gifts and slowly thank the Lord for it?
