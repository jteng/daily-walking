## 本章脉络

第二十八章是使徒行传的最后一章。**马耳他**（1-10节）：船难以后，土人生火接待他们；保罗被毒蛇咬了，却没有受伤；他为岛长部百流的父亲祷告，治好了他，岛上的病人也都得了医治。**往罗马去**（11-16节）：三个月以后，他们坐船到了意大利，罗马的弟兄们远道出来迎接，保罗「就感谢神，放心壮胆」。**对罗马的犹太人**（17-29节）：保罗请犹太人的首领来，从早到晚引摩西和先知的书讲论耶稣，有信的，有不信的；保罗引用以赛亚书，宣告「神这救恩，如今传给外邦人」。**结尾**（30-31节）：保罗在自己所租的房子里住了两年，「放胆传讲神国的道，将主耶稣基督的事教导人，并没有人禁止」。

使徒行传以耶稣吩咐门徒「在耶路撒冷、犹太全地，和撒马利亚，直到地极，作我的见证」开始（1:8），以保罗在罗马城中传道结束。罗马是当时世界的中心，从这里，道路通往天下各处。福音已经从耶路撒冷到了帝国的首都；而故事并没有真正结束。

## 深读 28:1-10 马耳他

**1. 非常的情分。** 「我们既已得救，才知道那岛名叫马耳他。土人看待我们，有非常的情分；因为当时下雨，天气又冷，就生火接待我们众人。」（28:1-2）「土人」原文是 *barbaroi*，希腊人用来称呼不说希腊话的人（马耳他人说的是腓尼基语系的布匿语），并没有「野蛮」的意思。路加特别记下这些外邦人的仁慈：神的普遍恩典，在不认识他的人身上也显明出来。

**2. 拾柴的使徒。** 「那时，保罗拾起一捆柴，放在火上。」（28:3）这位刚刚在船上给全船的人带来盼望、在君王面前作见证的使徒，在岸上和大家一起拾柴生火。他没有因为自己的身份而等着别人服事。

**3. 从「凶手」到「神」。** 一条毒蛇咬住保罗的手。土人说：「这人必是个凶手，虽然从海里救上来，天理还不容他活着。」（28:4）「天理」原文是 *Dikē*（公义），在希腊人的观念里是一位惩罚罪人的女神；路加用这个词，表达岛民相信神明必报应罪人的想法。可是保罗「把那毒蛇甩在火里，并没有受伤」，他们又转念说：「他是个神。」（28:5-6）人对属灵的事的判断，常常一下子从一个极端转到另一个极端。路加没有记载保罗怎样回应，但他在路司得已经表明了态度（14:14-15）。耶稣差遣门徒时曾说：「我已经给你们权柄可以践踏蛇和蝎子」（路10:19）。这是神在宣教路上的保守（参27:24，保罗「必定站在凯撒面前」），不是叫人去试探神。

**4. 祷告和按手。** 岛长部百流接待他们三天。「当时，部百流的父亲患热病和痢疾躺着。保罗进去，为他祷告，按手在他身上，治好了他。」（28:8）作为医生的路加（西4:14），记下了病情。请注意保罗先「祷告」：医治的是神，不是保罗。岛上其余的病人也来，得了医治（28:9）。那些曾经接待他们的人，现在也领受了福气。

## 深读 28:11-16 「感谢神，放心壮胆」

**1. 「宙斯双子」的船。** 「过了三个月，我们上了亚历山大的船往前行；这船以『宙斯双子』为记。」（28:11）「宙斯双子」（就是卡斯托耳和波吕克斯）是航海者的守护神。路加记下这个细节，或许带着一点讽刺：一路保守他们渡过风暴、又要带保罗到罗马的，不是船头的神像，而是保罗「所属所事奉的神」（27:23）。

**2. 「这样，我们来到罗马」。** 在部丢利，「遇见弟兄们，请我们与他们同住了七天。这样，我们来到罗马」（28:14）。早在保罗到达以前，罗马已经有信徒了（参罗1:7-8）。福音走在使徒的前面。

**3. 弟兄们的迎接。** 「那里的弟兄们一听见我们的信息就出来，到亚比乌市和三馆地方迎接我们。保罗见了他们，就感谢神，放心壮胆。」（28:15）亚比乌市离罗马约七十公里，三馆约五十公里。这些信徒走了一两天的路，来迎接一个被锁着的囚犯。保罗几年前写信给他们（罗马书），如今终于见了面。这位经历过鞭打、石头、船难的使徒，见到弟兄们，才「放心壮胆」：再坚强的人，也需要弟兄姊妹的爱。

**4. 另住一处。** 「进了罗马城，保罗蒙准和一个看守他的兵另住在一处。」（28:16）他仍是囚犯，被一个兵丁看守着，很可能用链子锁在一起（28:20），却可以住在自己的地方，接待来访的人。

> 要点：保罗以囚犯的身份到了罗马，可是神的应许正在成就：主说他「也必怎样在罗马为我作见证」（23:11），他到了；主说他「必定站在凯撒面前」（27:24），他正走在这条路上。而迎接他的，是一群早已在罗马信了主的弟兄。

## 深读 28:17-29 对罗马的犹太人

**1. 为以色列人所指望的。** 保罗到罗马三天，就请犹太人的首领来，说他没有做什么干犯本国百姓和祖宗规条的事，却「被锁绑，从耶路撒冷解在罗马人的手里」（28:17）；「我原为以色列人所指望的，被这链子捆锁」（28:20）。直到最后，保罗都坚持：福音不是背叛以色列，而是以色列盼望的应验。在使徒行传里，「以色列人所指望的」就是死人复活的盼望（23:6；26:6-8），而这盼望已经在被钉十字架又复活的耶稣身上应验了。

**2. 到处被毁谤的教门。** 犹太人的首领说，他们没有收到关于保罗的信，可是「这教门，我们晓得是到处被毁谤的」（28:22）。

**3. 从早到晚。** 「他们和保罗约定了日子，就有许多人到他的寓处来。保罗从早到晚，对他们讲论这事，证明神国的道，引摩西的律法和先知的书，以耶稣的事劝勉他们。」（28:23）这正是复活的主在以马忤斯路上所做的：「从摩西和众先知起，凡经上所指着自己的话都给他们讲解明白了。」（路24:27）

**4. 有信的，有不信的。** 「他所说的话，有信的，有不信的。」（28:24）在使徒行传中，这是一再出现的情景。福音从来不是自动被接受的。

**5. 以赛亚的话。** 保罗引用以赛亚书六章：「你们听是要听见，却不明白；看是要看见，却不晓得；因为这百姓油蒙了心，耳朵发沉，眼睛闭着。」（28:26-27；赛6:9-10）这段经文，耶稣也曾用来解释为什么许多人不明白他的比喻（路8:10），约翰也用它解释为什么许多人不信（约12:39-41）。这段话有两面。一面是人的责任：「眼睛闭着」是他们自己闭上的（28:27）；另一面是神的主权：在以赛亚书原文里，是神差先知去「使这百姓心蒙脂油」（赛6:10），约翰也说「他们所以不能信」，是因为「主叫他们瞎了眼，硬了心」（约12:39-40）。神使人刚硬，是对人一再拒绝的公义审判；人不信，责任仍在自己。保罗说这是圣灵藉先知所说的话（28:25）。但这不是最后的话：保罗在罗马书九至十一章说，以色列人「有几分是硬心的」，而且是暂时的，为要叫外邦人得救，也终要激动以色列人（罗11:7-11，25）。

**6. 传给外邦人。** 「所以你们当知道，神这救恩，如今传给外邦人，他们也必听受。」（28:28）这不是说神放弃了以色列（保罗在罗马书九至十一章说得很清楚，神没有弃绝他的百姓），而是说福音的门，现在向万民大大敞开。（有些抄本有第29节，记载犹太人彼此争论着走了；较早的抄本没有这一节。）

> 要点：保罗直到最后都向犹太人传福音，引用他们自己的圣经，讲论他们所盼望的弥赛亚。有人信，有人不信；而神的救恩，继续向外邦人传开。

## 深读 28:30-31 「并没有人禁止」

**1. 两年。** 「保罗在自己所租的房子里住了足足两年。凡来见他的人，他全都接待……」（28:30）许多学者认为，以弗所书、腓立比书、歌罗西书和腓利门书，很可能就是在这段时间写的（也有人认为部分写于凯撒利亚或以弗所）。保罗在腓立比书说：「我所遭遇的事更是叫福音兴旺，以致我受的捆锁在御营全军和其余的人中，已经显明是为基督的缘故。」（腓1:12-13）

**2. 放胆传讲。** 「放胆传讲神国的道，将主耶稣基督的事教导人，并没有人禁止。」（28:31）「神国」是耶稣复活后四十天讲论的主题（1:3），也是使徒行传最后一句话的主题。「并没有人禁止」是全书原文的最后一个字（*akōlytōs*）：保罗被锁着，神的道却没有被锁着。保罗后来写道：「我……甚至被捆绑，像犯人一样。然而神的道却不被捆绑。」（提后2:9）

**3. 没有结局的结尾。** 路加没有告诉我们保罗受审的结果，也没有记载他的死。按教会的传统，保罗后来在尼禄的逼迫中殉道；许多人认为他曾获释，后来再次下监，才殉道。为什么路加停在这里？有人认为，路加写作时保罗的案件还没有结果（参导读的成书年代）；也有人认为这是有意的安排：他要讲的从来不是保罗的一生，而是「神的道」怎样从耶路撒冷传开。两种看法并不互相排斥。使徒行传这卷圣经已经写完了，但它所记的故事还在继续：复活的主仍藉着圣灵使他的道传开，也把每一个领受、传扬福音的人写进这个故事里。

> 要点：使徒行传的最后一句话，不是保罗的结局，而是神的道「并没有人禁止」。传道的人会被捆锁、会死去，神的道却一直向前，直到今天，直到地极。

## 默想

**默想：** 刚刚在风暴中带给全船盼望、在君王面前作见证的保罗，在马耳他的岸上「拾起一捆柴」。我是否觉得某些平凡、卑微的事「不是我该做的」，要等别人来服事我？那位在最后的晚餐上束起腰来为门徒洗脚的主，是保罗拾柴的榜样，也是我的。保罗经历了鞭打、石头和船难，可是见到罗马的弟兄们远道来迎接，他「就感谢神，放心壮胆」。我身边有没有一位疲惫的同工、一位刚经历风暴的弟兄姊妹？我能不能像那些罗马信徒一样，走一段路去「迎接」他：一次探望，一顿饭，一句问候？最后，使徒行传的最后一个字是「并没有人禁止」。保罗被锁着，神的道却没有被锁着。我是否因为自己的限制（年纪、健康、语言、处境）而觉得神不能用我？神的道从来不靠传道人的自由。使徒行传停在这里，因为故事还在继续；而我，也是这个故事的一部分。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 28 is the last chapter of Acts. **Malta** (vv. 1–10): after the shipwreck the islanders build a fire and welcome them; Paul is bitten by a viper but suffers no harm; he prays for the father of Publius, the chief man of the island, and heals him, and the rest of the sick on the island are cured. **On to Rome** (vv. 11–16): three months later they sail to Italy, and believers from Rome come out a long way to meet them; Paul "thanked God and took courage." **To the Jews in Rome** (vv. 17–29): Paul calls the Jewish leaders together and from morning till evening explains Jesus from Moses and the Prophets; some are convinced, others disbelieve; Paul quotes Isaiah and declares that "this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles." **The ending** (vv. 30–31): Paul lives two years in his own rented quarters, "proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance."

Acts begins with Jesus telling his disciples, "you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (1:8), and ends with Paul preaching in the city of Rome. Rome was the center of the known world, and from it roads led everywhere. The gospel has traveled from Jerusalem to the capital of the empire; and the story is not really over.

## Going Deeper: 28:1–10 Malta

**1. Unusual kindness.** "After we were brought safely through, we then learned that the island was called Malta. The native people showed us unusual kindness, for they kindled a fire and welcomed us all, because it had begun to rain and was cold" (28:1–2). "Native people" is literally "barbarians," meaning locals who didn't speak Greek (the Maltese spoke Punic, a Phoenician dialect); the word was not necessarily an insult. Luke takes care to record the kindness of these pagans: God's common grace is visible even in people who do not know him.

**2. The apostle gathering sticks.** "When Paul had gathered a bundle of sticks and put them on the fire..." (28:3). The apostle who had just given hope to everyone on the ship and borne witness before a king was on the beach gathering firewood with everyone else. He didn't wait for others to serve him because of who he was.

**3. From "murderer" to "god."** A viper fastened on Paul's hand. The islanders said, "No doubt this man is a murderer. Though he has escaped from the sea, Justice has not allowed him to live" (28:4). "Justice" (*Dikē*) was, in Greek thought, a goddess who avenges wrongdoing. Luke uses the word to express the islanders' belief that the gods pay back the guilty. But Paul "shook off the creature into the fire and suffered no harm," and they changed their minds and said "he was a god" (28:5–6). People's spiritual judgments often swing from one extreme to the other. Luke doesn't record Paul's response, but Paul had already made his stance plain at Lystra (14:14–15). When Jesus sent out his disciples he said, "I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions" (Luke 10:19). This is God's protection of his messenger on mission (cf. 27:24, Paul "must stand before Caesar"), not an invitation to put God to the test.

**4. Prayer and laying on of hands.** Publius, the chief man of the island, entertained them for three days. "It happened that the father of Publius lay sick with fever and dysentery. And Paul visited him and prayed, and putting his hands on him healed him" (28:8). Luke, the physician (Col 4:14), notes the illness. Notice that Paul first "prayed": the healer was God, not Paul. The rest of the sick on the island came and were cured (28:9). Those who had welcomed them now received blessing in turn.

## Going Deeper: 28:11–16 "He Thanked God and Took Courage"

**1. A ship of the twin gods.** "After three months we set sail in a ship that had wintered in the island, a ship of Alexandria, with the twin gods as a figurehead" (28:11). The twin gods, Castor and Pollux, were patrons of sailors. Luke may record this with a touch of irony: the One who had kept them through the storm and would bring Paul to Rome was not the twin gods but "the God to whom I belong and whom I worship" (27:23).

**2. "And so we came to Rome."** At Puteoli "we found brothers and were invited to stay with them for seven days. And so we came to Rome" (28:14). There were believers in Rome long before Paul arrived (cf. Rom 1:7–8). The gospel had gone ahead of the apostle.

**3. Brothers come to meet them.** "And the brothers there, when they heard about us, came as far as the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns to meet us. On seeing them, Paul thanked God and took courage" (28:15). The Forum of Appius was about forty-three miles from Rome, Three Taverns about thirty-three. These believers walked a day or two to meet a prisoner in chains. Paul had written to them a few years earlier (Romans), and at last they met face to face. The apostle who had survived floggings, stoning and shipwreck "took courage" on seeing them: even the strongest need the love of brothers and sisters.

**4. Lodging by himself.** "And when we came into Rome, Paul was allowed to stay by himself, with the soldier that guarded him" (28:16). He was still a prisoner, guarded by a soldier, very likely chained to him (28:20), but he could live in his own place and receive visitors.

> The point: Paul arrived in Rome as a prisoner, but God's promise was being fulfilled: the Lord had said he "must testify also in Rome" (23:11), and he was there; he "must stand before Caesar" (27:24), and he was on the way. And those who came to meet him were brothers who had believed in Rome long before.

## Going Deeper: 28:17–29 To the Jews in Rome

**1. Because of the hope of Israel.** Three days after arriving, Paul called the Jewish leaders and said that although he had done nothing against the people or the customs of the fathers, "I was delivered as a prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans" (28:17); "it is because of the hope of Israel that I am wearing this chain" (28:20). To the very end Paul insisted that the gospel is not a betrayal of Israel but the fulfillment of Israel's hope. In Acts, "the hope of Israel" is the hope of the resurrection (23:6; 26:6–8), and it has been fulfilled in the crucified and risen Jesus.

**2. A sect spoken against everywhere.** The Jewish leaders said they had received no letters about Paul, "but... with regard to this sect we know that everywhere it is spoken against" (28:22).

**3. From morning till evening.** "When they had appointed a day for him, they came to him at his lodging in greater numbers. From morning till evening he expounded to them, testifying to the kingdom of God and trying to convince them about Jesus both from the Law of Moses and from the Prophets" (28:23). It is exactly what the risen Lord did on the Emmaus road: "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27).

**4. Some convinced, some not.** "And some were convinced by what he said, but others disbelieved" (28:24). This scene recurs throughout Acts. The gospel is never received automatically.

**5. Isaiah's words.** Paul quoted Isaiah 6: "You will indeed hear but never understand, and you will indeed see but never perceive. For this people's heart has grown dull, and with their ears they can barely hear, and their eyes they have closed" (28:26–27; Isa 6:9–10). Jesus used this passage to explain why many didn't understand his parables (Luke 8:10), and John used it to explain why many didn't believe (John 12:39–41). This passage has two sides. On one side is human responsibility: "their eyes they have closed" (28:27). On the other is God's sovereignty: in Isaiah it is God who sends the prophet to "make the heart of this people dull" (Isa 6:10), and John says "they could not believe" because "he has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart" (John 12:39–40). God's hardening is a just judgment on persistent rejection, and the guilt of unbelief remains theirs. Paul calls this the word of the Holy Spirit (28:25). But it is not the last word. In Romans 9–11 Paul says Israel's hardening is "partial" and temporary, serving the salvation of the Gentiles and finally provoking Israel itself (Rom 11:7–11, 25).

**6. Sent to the Gentiles.** "Therefore let it be known to you that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen" (28:28). This doesn't mean God has given up on Israel (Paul makes clear in Romans 9–11 that God has not rejected his people), but that the gospel's door now stands wide open to all nations. (Some manuscripts include a verse 29, saying the Jews departed arguing among themselves; earlier manuscripts lack it.)

> The point: To the very end Paul preached the gospel to the Jews, from their own Scriptures, about the Messiah they hoped for. Some believed, some did not; and God's salvation kept going out to the Gentiles.

## Going Deeper: 28:30–31 "Without Hindrance"

**1. Two years.** "He lived there two whole years at his own expense, and welcomed all who came to him" (28:30). Many scholars think Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians and Philemon were very likely written in this period (others place some of them in Caesarea or Ephesus). In Philippians Paul wrote, "What has happened to me has really served to advance the gospel, so that it has become known throughout the whole imperial guard and to all the rest that my imprisonment is for Christ" (Phil 1:12–13).

**2. With all boldness.** "Proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance" (28:31). "The kingdom of God" was the theme of the risen Jesus' teaching for forty days (1:3), and it is the theme of Acts' last sentence. "Without hindrance" (*akōlytōs*) is the very last word of the book in Greek: Paul was chained, but the word of God was not. Paul later wrote, "I am suffering, bound with chains as a criminal. But the word of God is not bound!" (2 Tim 2:9).

**3. An ending without an end.** Luke doesn't tell us the outcome of Paul's trial or record his death. Church tradition holds that Paul was later martyred under Nero; many think he was first released, then imprisoned again before his death. Why does Luke stop here? Some think Luke wrote before Paul's case was decided (see the introduction on dating). Others see a deliberate design: his story was never Paul's life, but how "the word of God" spread from Jerusalem. The two views are not mutually exclusive. The book of Acts is complete, but the story it tells goes on: the risen Lord is still spreading his word by his Spirit, and he writes everyone who receives and passes on the gospel into that story.

> The point: The last word of Acts isn't Paul's fate but that God's word went out "without hindrance." Preachers are chained and die, but God's word keeps going, to this day and to the ends of the earth.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Paul, who had just brought hope to everyone on a ship in a storm and borne witness before a king, "gathered a bundle of sticks" on the beach at Malta. Do I think certain ordinary, humble tasks are "not my job," and wait for others to serve me? The Lord who wrapped a towel around his waist and washed his disciples' feet at the Last Supper is Paul's example for gathering sticks, and mine. Paul had endured floggings, stones and shipwreck, yet when the brothers from Rome came a long way to meet him, he "thanked God and took courage." Is there a weary co-worker near me, or a brother or sister just through a storm? Could I, like those Roman believers, go a little way to "meet" them: a visit, a meal, a word of greeting? Finally, the last word of Acts is "without hindrance." Paul was in chains, but the word of God was not. Do I think my limitations (age, health, language, circumstances) mean God can't use me? God's word has never depended on the preacher's freedom. Acts stops here because the story is still going on; and I am part of that story too.
