## 本章脉络

第四章是歌罗西书的结尾。第1节「你们作主人的，要公公平平地待仆人」属于3:18起的治家守则，上一章已经讲过。从第2节起，全章有三段。

- **祷告和言语**（2-6节）。要恒切祷告，警醒感恩，也为保罗祷告，求神给他开传道的门。对外人要用智慧，言语要常常带着和气，好像用盐调和。
- **两位送信的人**（7-9节）。推基古和阿尼西谋要把保罗的景况告诉他们，安慰他们的心。
- **问安和嘱咐**（10-18节）。六位同工问安，其中有犹太人，也有外邦人。保罗请他们问候老底嘉的弟兄和宁法家里的教会，嘱咐他们交换书信，又嘱咐亚基布尽他的职分。最后保罗亲笔问安：「你们要记念我的捆锁。愿恩惠常与你们同在！」

这一章看起来像一串名单，却让我们看见，前三章所讲的「基督是包括一切，又住在各人之内」（3:11），怎样落实在一个个真实的人身上：一个曾经半途离开宣教队伍的马可，一个从前「没有益处」的奴隶阿尼西谋，一位很可能是外邦人的医生，一位打开自己的家让教会聚会的姊妹。保罗被捆锁，福音却藉着这些人继续传开。

## 深读 4:2-4 恒切祷告，求神开门

**1. 恒切祷告。** 「你们要恒切祷告，在此警醒感恩」（4:2）。「恒切」这个词也用在初期教会「同心合意地恒切祷告」（徒1:14；参罗12:12），有坚持、专心、不放手的意思。祷告不像民间信仰那样有求才拜、临急才烧香，而是生活的常态。耶稣也设比喻，「是要人常常祷告，不可灰心」（路18:1）。

**2. 警醒。** 「警醒」原文的意思是保持清醒、不睡觉。耶稣在客西马尼园对门徒说：「总要警醒祷告，免得入了迷惑」（太26:41）；他也吩咐门徒为他的再来警醒：「要警醒！」（可13:37）。所以许多解经家认为，这里的警醒有两层意思：一是留心试探，也留心身边人的需要；二是记得主必再来。

**3. 感恩。** 歌罗西书常常提到感谢（1:3、12；2:7；3:15、17）。保罗把感恩放在祷告里，不是叫人假装一切都好，而是提醒我们：我们所求的这位神，已经「救了我们脱离黑暗的权势，把我们迁到他爱子的国里」（1:13）。祈求是因为我们有需要，感恩是因为神已经成就了救恩。

**4. 不只求出监，更求开门。** 「也要为我们祷告，求神给我们开传道的门，能以讲基督的奥秘（我为此被捆锁）」（4:3）。保罗被捆锁，但在这封信里，他请人祷告的重点不是早日获释（虽然他在门22也盼望「藉着你们的祷告」得释放），而是有传道的「门」。「开门」是保罗常用的说法，指传福音的机会（林前16:9；林后2:12；参徒14:27）。门是神开的；保罗的责任是在门开的时候讲。「基督的奥秘」就是从前隐藏、如今显明的福音：连外邦人也因基督在他们心里，得着荣耀的盼望（西1:26-27；2:2）。

**5. 连保罗也需要人代祷。** 「叫我按着所该说的话将这奥秘发明出来」（4:4）。和合本的「发明」是显明、讲清楚的意思。保罗是大使徒，却请歌罗西教会为他祷告，求神帮助他把福音讲清楚，而那里大多数人从没见过他的面（2:1）。传福音需要胆量（弗6:19-20），也需要清楚。能把福音讲清楚，本身就是神的恩赐，要靠祷告求来。

> 要点：我们向那位已经救我们脱离黑暗权势的神祷告，所以祷告可以持续、清醒，并且带着感恩。我们为传福音的人求的，不只是平安顺利，更是求神开门，叫福音被讲清楚；连使徒保罗也靠着弟兄姊妹的代祷开口。

## 深读 4:5-6 用智慧与外人交往

**1. 谁是「外人」。** 「你们要爱惜光阴，用智慧与外人交往」（4:5）。「外人」指教会以外、还没有信主的人（参林前5:12-13；帖前4:12）。和合本译作「交往」的这个词，原文是「行」，和1:10「行事为人对得起主」、2:6「就当遵他而行」的「行」是同一个词。保罗关心的不只是信徒怎样说话，也是他们怎样在邻居、同事和家人面前过日子。

**2. 爱惜光阴。** 「爱惜光阴」原文可以直译为买回时机（参弗5:16）。这里的「光阴」是时机、机会的意思。这不是叫信徒把每一分钟都排满，而是说：神放在我们身边的机会不会一直在那里，要珍惜。保罗在第3节求神开门，在这里吩咐信徒把握门开的时候。

**3. 言语带着和气。** 「你们的言语要常常带着和气」（4:6）。和合本译作「和气」的，原文就是「恩典」这个词。信徒的话不只是要客气、有礼貌，更要流露出自己所领受的恩典。众人曾希奇耶稣「口中所出的恩言」（路4:22）；道成肉身的主「充充满满地有恩典有真理」（约1:14）。这里的「和气」不是华人常说的以和为贵、息事宁人，而是基督的恩典。有恩典的话并非不讲真理；讲真理，也不可没有恩典。「带着和气」也不等于凡事附和，或对罪恶和欺压闭口不言。

**4. 好像用盐调和。** 「好像用盐调和」是什么意思？解经家有几种看法。有人认为盐使食物有味道，指说话要有分量、不乏味（古希腊文学里，「盐」有时指机智风趣的话）；有人认为盐能防止腐坏，指话语要纯净，不说污秽的话（参弗4:29）；也有人联系到耶稣说的「你们里头应当有盐，彼此和睦」（可9:50；参太5:13）。这几层意思可以并存：信徒的话既要有恩典，也要有味道、有内容，不是空洞的套话。

**5. 知道该怎样回答各人。** 「就可知道该怎样回答各人」（4:6）。每个人的问题不同，处境也不同：有人真心想知道，有人带着嘲笑，有人在悲伤中发问。有智慧的回答，先要听清楚对方在问什么。彼得也说：「有人问你们心中盼望的缘由，就要常作准备，以温柔、敬畏的心回答各人」（彼前3:15）。回答不是为了辩赢，而是为了让对方看见基督。

**6. 在华人家庭和职场里。** 过年回家，长辈也许会问：「你信了洋教，以后还拜不拜祖先？」同事也许会笑你：「周末不加班，跑去教会干什么？」有人因为怕伤和气、怕没面子，就一句话也不说；有人一开口就是争论，把亲人当成要驳倒的对手。保罗指出了另一条路：不躲避，也不攻击；带着恩典，也带着真理。长辈这样问，心里常常是在问：你还是不是这家的人？我们走了以后，还有没有人记得我们？信徒可以坚持不祭拜祖先、不拜偶像，同时更加孝敬父母（参出20:12）：父母在世时多陪伴、多照顾；先人离世后，用追思礼拜、清明扫墓献花、讲述他们的生平来纪念他们。也要在合适的时候用话说明：我们敬拜的是创造天地、赐人生命的神，祖先也是他所造的人，所以我们纪念祖先，却不敬拜他们（本计划在哥林多前书第八章和第十章有较详细的讨论）。这样，家人可以从我们的行动和话语中看见，信耶稣的人没有忘本。

> 要点：我们对外人说的话，应当流露出我们自己领受的恩典：先听清楚，再温柔、诚实地回答。开门是神的事，回答是我们服事人的方式。

## 深读 4:7-9 推基古和阿尼西谋

**1. 推基古。** 「有我亲爱的兄弟推基古要将我一切的事都告诉你们。他是忠心的执事，和我一同作主的仆人」（4:7）。推基古是亚细亚人（徒20:4），也替保罗把以弗所书带到收信的教会（弗6:21-22）。保罗用三个称呼形容他：亲爱的兄弟、忠心的执事、一同作主的仆人。送信要走很远的路，这样的服事并不显眼，保罗却很看重。

**2. 安慰人心。** 「我特意打发他到你们那里去，好叫你们知道我们的光景，又叫他安慰你们的心」（4:8）。保罗在监里，却打发人去安慰歌罗西人。他不只盼望别人关心他，也关心别人听到他坐监的消息后会怎样。

**3. 阿尼西谋。** 「我又打发一位亲爱忠心的兄弟阿尼西谋同去；他也是你们那里的人」（4:9）。从腓利门书看，阿尼西谋原是歌罗西信徒腓利门的奴隶，离开了主人，后来在保罗那里信了主（门10）。他为什么离开，学者看法不一：有人认为他是逃跑的奴隶，也有人认为他得罪了主人之后，特意去找主人的朋友保罗代为求情。保罗说他「从前与你没有益处，但如今与你我都有益处」（门11）。

**4. 「亲爱忠心的兄弟」。** 保罗称推基古为「亲爱的兄弟」「忠心的执事」，也称阿尼西谋为「亲爱忠心的兄弟」。在罗马的法律里，奴隶是主人的财产；但在教会里，奴隶阿尼西谋和使徒的同工推基古，得着同样的称呼：「亲爱的兄弟」。这就是3:11「为奴的、自主的……惟有基督是包括一切」落实在一个人身上。歌罗西的教会要接待的，不是一个闯过祸的奴隶，而是「你们那里的人」，一位弟兄。

**5. 保罗与奴隶制度。** 上一章已经谈过保罗怎样看奴隶制度（3:22-4:1）。这里只要留意：保罗请腓利门接待阿尼西谋，「不再是奴仆，乃是高过奴仆，是亲爱的兄弟」（门16）。福音里的弟兄关系，从根本上否定了把人当作财产的制度。

> 要点：在基督里，一个从前「没有益处」的奴隶，成了「亲爱忠心的兄弟」。福音改变的不只是人的心，也改变人与人之间的关系。

## 深读 4:10-14 六位同工

**1. 亚里达古。** 「与我一同坐监的亚里达古问你们安」（4:10）。亚里达古是马其顿的帖撒罗尼迦人，曾在以弗所的暴动中被众人拿住（徒19:29），后来又陪保罗坐船往罗马去（徒27:2）。「一同坐监」原文直译是一同被掳的战俘，可能是他也被拘禁，也可能是他自愿陪着保罗受囚。在腓利门书里，「与我同坐监的」是以巴弗（门23），亚里达古则被称为同工（门24），所以也有人认为同工们是轮流陪伴保罗的。

**2. 马可。** 「巴拿巴的表弟马可也问你们安。（说到这马可，你们已经受了吩咐；他若到了你们那里，你们就接待他。）」（4:10）。马可曾在第一次宣教旅程中途离开（徒13:13），后来保罗不肯再带他同去，甚至和巴拿巴「起了争论，甚至彼此分开」（徒15:39）。圣经没有告诉我们当时谁对谁错。如今马可在保罗身边作同工；在保罗最后的一封信里，他还说「他在传道的事上于我有益处」（提后4:11）。彼得也称他为「我儿子马可」（彼前5:13），教会历来的传统认为马可福音就是他写的。一个曾经半途而废的人，没有被永远定型；保罗特别嘱咐歌罗西人接待他，也许正是因为有人还记得他过去的事。

**3. 耶数又称为犹士都。** 「耶数又称为犹士都，也问你们安」（4:11）。「耶数」在原文和「耶稣」是同一个名字（相当于希伯来文的约书亚），当时是很普通的犹太名字；和合本译作「耶数」，与主的名字区分开来。保罗接着说：「奉割礼的人中，只有这三个人是为神的国与我一同做工的，也是叫我心里得安慰的」（4:11）。保罗自己是犹太人，可是此时在他身边、奉割礼的同工，只有这三位。这句话里有孤单，也有感谢。

**4. 以巴弗。** 以巴弗是歌罗西人，很可能是最早把福音带到歌罗西的人（1:7）。他「在祷告之间，常为你们竭力地祈求，愿你们在神一切的旨意上得以完全，信心充足，能站立得稳」（4:12）。和合本译作「竭力」的这个词，和1:29保罗「尽心竭力」、2:1「何等地尽心竭力」是同一个词根，原意是在竞技场上拼搏（参罗15:30）。祷告是真实的劳苦。以巴弗为他们求的，不是生意兴隆，而是在神的旨意上成熟、确信、站立得稳，正好针对那些要把他们「掳去」的假教训（2:8）。他不只为歌罗西劳苦，也为附近老底嘉和希拉坡里的弟兄劳苦（4:13）。

**5. 路加。** 「所亲爱的医生路加和底马问你们安」（4:14）。保罗在第11节列完「奉割礼的人」，才提到以巴弗、路加和底马，所以许多学者推断路加是外邦人；也有学者（如埃利斯）认为，「奉割礼的人」在这里可能指一群较严格的犹太信徒，所以路加也可能是讲希腊话的犹太人。如果路加是外邦人，那么占新约很大篇幅的路加福音和使徒行传，就是出自一位外邦信徒的手。保罗称他为「所亲爱的医生」；在保罗最后的日子，「独有路加在我这里」（提后4:11）。

**6. 底马。** 底马在这里只提了名字，没有任何称赞（在门24他被称为同工）。后来保罗写道：「底马贪爱现今的世界，就离弃我往帖撒罗尼迦去了」（提后4:10）。他后来有没有回转，圣经没有说，我们也不必猜测。马可和底马的名字放在一起，是一个提醒：曾经跌倒的人可以被恢复；「自己以为站得稳的，须要谨慎，免得跌倒」（林前10:12）。我们的盼望不在自己过去的服事，而在「那能保守你们不失脚」的神（犹24）。

> 要点：保罗身边是一群有名有姓、各有故事的同工：有一同坐监的，有被恢复的，有竭力祷告的，有默默陪伴的，也有后来离开的。福音的工作从来不是一个人的事，而是神用一群软弱的人一同做成的。

## 深读 4:15-18 家里的教会，亲笔的问安

**1. 宁法家里的教会。** 「请问老底嘉的弟兄和宁法，并她家里的教会安」（4:15）。初期教会没有礼拜堂，常在信徒家里聚会（罗16:5；林前16:19；门2）。有些抄本把「她家里」写成「他家里」，这样宁法就成了男子；但多数学者根据梵蒂冈抄本等重要抄本。他们又考虑到抄写的人比较可能把女名改成男名，而不是反过来，认为宁法是一位姊妹，和合本也这样翻译。她打开自己的家，让教会在那里聚会。

**2. 交换书信。** 「你们念了这书信，便交给老底嘉的教会，叫他们也念；你们也要念从老底嘉来的书信」（4:16）。保罗的信是要在教会中公开宣读的（参帖前5:27），也要在教会之间传阅。「从老底嘉来的书信」是哪一封，有几种看法：
- **一封失传的信：** 保罗另外写给老底嘉教会的信，没有保存下来。
- **以弗所书：** 以弗所书的导论提过，以弗所书可能原是一封在亚细亚众教会中传阅的信；持这个看法的人认为，它是经过老底嘉再传到歌罗西的。据特土良记载，二世纪的马吉安就称以弗所书为《致老底嘉人书》。莱特福特等学者持这个看法。但有些学者（如霍纳；见以弗所书导论）认为以弗所书1:1的「在以弗所」是原文，他们多半认为这是一封失传的信。
- **腓利门书：** 也有人这样认为，但接受的人较少。

后来流传的一封拉丁文《致老底嘉人书》，内容大多是从腓立比书拼凑而成；中世纪许多拉丁文圣经抄本收了它，但教会从来没有把它列为正典。无论是哪一封，神已经把他要教会领受的话保守在圣经里（参彼前1:25；提后3:16-17）。

**3. 亚基布。** 「要对亚基布说：『务要谨慎，尽你从主所受的职分。』」（4:17）。亚基布也出现在腓利门书，被称为「与我们同当兵的」（门2）；有人推测他是腓利门的儿子，但圣经没有说。他的职分是什么，我们也不知道；有人认为在以巴弗不在的时候，他在歌罗西承担服事。这句嘱咐要在全教会面前念出来，带着鼓励，也带着提醒。职分是「从主所受」的，所以要向主交账，也可以靠主完成（参1:25、29）。

**4. 亲笔问安。** 「我－保罗亲笔问你们安」（4:18）。保罗的信常常由别人代笔（参罗16:22），他在信末亲手写几句，证明这封信确实出于他（帖后3:17；参林前16:21；加6:11）。

**5. 「你们要记念我的捆锁」。** 保罗最后的请求，不是要人同情他，而是要他们记得为他祷告（参4:3），也记得这位福音的使者是戴着锁链的（参弗6:20）。然而，「神的道却不被捆绑」（提后2:9）。

**6. 「愿恩惠常与你们同在！」** 这封信以恩惠开始：「愿恩惠、平安从神我们的父归与你们！」（1:2）；也以恩惠结束。保罗讲了基督的超越，揭穿了假教师的规条，描绘了新人的生活和家中的关系，最后一句话却不是叫人更加努力，而是「恩惠」。信徒的整个生活，从起头到末了，都活在神的恩典里。

> 要点：福音在家里的聚会中传开，在教会之间被传阅，由一群同工带到各处；而这一切的起头和结尾，都是神的恩惠。

## 默想

**默想：** 「你们的言语要常常带着和气，好像用盐调和」（4:6）。想一位你常见面、却还没有信主的人：同事、同学，或者过年才见面的亲戚。回想你们上一次谈到信仰的时候：你是怕气氛尴尬，什么都没说？还是急着说服他，越说越大声？还是根本没有听清楚他在问什么？若我不开口是因为怕人、爱面子，就要先向主认这个罪。保罗在锁链中，最先请人代求的不是早日出狱，而是能把福音讲清楚（4:3-4）。你可以把这个人的名字告诉一位弟兄姊妹，请他为你，也为这个人祷告。门是神开的，人心也是神改变的；你不必靠自己的口才赢得一个人，也不必因为上次说错了话或者没有开口，就以为门永远关上了。

再想一想：你自己还是「外人」的时候（4:5），基督是怎样待你的？就算你从小在教会长大，也曾经是那个需要恩典、需要被等候的人（参弗2:12）。他没有用一句话把你驳倒，而是一次又一次地忍耐你、等候你，藉着别人，也藉着他的话，向你说恩典的话。你今天能说出的每一句带着和气的话，都是先从他那里领受的恩典。所以保罗在信的最后没有再加上一条吩咐，只说：「愿恩惠常与你们同在！」（4:18）。下一次开口的时候，这恩惠也与你同在。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 4 brings Colossians to a close. Verse 1, "Masters, treat your slaves justly and fairly," belongs to the household code that began at 3:18 and was covered in the last chapter. From verse 2 the chapter has three parts.

- **Prayer and speech** (vv. 2–6). Continue steadfastly in prayer, watchful and thankful, and pray for Paul too, that God would open a door for the word. Walk wisely toward outsiders, and let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt.
- **Two messengers** (vv. 7–9). Tychicus and Onesimus will tell them how Paul is and encourage their hearts.
- **Greetings and charges** (vv. 10–18). Six co-workers send greetings, some Jewish and some Gentile. Paul greets the brothers at Laodicea and the church in Nympha's house, asks the churches to exchange letters, and gives Archippus a charge to fulfill his ministry. Finally Paul writes with his own hand: "Remember my chains. Grace be with you."

The chapter can look like a list of names, but it shows how the claim of the first three chapters, "Christ is all, and in all" (3:11), takes shape in real people: Mark, who once left the mission halfway; Onesimus, a slave who was once "useless"; a physician who was very likely a Gentile; a woman who opened her home to the church. Paul is in chains, yet through these people the gospel keeps moving.

## Going Deeper: 4:2–4 Continue in Prayer, Pray for an Open Door

**1. Continue steadfastly.** "Continue steadfastly in prayer, being watchful in it with thanksgiving" (4:2). The same verb describes the early church, who "with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer" (Acts 1:14; see Rom 12:12). It means persisting, giving close attention, not letting go. Prayer is not like folk religion's burning incense only in a crisis; it is the steady texture of life. Jesus told a parable "to the effect that they ought always to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1).

**2. Watchful.** The word means to stay awake and alert. In Gethsemane Jesus told his disciples, "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Matt 26:41), and he told them to watch for his return: "Stay awake" (Mark 13:37). So many interpreters hear two things here: alertness to temptation and to the needs of those around us, and remembering that the Lord will come again.

**3. With thanksgiving.** Colossians speaks of thanks again and again (1:3, 12; 2:7; 3:15, 17). Paul puts thanksgiving inside prayer, not to make us pretend that all is well, but to remind us that the God we ask "has delivered us from the domain of darkness and transferred us to the kingdom of his beloved Son" (1:13). We ask because we are needy; we give thanks because God has already accomplished our salvation.

**4. Not only release, but a door.** "At the same time, pray also for us, that God may open to us a door for the word, to declare the mystery of Christ, on account of which I am in prison" (4:3). Paul is in chains, but in this letter the prayer he asks for is not chiefly release (though he hopes for that "through your prayers" in Phlm 22). It is a "door." An open door is Paul's way of speaking about an opportunity for the gospel (1 Cor 16:9; 2 Cor 2:12; see Acts 14:27). God opens the door; Paul's part is to speak when it opens. "The mystery of Christ" is the gospel once hidden and now revealed: that even Gentiles have "Christ in you, the hope of glory" (Col 1:26–27; 2:2).

**5. Even Paul needed prayer.** "That I may make it clear, which is how I ought to speak" (4:4). Paul the apostle asks a church most of whose members had never seen him (2:1) to pray that God would help him make the gospel clear. Gospel speech needs boldness (Eph 6:19–20), and it also needs clarity. Being able to make the gospel plain is itself a gift of God, and it is sought in prayer.

> The point: We pray to the God who has already rescued us from the domain of darkness, so our prayer can be persistent, alert and full of thanks. For those who preach the gospel we ask not only for safety and success, but for God to open doors and to make the message clear. Even the apostle Paul opened his mouth leaning on the prayers of the saints.

## Going Deeper: 4:5–6 Wisdom Toward Outsiders

**1. Who are "outsiders"?** "Conduct yourselves wisely toward outsiders, making the best use of the time" (4:5). Outsiders are those outside the church who do not yet believe (see 1 Cor 5:12–13; 1 Thess 4:12). The verb behind "conduct yourselves" is "walk," the same word as in 1:10, "walk in a manner worthy of the Lord," and 2:6, "so walk in him." Paul cares not only about how believers talk but about how they live before neighbors, colleagues and family.

**2. Making the best use of the time.** The phrase can be rendered "buying up the opportunity" (see Eph 5:16). The word for "time" here means a season or opportunity. Paul is not telling believers to fill every minute. He is saying that the openings God places around us will not stay open forever, so treasure them. In verse 3 Paul asked God to open a door; here he tells believers to walk through it while it is open.

**3. Gracious speech.** "Let your speech always be gracious" (4:6). Literally, "let your speech always be with grace." Believers are to be more than polite; their words should carry something of the grace they have received. People marveled at "the gracious words that were coming from [Jesus'] mouth" (Luke 4:22); the Word made flesh was "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). This "grace" is not the Chinese ideal of keeping the peace at any cost, but Christ's grace. Gracious words are not empty of truth, and truthful words must not be empty of grace. Gracious speech also does not mean agreeing with everything, or keeping silent about sin and oppression.

**4. Seasoned with salt.** What does "seasoned with salt" mean? Interpreters offer several readings. Some say salt gives food flavor, so speech should be interesting and substantial, not dull (in Greek writers "salt" could mean wit). Some say salt preserves from decay, so speech should be clean, free of corrupting talk (see Eph 4:29). Others connect it with Jesus' words, "Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another" (Mark 9:50; see Matt 5:13). These can stand together: a believer's words should carry grace, and also flavor and weight, not stock phrases.

**5. Knowing how to answer each person.** "So that you may know how you ought to answer each person" (4:6). People's questions differ, and so do their situations. One person truly wants to know, another is mocking, another asks out of grief. A wise answer begins by hearing what is actually being asked. Peter says the same: "always being prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you" (1 Pet 3:15), and he goes on to say it should be done with gentleness and respect. The aim of an answer is not to win but to let the other person see Christ.

**6. In Chinese families and workplaces.** At New Year an older relative may ask, "You've joined a foreign religion. Will you still worship the ancestors?" A colleague may tease, "You won't work weekends because you go to church?" Some of us say nothing, afraid of spoiling the peace or losing face. Others argue at once and treat relatives as opponents to be defeated. Paul shows another way: neither hiding nor attacking, carrying both grace and truth. Behind an elder's question there is often another one: are you still one of this family, and will anyone remember us when we are gone? A believer can decline to worship the ancestors or any idol and still honor parents all the more (see Exod 20:12): spending time with them and caring for them while they live, and after they die remembering them through a memorial service, by tending the grave and laying flowers at Qingming, and by telling their stories. When the time is right, we also explain in words: we worship the God who made heaven and earth and gives life, and our ancestors too were people he made, so we remember them but do not worship them (this plan discusses these questions more fully in 1 Corinthians, chapters 8 and 10). In this way the family can see from what we do and say that following Jesus does not mean forgetting one's roots.

> The point: Our words to outsiders should carry the grace we ourselves have received: listen first, then answer gently and honestly. Opening the door is God's work; answering is one way we serve people.

## Going Deeper: 4:7–9 Tychicus and Onesimus

**1. Tychicus.** "Tychicus will tell you all about my activities. He is a beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow servant in the Lord" (4:7). Tychicus came from Asia (Acts 20:4) and also carried Ephesians to the churches it was written for (Eph 6:21–22). Paul gives him three titles: beloved brother, faithful minister, fellow servant. Carrying letters meant long journeys and little notice, but Paul valued that service highly.

**2. Encouraging hearts.** "I have sent him to you for this very purpose, that you may know how we are and that he may encourage your hearts" (4:8). Paul, in prison, sends someone to comfort the Colossians. He does not only want others to care about him; he cares how they will take the news of his imprisonment.

**3. Onesimus.** "And with him Onesimus, our faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you" (4:9). From Philemon we learn that Onesimus was the slave of Philemon, a believer in Colossae. He had left his master and later came to faith through Paul (Phlm 10). Why he left is debated: some think he was a runaway; others think that, having wronged his master, he went to Paul, his master's friend, to ask him to intercede. Paul writes, "Formerly he was useless to you, but now he is indeed useful to you and to me" (Phlm 11).

**4. "Faithful and beloved brother."** Paul calls Tychicus "a beloved brother and faithful minister," and he calls Onesimus "our faithful and beloved brother." In Roman law a slave was his master's property; in the church the slave Onesimus and the apostle's co-worker Tychicus are given the same title, "beloved brother." This is 3:11, "slave, free; but Christ is all," lived out in one man. The church in Colossae was to welcome not a slave who had caused trouble, but "one of you," a brother.

**5. Paul and slavery.** The previous chapter has already looked at how Paul treats slavery (3:22–4:1). Here we need only notice that Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (Phlm 16). The brotherhood of the gospel undercuts at the root any system that treats people as property.

> The point: In Christ a slave once "useless" became a "faithful and beloved brother." The gospel changes not only hearts but the relationships between people.

## Going Deeper: 4:10–14 Six Co-workers

**1. Aristarchus.** "Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you" (4:10). Aristarchus was a Macedonian from Thessalonica. He was seized by the mob in the Ephesian riot (Acts 19:29) and later sailed with Paul toward Rome (Acts 27:2). "Fellow prisoner" (literally "fellow prisoner of war") may mean he too was detained, or that he chose to share Paul's confinement. In Philemon it is Epaphras who is called "my fellow prisoner" (Phlm 23) and Aristarchus a fellow worker (Phlm 24), so some think the co-workers took turns staying with Paul.

**2. Mark.** "And Mark the cousin of Barnabas (concerning whom you have received instructions—if he comes to you, welcome him)" (4:10). Mark had left partway through the first missionary journey (Acts 13:13). Later Paul refused to take him again, and "there arose a sharp disagreement" between Paul and Barnabas, "so that they separated from each other" (Acts 15:39). Scripture does not tell us who was right. Now Mark is with Paul as a co-worker, and in his last letter Paul says, "he is very useful to me for ministry" (2 Tim 4:11). Peter calls him "Mark, my son" (1 Pet 5:13), and the church's long tradition holds that he wrote the Gospel of Mark. A man who once quit halfway was not fixed forever by that failure; Paul's instruction to welcome him may be because some still remembered his past.

**3. Jesus called Justus.** "And Jesus who is called Justus" (4:11). "Jesus" (the Greek form of the Hebrew name Joshua) was a common Jewish name; the Chinese Union Version spells his name differently to distinguish him from the Lord. Paul adds, "These are the only men of the circumcision among my fellow workers for the kingdom of God, and they have been a comfort to me" (4:11). Paul was a Jew himself, yet of the men of the circumcision with him at this time, only these three were his fellow workers. There is loneliness in that sentence, and gratitude too.

**4. Epaphras.** Epaphras was from Colossae and was very likely the one who first brought the gospel there (1:7). He is "always struggling on your behalf in his prayers, that you may stand mature and fully assured in all the will of God" (4:12). "Struggling" shares its root with Paul's "struggling" in 1:29 and his "great struggle" in 2:1; it was used of an athlete's contest (see Rom 15:30). Prayer is real labor. Epaphras did not pray for their business to prosper but for them to stand mature and fully assured in God's will, which is exactly what the false teaching that would "take [them] captive" threatened (2:8). He worked hard not only for Colossae but for nearby Laodicea and Hierapolis (4:13).

**5. Luke.** "Luke the beloved physician greets you, as does Demas" (4:14). Paul finishes listing "the men of the circumcision" in verse 11 before naming Epaphras, Luke and Demas, so many scholars infer that Luke was a Gentile. Others (e.g., E. Earle Ellis) argue that "the men of the circumcision" may denote a narrower group of strict Jewish believers, so Luke may have been a Greek-speaking Jew. If Luke was a Gentile, then Luke and Acts, a large share of the New Testament, came from a Gentile believer's hand. Paul calls him "the beloved physician," and in Paul's last days, "Luke alone is with me" (2 Tim 4:11).

**6. Demas.** Demas is simply named here, with no word of praise (in Phlm 24 he is called a fellow worker). Later Paul writes, "Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me" (2 Tim 4:10). Whether he ever returned, Scripture does not say, and we need not guess. Mark and Demas side by side are a reminder: one who has fallen can be restored, and "let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Cor 10:12). Our hope is not in our past service but in "him who is able to keep you from stumbling" (Jude 24).

> The point: Around Paul was a group of named co-workers, each with a story: one shared his imprisonment, one had been restored, one wrestled in prayer, one quietly stayed, and one later left. Gospel work is never one person's work; God accomplishes it through a company of weak people together.

## Going Deeper: 4:15–18 A Church in a House, a Greeting in Paul's Hand

**1. The church in Nympha's house.** "Give my greetings to the brothers at Laodicea, and to Nympha and the church in her house" (4:15). The early church had no church buildings and often met in believers' homes (Rom 16:5; 1 Cor 16:19; Phlm 2). Some manuscripts read "his house," which makes the name masculine (Nymphas). But most scholars, following Codex Vaticanus and judging that scribes were likelier to turn a woman's name into a man's than the reverse, take Nympha to be a woman, as the CUV and ESV do. She opened her home for the church to meet there.

**2. Exchanging letters.** "And when this letter has been read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and see that you also read the letter from Laodicea" (4:16). Paul's letters were meant to be read aloud in the church (see 1 Thess 5:27) and passed between churches. Which letter is "the letter from Laodicea"? There are several views:
- **A lost letter:** another letter Paul wrote to the Laodicean church, which has not survived.
- **Ephesians:** as the introduction to Ephesians noted, Ephesians may first have been a circular letter for the churches of Asia; on this view it reached Colossae by way of Laodicea. According to Tertullian, Marcion in the second century called Ephesians the letter "to the Laodiceans." Lightfoot and others held this view; scholars who take "in Ephesus" in Eph 1:1 as original (e.g., Hoehner; see the Ephesians introduction) generally favor a lost letter.
- **Philemon:** some have suggested this, but few accept it.

A Latin "Epistle to the Laodiceans" that circulated later is made up largely of phrases from Philippians; many medieval Latin Bibles copied it, but the church never received it as canonical. Whichever letter Paul meant, God has kept in Scripture the words he meant his church to receive (see 1 Pet 1:25; 2 Tim 3:16–17).

**3. Archippus.** "And say to Archippus, 'See that you fulfill the ministry that you have received in the Lord'" (4:17). Archippus also appears in Philemon as "our fellow soldier" (Phlm 2). Some guess he was Philemon's son, but Scripture does not say. Nor do we know what his ministry was; some think he served the church in Colossae while Epaphras was away. The charge was to be read before the whole church, as both encouragement and reminder. The ministry was "received in the Lord," so it is answerable to the Lord and can be fulfilled in his strength (see 1:25, 29).

**4. In Paul's own hand.** "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand" (4:18). Paul's letters were often written down by someone else (cf. Rom 16:22); he added a few lines in his own hand at the end as a mark that the letter was genuine (2 Thess 3:17; see 1 Cor 16:21; Gal 6:11).

**5. "Remember my chains."** Paul's last request is not for pity. He wants them to keep praying for him (see 4:3) and to remember that the gospel's ambassador is in chains (see Eph 6:20). Yet "the word of God is not bound!" (2 Tim 2:9).

**6. "Grace be with you."** The letter opened with grace: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father" (1:2), and it closes with grace. Paul has spoken of Christ's supremacy, exposed the false teachers' rules, and described the new self and the life of the household, and his last word is not "try harder" but "grace." The whole Christian life, from first to last, is lived in the grace of God.

> The point: The gospel spread through gatherings in homes, letters passed from church to church, and a company of co-workers carrying it from place to place; and all of it begins and ends with the grace of God.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt" (4:6). Think of someone you see often who does not yet believe: a colleague, a classmate, or a relative you see only at New Year. Recall the last time faith came up between you. Did you say nothing because it felt awkward? Did you rush to convince them, getting louder as you went? Or did you never quite hear what they were asking? If I kept silent out of fear of people or of losing face, I confess that to the Lord first. Paul in chains asked first not for an early release but that he could make the gospel clear (4:3–4). You might tell a brother or sister this person's name and ask them to pray for you and for them. God opens the door, and God changes hearts. You do not have to win this person by your own eloquence, and you need not think the door is shut forever because last time you said the wrong thing, or nothing at all.

Then think again: when you yourself were an "outsider" (4:5), how did Christ treat you? Even if you grew up in church, you too were once someone who needed grace and needed to be waited for (see Eph 2:12). He did not defeat you with one sharp sentence. He was patient with you and waited for you, again and again, and spoke words of grace to you through other people and through his word. Every gracious word you can say today is grace you first received from him. That is why Paul ends the letter without one more command, only this: "Grace be with you" (4:18). The next time you open your mouth, that grace is with you too.
