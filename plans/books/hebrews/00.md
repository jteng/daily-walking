## 这卷书在讲什么

希伯来书一开头没有问安，也没有写信人的名字，而是直接讲神：「神既在古时藉着众先知多次多方地晓谕列祖，就在这末世藉着他儿子晓谕我们。」（1:1-2）到了结尾，作者才说：「弟兄们，我略略写信给你们，望你们听我劝勉的话。」（13:22）

「劝勉的话」这个说法，在使徒行传里用来指会堂里读经以后的讲道（徒13:15）。所以许多学者认为，希伯来书本来是一篇讲章，写下来寄给一群信徒，最后附上几句书信式的问候（13:22-25）。

这群信徒信主已经有一段时间了。他们起初很火热，为主受过苦：「你们要追念往日，蒙了光照以后所忍受大争战的各样苦难」，「你们的家业被人抢去，也甘心忍受」（10:32、34）。可是现在，有些人累了。他们「听不进去」（5:11），有人「停止聚会」（10:25），有人有退回去的危险（10:38-39）。作者怕他们「随流失去」（2:1）。

作者怎样劝他们？他没有只是叫他们再加把劲，而是一次又一次地把耶稣摆在他们面前：
- **耶稣比天使更尊贵**（1-2章）。
- **耶稣比摩西更配得荣耀**（3章）。
- **耶稣是比亚伦更大的大祭司**，「照着麦基洗德的等次」永远为祭司（5-7章）。
- **耶稣是更美之约的中保**（8章），他献上的祭比一切牛羊的血都更美（9-10章）。

全书反复出现「更美」这个词：更美的指望（7:19）、更美之约（7:22；8:6）、更美的应许（8:6）、更美的家业（10:34）、更美的家乡（11:16）。疲倦的信徒需要的，不是一个新的方法，而是重新看见耶稣有多么美。所以作者说：「仰望为我们信心创始成终的耶稣。」（12:2）

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 谁写的？** 希伯来书没有写出作者的名字。早期教会对此就有不同的看法：
- **保罗：** 东方的教会，特别是亚历山大的教会，多认为是保罗写的。亚历山大的革利免认为，保罗用希伯来文写成，路加译成希腊文（优西比乌《教会史》第六卷第十四章）。现存最早的保罗书信抄本（P46，约主后200年）把希伯来书放在罗马书后面。西方的教会却长期不认为是保罗写的。
- **不是保罗：** 俄利根注意到，这卷书的文风和保罗书信很不一样。他认为书中的思想是使徒的，文字却出于另一个人；他又说：「至于是谁写的，只有神真知道。」（《教会史》第六卷第二十五章）作者说这救恩「起先是主亲自讲的，后来是听见的人给我们证实了」（2:3），似乎把自己放在第二代信徒中间；保罗却强调他所传的福音是「从耶稣基督启示来的」（加1:12）。所以宗教改革时期，加尔文也不认为是保罗写的。
- **其他人选：** 特土良认为是巴拿巴，马丁·路德提出亚波罗（他「是有学问的，最能讲解圣经」，徒18:24），也有人提出路加，十九世纪末的哈纳克还提出百基拉。

作者显然很熟悉旧约（他用的是希腊文的七十士译本），希腊文写得很优美，又认识提摩太（13:23）。本研读不确定作者是谁，只称他为「作者」。作者是谁虽不能确定，教会却渐渐一致承认这卷书是神所默示的圣经：一世纪末的《革利免一书》已经带着权威引用它；到了四世纪后期，东方（如亚他那修在主后367年的第三十九封节期书信）和西方的教会都把它列在新约正典中。

**2. 写给谁？** 「希伯来书」这个标题，至少在主后二世纪末已经有了。从内容看，读者很熟悉旧约的献祭和祭司制度，作者也常常用这些来讲基督，所以大多数学者认为，读者主要是信了耶稣的犹太人，或者至少是很熟悉旧约的信徒。他们在哪里？
- **罗马：** 「从意大利来的人也问你们安」（13:24），可以理解为离开意大利的人向家乡的信徒问安（也可以理解为在意大利的信徒向外地问安）。最早引用希伯来书的，是罗马的革利免在主后一世纪末写的《革利免一书》（第三十六章）。主后49年，罗马皇帝克劳第把犹太人赶出罗马（徒18:2），这可能就是10:32-34所说的「往日」的苦难。
- **耶路撒冷或巴勒斯坦：** 也有学者认为，读者住在圣殿附近，很容易被圣殿的献祭吸引回去。

**3. 什么时候写的？**
- **在主后70年以前：** 许多学者认为，这卷书写于主后60年代，圣殿被毁以前。作者讲到前约「渐旧渐衰的，就必快归无有了」（8:13），如果圣殿已经被毁，他很可能会提到这件事。读者也「还没有抵挡到流血的地步」（12:4），似乎尼禄在主后64年开始的逼迫还没有临到他们。
- **在主后70年以后：** 也有学者认为写于主后80年代。他们指出，作者讲的是旷野的会幕，不是圣殿；犹太作者约瑟夫和《革利免一书》在圣殿被毁以后，也仍然用现在式讲献祭。
- 无论哪一种看法，这卷书都写于主后96年左右以前，因为一般认为写于主后96年左右的《革利免一书》已经引用了它。

**4. 读者的试探。** 从书中看，读者面对几种压力：
- **疲倦：** 信主多年，起初的热心渐渐冷了（5:11-12；12:3、12）。
- **羞辱和损失：** 他们曾经「被毁谤，遭患难，成了戏景，叫众人观看」（10:33），家业被人抢去。
- **退回去的试探：** 对犹太信徒来说，回到会堂和祖宗的传统，可以少受许多羞辱。作者却说，那些都是「影像」（8:5；10:1），实体是基督；离开基督，就再也没有别的祭（10:26）。

## 钥匙经文：1:1-3；4:14-16；12:1-2

**1. 「藉着他儿子晓谕我们」。** 「神既在古时藉着众先知多次多方地晓谕列祖，就在这末世藉着他儿子晓谕我们；又早已立他为承受万有的，也曾藉着他创造诸世界。他是神荣耀所发的光辉，是神本体的真像，常用他权能的命令托住万有。他洗净了人的罪，就坐在高天至大者的右边。」（1:1-3）这几节是全书的序言。神没有沉默；他最后、最完全的话，就是他的儿子。这位儿子是创造者、托住万有的主、洗净罪的祭司，又是坐在宝座上的王。全书要讲的，都在这里了。

**2. 「施恩的宝座」。** 「我们既然有一位已经升入高天尊荣的大祭司，就是神的儿子耶稣，便当持定所承认的道。因我们的大祭司并非不能体恤我们的软弱。他也曾凡事受过试探，与我们一样，只是他没有犯罪。所以，我们只管坦然无惧地来到施恩的宝座前，为要得怜恤，蒙恩惠，作随时的帮助。」（4:14-16）这位大祭司又高又近：他「升入高天」，又能「体恤我们的软弱」。疲倦和软弱的人，不必躲开神，反而可以「坦然无惧地」到他面前。

**3. 「仰望……耶稣」。** 「我们既有这许多的见证人，如同云彩围着我们，就当放下各样的重担，脱去容易缠累我们的罪，存心忍耐，奔那摆在我们前头的路程，仰望为我们信心创始成终的耶稣。他因那摆在前面的喜乐，就轻看羞辱，忍受了十字架的苦难，便坐在神宝座的右边。」（12:1-2）信心的路很长，需要忍耐。可是跑这条路的力量，不是来自盯着自己的脚步，而是来自仰望那位已经跑完全程的耶稣。

## 全书结构

希伯来书的结构，是讲解和劝勉交替出现。作者讲一段基督的伟大，就停下来劝读者一次。大致可以这样分：
- **序言**（1:1-4）：神藉着他儿子说话。
- **儿子比天使更尊贵**（1:5-2:18）：中间有第一次警告，不可「随流失去」（2:1-4）；儿子成为我们的弟兄，作慈悲忠信的大祭司。
- **耶稣比摩西更尊贵，神的安息**（3:1-4:13）：「你们今日若听他的话，就不可硬着心」。
- **耶稣是大祭司**（4:14-5:10）：能体恤我们的软弱，照着麦基洗德的等次。
- **劝勉和警告**（5:11-6:20）：不要作吃奶的；离弃道理的危险；神的应许像灵魂的锚。
- **麦基洗德的等次**（7章）：一位长远活着、永远为我们祈求的祭司。
- **更美之约**（8章）：神应许把律法写在人心上。
- **更美的祭**（9:1-10:18）：基督用自己的血，只一次进入圣所。
- **坦然进到神面前**（10:19-39）：既有又新又活的路，就当来到神面前，不可停止聚会；又有一次严厉的警告。
- **信心的见证人**（11章）：亚伯、挪亚、亚伯拉罕、摩西……他们都是存着信心死的。
- **奔跑前面的路程**（12章）：仰望耶稣；神管教他所爱的儿子；我们来到锡安山，得了不能震动的国。
- **最后的劝勉和问安**（13章）：彼此相爱，出到营外就了耶稣去；耶稣基督昨日、今日、一直到永远是一样的。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 旁边打开旧约。** 希伯来书引用旧约的地方非常多，单单第一章就引了七处。作者用的多是七十士译本，所以有些引文和和合本的旧约不完全一样（例如1:6「神的使者都要拜他」，10:5「你曾给我预备了身体」）。读的时候，可以翻到旧约原处，看作者怎样从那里看见基督。主耶稣自己就是这样「从摩西和众先知起，凡经上所指着自己的话都给他们讲解明白了」（路24:27）。

**2. 留意「更美」和「一次」。** 旧约的祭司「每日」献祭，年年重复（7:27；10:1、11）；基督却「只一次」将自己献上（7:27；9:12、26、28；10:10）。旧的不是坏的，而是指向更美的；影像会过去，实体却长存。

**3. 认真听警告，也认真听安慰。** 希伯来书有五段严厉的警告（2:1-4；3:7-4:13；5:11-6:12；10:26-39；12:14-29），特别是6:4-6和10:26-27，历代信徒有不同的理解。改革宗传统一般认为，真正重生的人必蒙保守到底，这些警告是神保守信徒的方法之一（如施莱纳），或者是针对那些看起来信了、其实没有真正重生的人（如欧文）。阿米念传统一般认为，真正的信徒有可能离弃信仰而失落（如马歇尔、麦克奈特）。本研读在相关的章节会把这些看法公平地摆出来。但无论哪一种看法，作者自己都把警告和安慰放在一起：「亲爱的弟兄们，我们虽是这样说，却深信你们的行为强过这些，而且近乎得救。」（6:9）警告是为了叫人回到基督那里，不是为了把人推开。

**4. 把它当作讲道来听。** 这是一篇「劝勉的话」。作者常用「我们」：「我们当越发郑重所听见的道理」（2:1），「我们只管坦然无惧地来到施恩的宝座前」（4:16）。他不是站在远处责备读者，而是和他们一同奔跑。读的时候，可以留意每一段讲解后面的「所以」：作者讲了基督是谁、做了什么，然后才说我们当怎样行。

## 默想

**默想：** 「这救恩起先是主亲自讲的，后来是听见的人给我们证实了。」（2:3）希伯来书的读者，是从别人那里听到福音的第二代信徒。我也许也是这样：从小跟着父母上教会，主日学的故事都听过，诗歌都会唱，许多经文背得出来。可是有时候我觉得，这个信仰好像是父母的，是教会的，是我从小就有的一件旧衣服，却不太像是我自己的。听道的时候，我常常心里想：这些我都知道了。作者对这样的读者说的，不是「你要更努力去感受」，而是「神……就在这末世藉着他儿子晓谕我们」（1:1-2）。说话的是神自己，他说的话就是他的儿子。我所听惯了的那些故事，原来都是神亲口对我说的话。耶稣在登山变像的时候，父也是这样说：「这是我的爱子，我所喜悦的。你们要听他！」（太17:5）我的信仰也许是从父母、从教会那里听来的，可是救我的，不是我从小听惯的那些话，也不是我自己感受得够不够深，而是那位「洗净了人的罪，就坐在高天至大者的右边」的儿子（来1:3）。他已经把我的罪洗净了，包括我对他冷淡、把他的话当作旧话的罪。他也「不以为耻」，称信他的人为弟兄（2:11）。如果我从来没有亲自信靠他，今天就可以第一次向他说：主耶稣，我不要只靠父母的信仰，我要信靠你。如果我早已信了，只是心冷了，我也可以回到他面前。今天读希伯来书，我可以在读经以前先这样祷告：父啊，求你藉着你的儿子对我说话；不是对我的父母，不是对教会里别的人，而是对我。愿我在你儿子的脸上重新看见你的荣耀。

## What This Book Is About

Hebrews begins with no greeting and no writer's name. It begins with God: "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (1:1–2). Only at the end does the author say, "I appeal to you, brothers, bear with my word of exhortation, for I have written to you briefly" (13:22).

In Acts, "word of exhortation" describes the sermon given in a synagogue after the reading of Scripture (Acts 13:15). So many scholars think Hebrews was first a sermon, written down and sent to a group of believers, with a few letter-style greetings added at the end (13:22–25).

These believers had been Christians for some time. At first they were zealous and had suffered for the Lord: "recall the former days when, after you were enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings," and "you joyfully accepted the plundering of your property" (10:32, 34). But now some of them were tired. They had "become dull of hearing" (5:11); some were "neglecting to meet together" (10:25); some were in danger of shrinking back (10:38–39). The author fears they will "drift away" (2:1).

How does he encourage them? He does not simply tell them to try harder. Again and again he sets Jesus before them:
- **Jesus is greater than the angels** (chs. 1–2).
- **Jesus is worthy of more glory than Moses** (ch. 3).
- **Jesus is a greater high priest than Aaron**, a priest forever "after the order of Melchizedek" (chs. 5–7).
- **Jesus is the mediator of a better covenant** (ch. 8), and the sacrifice he offered is better than the blood of all bulls and goats (chs. 9–10).

The word "better" runs through the whole book: a better hope (7:19), a better covenant (7:22; 8:6), better promises (8:6), a better possession (10:34), a better country (11:16). Tired believers do not need a new technique; they need to see again how glorious Jesus is. So the author says: "looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith" (12:2).

## Author and Setting

**1. Who wrote it?** Hebrews does not name its author. The early church already held different views:
- **Paul:** the churches of the East, especially Alexandria, mostly held that Paul wrote it. Clement of Alexandria thought Paul wrote it in Hebrew and Luke translated it into Greek (Eusebius, *Church History* 6.14). The earliest surviving manuscript of Paul's letters (P46, about AD 200) places Hebrews right after Romans. The Western church, however, long doubted that Paul wrote it.
- **Not Paul:** Origen noticed that the style is very different from Paul's letters. He held that the thoughts were the apostle's but the wording was someone else's, and he said, "But who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (*Church History* 6.25). The author says this salvation "was declared at first by the Lord, and it was attested to us by those who heard" (2:3), which seems to place him among second-generation believers; Paul, by contrast, insists that he received his gospel "through a revelation of Jesus Christ" (Gal 1:12). So at the Reformation, Calvin too did not think Paul wrote it.
- **Other candidates:** Tertullian named Barnabas; Martin Luther suggested Apollos ("an eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures," Acts 18:24); others have suggested Luke; and at the end of the nineteenth century Harnack even proposed Priscilla.

The author clearly knew the Old Testament well (he uses the Greek Septuagint), wrote polished Greek, and knew Timothy (13:23). This study does not claim to know who he was and simply calls him "the author." Though the author cannot be named with certainty, the church came to receive this book as Scripture inspired by God: *1 Clement* already used it with authority at the end of the first century, and by the late fourth century it was received in both East (for example, Athanasius's Festal Letter 39, AD 367) and West.

**2. To whom?** The title "To the Hebrews" was in use at least by the end of the second century. From the contents, the readers knew the Old Testament sacrifices and priesthood well, and the author often uses them to explain Christ, so most scholars think the readers were mainly Jewish believers in Jesus, or at least believers steeped in the Old Testament. Where did they live?
- **Rome:** "Those who come from Italy send you greetings" (13:24) can be read as people away from Italy greeting believers back home (it can also mean believers in Italy sending greetings elsewhere). The first writer to quote Hebrews is Clement of Rome, in *1 Clement* (chapter 36), at the end of the first century. In AD 49 the emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2), and this may be the suffering of "the former days" in 10:32–34.
- **Jerusalem or Palestine:** other scholars think the readers lived near the temple and were easily drawn back to its sacrifices.

**3. When was it written?**
- **Before AD 70:** many scholars think it was written in the 60s, before the temple was destroyed. The author says the first covenant "is becoming obsolete and growing old" and "is ready to vanish away" (8:13); had the temple already fallen, he would likely have said so. The readers have also "not yet resisted to the point of shedding [their] blood" (12:4), which suggests that Nero's persecution, begun in AD 64, had not yet reached them.
- **After AD 70:** others date it to the 80s. They point out that the author speaks of the wilderness tabernacle, not the temple, and that the Jewish writer Josephus and *1 Clement* still describe the sacrifices in the present tense after the temple fell.
- On either view, it was written before about AD 96, since *1 Clement*, usually dated about AD 96, already draws on it.

**4. The readers' temptations.** From the book, the readers faced several pressures:
- **Weariness:** after years of faith, their first zeal had cooled (5:11–12; 12:3, 12).
- **Shame and loss:** they had been "publicly exposed to reproach and affliction" (10:33), and their property had been plundered.
- **The pull to go back:** for Jewish believers, returning to the synagogue and their ancestral traditions would spare them much shame. But the author says those things were "a copy and shadow" (8:5; 10:1); the reality is Christ, and apart from him "there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins" (10:26).

## Key Verses: 1:1–3; 4:14–16; 12:1–2

**1. "He has spoken to us by his Son."** "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son, whom he appointed the heir of all things, through whom also he created the world. He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. After making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" (1:1–3). These verses are the prologue to the whole book. God has not been silent; his final and fullest word is his Son. This Son is the Creator, the Lord who upholds all things, the priest who cleanses sin, and the King seated on the throne. Everything the book will say is already here.

**2. "The throne of grace."** "Since then we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God, let us hold fast our confession. For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin. Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (4:14–16). This high priest is both high and near: he "has passed through the heavens," and he is able "to sympathize with our weaknesses." The tired and the weak need not hide from God; they may "with confidence draw near."

**3. "Looking to Jesus."** "Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God" (12:1–2). The road of faith is long and needs endurance. But the strength to run it does not come from watching our own feet; it comes from looking to Jesus, who has already run the whole course.

## Structure of the Book

Hebrews alternates between exposition and exhortation. The author explains part of Christ's greatness, then stops to urge his readers. It can be outlined like this:
- **Prologue** (1:1–4): God has spoken by his Son.
- **The Son is greater than the angels** (1:5–2:18): with the first warning, against drifting away (2:1–4); the Son becomes our brother, a merciful and faithful high priest.
- **Jesus is greater than Moses; God's rest** (3:1–4:13): "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts."
- **Jesus the high priest** (4:14–5:10): able to sympathize with our weaknesses, after the order of Melchizedek.
- **Exhortation and warning** (5:11–6:20): do not stay on milk; the danger of falling away; God's promise as an anchor of the soul.
- **The order of Melchizedek** (ch. 7): a priest who always lives to make intercession for us.
- **A better covenant** (ch. 8): God promises to write his laws on our hearts.
- **A better sacrifice** (9:1–10:18): Christ entered the holy places once for all by his own blood.
- **Drawing near with confidence** (10:19–39): since we have a new and living way, let us draw near and not neglect meeting together; with another severe warning.
- **Witnesses of faith** (ch. 11): Abel, Noah, Abraham, Moses... they all died in faith.
- **Running the race** (ch. 12): looking to Jesus; God disciplines the sons he loves; we have come to Mount Zion and received a kingdom that cannot be shaken.
- **Final exhortations and greetings** (ch. 13): love one another; go to Jesus outside the camp; Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Keep the Old Testament open.** Hebrews quotes the Old Testament constantly; chapter 1 alone quotes it seven times. The author mostly uses the Septuagint, so some quotations differ from our translations of the Old Testament (for example, 1:6, "Let all God's angels worship him," and 10:5, "a body have you prepared for me"). As you read, turn to the Old Testament passage and see how the author finds Christ there. The Lord Jesus himself did this: "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27).

**2. Watch for "better" and "once for all."** The Old Testament priests offered sacrifices "daily" and repeated them year after year (7:27; 10:1, 11); Christ offered himself "once for all" (7:27; 9:12, 26, 28; 10:10). The old was not bad; it pointed to something better. The shadow passes; the reality remains.

**3. Take the warnings seriously, and the comfort too.** Hebrews has five severe warnings (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:26–39; 12:14–29). Faithful believers have understood them, especially 6:4–6 and 10:26–27, in different ways. The Reformed tradition generally holds that those truly born again will be kept to the end, and reads these warnings either as one of the means by which God keeps his people (so Thomas Schreiner), or as aimed at those who seemed to believe but were never truly regenerate (so John Owen). The Arminian tradition generally holds that true believers can fall away from faith and be lost (so I. Howard Marshall, Scot McKnight). This study will set out these views fairly in the chapters concerned. But on either view, the author himself puts warning and comfort side by side: "Though we speak in this way, yet in your case, beloved, we feel sure of better things—things that belong to salvation" (6:9). The warnings are meant to bring people back to Christ, not to push them away.

**4. Hear it as a sermon.** This is a "word of exhortation." The author often says "we": "we must pay much closer attention to what we have heard" (2:1); "let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace" (4:16). He does not scold his readers from a distance; he runs alongside them. As you read, watch for the "therefore" after each stretch of exposition: the author first says who Christ is and what he has done, and only then says how we should live.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "It was declared at first by the Lord, and it was attested to us by those who heard" (2:3). The readers of Hebrews were second-generation believers who heard the gospel from others. Perhaps I am too. I grew up going to church with my parents; I have heard all the Sunday school stories, I can sing the hymns, and I can recite many verses. Yet sometimes this faith feels as if it belongs to my parents, or to the church: an old garment I have worn since childhood, but not quite my own. When I listen to a sermon, I often think, "I know this already." To readers like this the author does not say, "Try harder to feel it." He says, "God... in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (1:1–2). The one speaking is God himself, and his word is his Son. The stories I have grown used to were God's own words to me. At the transfiguration the Father said the same: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him" (Matt 17:5). I may have heard my faith from my parents and my church, but what saves me is not the words I grew used to as a child, nor how deeply I feel them. It is the Son who, after making purification for sins, "sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" (Heb 1:3). He has already washed away my sins, including the sin of growing cold toward him and treating his word as old news. And "he is not ashamed" to call those who trust him his brothers (2:11). If I have never trusted him for myself, today I can say to him for the first time: Lord Jesus, I do not want to lean only on my parents' faith; I want to trust you. If I trusted him long ago and have only grown cold, I can come back to him too. Today, as I begin Hebrews, I can pray before I read: Father, speak to me through your Son; not to my parents, not to others in the church, but to me. Let me see your glory again in the face of your Son.
