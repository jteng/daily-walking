## 这卷书在讲什么

雅各书一开头只有一句简短的问安：「作神和主耶稣基督仆人的雅各请散住十二个支派之人的安。」（1:1）接着第二节就直接进入正题：「我的弟兄们，你们落在百般试炼中，都要以为大喜乐。」（1:2）全书只有五章，一百零八节，却有五十多个命令句。读的人很难只是坐着听，因为这卷书一直在问：你听见了，然后呢？

这卷书最出名的一句话，大概就是：「只是你们要行道，不要单单听道，自己欺哄自己。」（1:22）雅各关心的，是信仰有没有落到日常生活里：
- **在试炼中：** 苦难会不会叫我们的信心长出忍耐（1:2-4、12）？
- **在言语上：** 我们用同一张嘴颂赞神，又咒诅照着神形象被造的人（3:9-10）。
- **在贫富之间：** 教会会不会给穿华美衣服的人好位子，叫穷人站在一边（2:1-4）？富足的人有没有亏欠工人的工钱（5:4）？
- **在信心和行为之间：** 一个人说自己有信心，却对饥寒的弟兄姊妹说「平平安安地去吧」，这信心能救他吗（2:14-17）？
- **在计划和祷告中：** 我们说「今天明天我们要往某城里去」，却忘了「主若愿意」（4:13-15）；我们在受苦、患病、犯罪的时候，是否彼此认罪，互相代求（5:13-16）？

雅各书常被看作新约中的「智慧书」，很像旧约的箴言：话短，比喻多，一句接一句，从一个题目跳到另一个题目。它也很像先知书：对欺压穷人的富人说话很重（5:1-6）。最重要的是，它很像主耶稣的登山宝训。这不奇怪，因为雅各是主的兄弟，后来又作耶路撒冷教会的领袖，传讲主的教训。

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 哪一位雅各？** 新约中有好几位雅各：
- **西庇太的儿子雅各：** 十二使徒之一，约翰的哥哥。他在主后44年左右就被希律杀了（徒12:2），一般认为不太可能写这封信。
- **亚勒腓的儿子雅各：** 也是十二使徒之一（太10:3），但新约很少提到他。
- **主的兄弟雅各：** 教会历来认为这封信是他写的，许多学者（特别是福音派学者）也这样看；就连认为这封信是后人托名写的学者，也同意信中的「雅各」是指主的兄弟。他在耶路撒冷教会是一位很重要的领袖：彼得出监以后说「你们把这事告诉雅各和众弟兄」（徒12:17）；在耶路撒冷会议上，是他作了结论（徒15:13-21）；保罗称他为「主的兄弟雅各」（加1:19），又把他和彼得、约翰一同称为「教会柱石」（加2:9）。

作者只称自己是「神和主耶稣基督仆人」，没有说明自己是谁。这正好说明他在当时的教会中很有名，一说「雅各」，大家就知道是谁。信中的希腊文写得相当好，有人因此怀疑一个加利利人能否写出这样的文字；也有一些学者（如德国的狄贝留）认为，这是后来的人借雅各的名字写的。不过，许多学者（如穆尔、戴维斯、约翰逊）指出，一世纪的加利利本来就通行希腊文，雅各也可能请人帮忙润饰文字；信中把信徒的聚会称为「会堂」（2:2），也看不出教会已经和犹太人分开，这些都很符合早期耶路撒冷教会的背景。本研读采用传统的看法。

**2. 「主的兄弟」是什么意思？** 福音书提到耶稣的「弟兄们」，其中第一个就是雅各（太13:55；可6:3）。历代教会对这个称呼有不同的理解：
- **约瑟和马利亚所生的儿子：** 这是大多数新教学者的看法，二世纪末的特土良和四世纪的赫尔维迪乌已经这样主张。按这个看法，雅各是马利亚在耶稣以后所生的，是耶稣同母的弟弟（耶稣是从圣灵感孕而生的）。他们指出，福音书和使徒行传常把这些弟兄和马利亚放在一起（可3:31；徒1:14），又说约瑟「没有和她同房，等她生了儿子」（太1:25）。
- **约瑟前妻所生的儿子：** 这是东正教的传统看法（可追溯到二世纪的《雅各原始福音》和四世纪的伊皮法纽），按这个看法，雅各是耶稣名义上的哥哥，马利亚终身是童女。他们也指出，耶稣在十字架上把母亲交托给约翰（约19:26-27），可见她没有别的儿子。
- **耶稣的表兄弟：** 这是耶柔米提出、后来天主教普遍采用的看法，「弟兄」是亲族的意思；他们指出，希伯来文和亚兰文常用「弟兄」称呼亲戚（如创13:8亚伯兰对侄子罗得说「我们是骨肉」，原文就是「我们是弟兄」）。

无论哪一种看法，雅各都是耶稣的近亲，很可能从小就认识他；按前两种看法，他更是和耶稣在同一个家里长大的。本研读采用第一种看法。

**3. 从不信到「仆人」。** 耶稣在世的时候，雅各并不信他。约翰福音说：「因为连他的弟兄说这话，是因为不信他。」（约7:5）马可福音记载，耶稣的亲属「说他癫狂了」，出来要拉住他，接着他的母亲和弟兄也来找他（可3:21、31）。可是到了五旬节以前，耶稣的弟兄已经和门徒一同祷告了（徒1:14）。中间发生了什么？保罗告诉我们：复活的耶稣「以后显给雅各看」（林前15:7）。福音书没有记载这次见面，但很可能就是这次相遇改变了雅各的一生。这个人曾经不信耶稣，家里的人甚至一度说耶稣癫狂了；后来他却称这位哥哥为「我们荣耀的主耶稣基督」（2:1），称自己为他的「仆人」（1:1）。

**4. 雅各的殉道。** 犹太史家约瑟夫记载，主后62年，大祭司亚拿努趁罗马巡抚还没有到任，把雅各交给人用石头打死；约瑟夫称他为「那称为基督的耶稣的兄弟」（《犹太古史》第二十卷第九章第一节）。二世纪的赫格西仆说，雅各因为常常跪着为百姓祷告，膝盖硬得像骆驼的膝盖，人称他为「义人雅各」（见优西比乌《教会史》第二卷第二十三章）。这些细节有传说的成分，却可以看出，雅各是一个以祷告和正直闻名的人。

**5. 写给谁？什么时候写的？** 收信人是「散住十二个支派之人」（1:1）。
- **分散的犹太信徒：** 许多学者认为，这是指司提反殉道以后，从耶路撒冷分散到各地的犹太基督徒（徒8:1；11:19）。他们在异乡多半贫穷，受富人欺压（2:6；5:4-6）。
- **整个教会：** 也有学者认为，「十二个支派」是把教会看作神的新以色列，「散住」是说信徒在世上都是寄居的（参彼前1:1）。

至于写作时间，雅各在主后62年殉道，信一定写在这以前。有些学者（如穆尔）认为写于主后40年代中期，在耶路撒冷会议（约主后49年）以前；这可以解释信中为什么完全没有提到外邦信徒和割礼的争论。若是这样，雅各书可能是新约中最早写成的一卷书。另一些学者认为写于主后50年代后期或60年代初，雅各在2:14-26是要纠正有人对保罗「因信称义」的误解。

**6. 雅各书在正典中的位置。** 教会普遍承认雅各书，经过了比较长的时间。三世纪上半叶的俄利根，是已知最早明确引用这卷书、称它为圣经的教父；四世纪初的优西比乌把这卷书列在「有争议的书卷」中，同时也说，大多数教会已经在聚会中公开诵读它（《教会史》第二卷第二十三章；第三卷第二十五章）。到了主后367年，亚他那修的第三十九封节期书信把雅各书列在新约二十七卷之中，后来东西方的教会都承认它是神所默示的圣经。

宗教改革时期，马丁·路德在1522年的新约序言中，拿雅各书和约翰福音、约翰一书、保罗书信（特别是罗马书、加拉太书、以弗所书）和彼得前书相比，说它「实在是一封稻草书信」，因为他在其中看不到福音。他在雅各书的序言中又说，这是一卷好书，「大力宣扬神的律法」，但他不认为这卷书出于使徒，又认为它把称义归于行为，与保罗相反。他把雅各书放在他所译新约的末尾，没有编号。以后的版本删去了「稻草书信」这句话；路德宗的信条（《奥斯堡信条辩护》第四条）也没有跟随他，反而正面地解释雅各书2:24。加尔文则完全接受雅各书，并且在《基督教要义》中解释，雅各和保罗并不冲突（第三卷第十七章）。

## 钥匙经文：1:2-4；2:14-17；4:6-8

**1. 「都要以为大喜乐」。** 「我的弟兄们，你们落在百般试炼中，都要以为大喜乐；因为知道你们的信心经过试验，就生忍耐。但忍耐也当成功，使你们成全、完备，毫无缺欠。」（1:2-4）雅各一开头就讲苦难。他没有说苦难本身是喜乐，而是说，神要藉着试炼成全我们的信心。这喜乐也不是假装不痛；同一封信说「你们中间有受苦的呢，他就该祷告」（5:13），又叫我们看约伯，看见主「满心怜悯，大有慈悲」（5:11）。希腊文「完全」这一组词在全书出现了好几次（和合本译作「成全」「全备」「完全」，1:4、17、25；2:22；3:2）。神要的不只是叫我们熬过试炼，而是要我们长大成熟，成为一个完整的人；而这份「全备」，雅各在1:17说，正是从众光之父那里降下来的赏赐。

**2. 「信心若没有行为就是死的」。** 「我的弟兄们，若有人说自己有信心，却没有行为，有什么益处呢？这信心能救他吗？若是弟兄或是姊妹，赤身露体，又缺了日用的饮食；你们中间有人对他们说：『平平安安地去吧！愿你们穿得暖，吃得饱』，却不给他们身体所需用的，这有什么益处呢？这样，信心若没有行为就是死的。」（2:14-17）这是全书最有名、也最常被误解的一段。雅各不是说人可以靠行为赚得救恩，而是说，只停在嘴上的「信心」根本不是救人的信心。雅各这话不是要叫在软弱中挣扎、为自己的失败难过的信徒怀疑得救，而是对那些满足于口头承认、生活怎样都无所谓的人说的；为自己的冷淡难过，本身就是活信心的记号。

**3. 「他赐更多的恩典」。** 「但他赐更多的恩典，所以经上说：神阻挡骄傲的人，赐恩给谦卑的人。故此，你们要顺服神。务要抵挡魔鬼，魔鬼就必离开你们逃跑了。你们亲近神，神就必亲近你们。有罪的人哪，要洁净你们的手！心怀二意的人哪，要清洁你们的心！」（4:6-8）雅各对读者说了很重的话，称他们为「淫乱的人」（4:4），可是紧接着就说：「他赐更多的恩典。」我们的罪多，神的恩典更多。雅各书所有的命令，都要放在这句话的光中来读。

## 全书结构

雅各书不像罗马书那样一步一步地推论，而是围绕几个主题，反复回来讲。学者对它的结构看法很多，大致可以这样分：
- **问安**（1:1）。
- **试炼、智慧和真正的虔诚**（1:2-27）：在试炼中喜乐；缺少智慧的，应当求神；试探不是从神来的，各样美善的恩赐都是从上头来的；要快快地听，慢慢地说，要行道，不要单单听道。这一章像全书的目录，后面各章的题目几乎都在这里出现了。
- **不可偏心，信心要有行为**（2:1-26）：不可按着外貌待人；要全守「爱人如己」这至尊的律法；信心若没有行为就是死的，亚伯拉罕和喇合都是例子。
- **舌头和两种智慧**（3:1-18）：舌头虽小，却像火；从上头来的智慧先是清洁，后是和平。
- **争战的根源和谦卑的路**（4:1-17）：争战是从私欲来的；与世俗为友就是与神为敌；但神赐更多的恩典；不可彼此批评；不可说「明天我们要……」而忘了「主若愿意」。
- **富人、忍耐和祷告**（5:1-20）：欺压工人的富人当哭泣；要忍耐等候主来；不可起誓；受苦的要祷告，有病的要请长老来；义人祈祷大有功效；要使失迷的人回转。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 一段一段地读，像读箴言。** 雅各书的话很浓缩，一节就可以默想很久。读的时候不要急着读完，可以每次只读一小段，问自己：这句话，我在哪里还没有活出来？也要问：这句话让我看见，神是一位怎样的神？

**2. 听主耶稣的声音。** 雅各书从来没有明说是在引用耶稣的话，却处处回响着登山宝训。比如：
- 在试炼中喜乐（1:2；太5:11-12）；
- 祈求，就必赐给他（1:5；太7:7）；
- 神拣选贫穷人承受他的国（2:5；太5:3）；
- 不要论断人（4:11-12；太7:1）；
- 财物坏了，衣服被虫子咬了（5:2-3；太6:19）；
- 不可起誓，是就说是，不是就说不是（5:12；太5:34-37）。

登山宝训的结尾说：「凡听见我这话就去行的，好比一个聪明人，把房子盖在磐石上。」（太7:24）雅各书可以说是这句话的长篇注释。

**3. 把雅各和保罗放在一起读。** 保罗说：「人称义是因着信，不在乎遵行律法。」（罗3:28）雅各说：「人称义是因着行为，不是单因着信。」（2:24）这两句话好像正面冲突。历代信徒有不同的解释：
- **改革宗和路德宗传统：** 保罗讲的是人怎样在神面前被算为义，根据单单是基督，途径单单是信心；雅各讲的是这信心是不是真的，真信心必然生出行为来。加尔文认为，雅各所说的「称义」是指人的义显明出来，不是指神把义归算给人（《基督教要义》第三卷第十七章第十一至十二节）。他在《天特会议决议的解毒剂》（1547年）中有一句常被引用的话：叫人称义的只是信心，但叫人称义的信心不是孤单的。也有不少福音派解经家（如穆尔）认为，雅各所说的「称义」是指神在末日审判时的宣告，那时行为是真信心的凭据，而不是称义的根据。另外，雅各在这里所批评的「信」，是连鬼魔也有的那种头脑上的相信（2:19），不是保罗所说的投靠基督的信心。
- **天主教传统：** 天特会议教导，人最初的称义是白白得的恩典（第六会期，第八章）；又引用雅各书2:24，教导人称义以后，可以藉着遵守神和教会的诫命、行善，使所领受的义「增长」，「更加称义」（第十章）。
- **两人的对手不同：** 许多解经家也指出，两人所面对的错误不同。保罗反对的，是以为靠遵守律法（如受割礼）就能在神面前称义；他排除的也不只是割礼这类礼仪，而是人一切的行为，没有一样能成为称义的根据（罗4:4-5；弗2:9「也不是出于行为」）。雅各反对的，是以为口里承认就够了，怎样生活都无所谓。保罗所说的「行为」是「遵行律法」（罗3:28），雅各所说的行为是信心生出来的爱心行动。保罗自己也说，在基督里有功效的是「使人生发仁爱的信心」（加5:6），又说我们得救是本乎恩，也因着信，而我们是在基督耶稣里造成的，「为要叫我们行善」（弗2:8-10）。

本研读在第二章会更仔细地讲这个问题。

**4. 在命令后面看见那位赐恩的神。** 雅各书只直接提到耶稣基督的名字两次（1:1；2:1），所以有人觉得它的福音不够清楚。可是雅各所讲的神，是「厚赐与众人、也不斥责人的神」（1:5），是「众光之父」，从他那里降下各样美善的恩赐（1:17），是「按自己的旨意，用真道生了我们」的神（1:18），是「满心怜悯，大有慈悲」的主（5:11）。雅各又称耶稣为「荣耀的主」（2:1），说「主来的日子近了」（5:8），把旧约归给神的称呼用在耶稣身上。他又说「怜悯原是向审判夸胜」（2:13），「他赐更多的恩典」（4:6）。雅各的命令不是叫我们靠自己去赚得神的爱，而是告诉那些神已经用真道生了的人（1:18），新生命是什么样子。读的时候如果觉得自己处处做不到，那正是要我们像4:6-10所说的，谦卑下来，为自己的罪忧伤，回到神面前去。

## 默想

**默想：** 「作神和主耶稣基督仆人的雅各。」（1:1）读雅各书，我有时候会有点怕。这卷书好像一份体检报告，一项一项地标出问题：你在试炼中喜乐吗？你勒住舌头了吗？你偏心待人吗？你的信心有行为吗？读完一章，满纸都是红色的箭头。可是写这封信的雅各，并不是一个从小就做得很好的人。他和耶稣在同一个家里长大，却不信耶稣；家里的人还一度说耶稣「癫狂了」（可3:21），雅各很可能也在其中。他后来能写出这些话，不是因为他比别人更努力，而是因为复活的耶稣亲自来找他：「以后显给雅各看。」（林前15:7）圣经没有记载耶稣责备他，也没有记载耶稣问他：你从前为什么不信我？只说他「显给雅各看」，活着站在雅各面前。所以雅各写信的时候，没有称自己是「主的兄弟」，那是他最可以夸口的身份；他只说自己是「仆人」。也许正因为他知道，他的一切都是恩典。这样，这封信里的每一个命令，都是一个被恩典找到的人写的。所以，读到自己做不到的地方，我不必把这卷书合上。雅各自己在4:6说：「但他赐更多的恩典。」他没有等雅各先变好了才来找他，也没有等我。不管我是信主多年、却越读越心虚，还是到今天都还没有真正认识他，那位向雅各显现的主，今天也藉着这卷书向我说话：他为我的罪死了，又复活了，他「赐恩给谦卑的人」（4:6）。我能做的，就是像雅各一样，以仆人的身份谦卑地来到他面前，承认自己的罪。雅各的家人曾说这位哥哥癫狂了，雅各后来却称他为「我们荣耀的主耶稣基督」（2:1）。开始读这卷书，我也想先这样称呼他：他不只是给我看化验结果的医生，也不只是我要学的榜样，他是荣耀的主。这五章里的每一个命令，都是他的声音；而他自己，比我能做到的一切都更宝贵。

## What This Book Is About

James opens with a single short greeting: "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, To the twelve tribes in the Dispersion: Greetings" (1:1). Verse 2 goes straight to the point: "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds" (1:2). The letter has only five chapters and 108 verses, yet more than fifty commands. It is hard to read it while sitting back and only listening, because it keeps asking: you have heard this, so what now?

The letter's best-known sentence is probably this: "But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves" (1:22). James cares whether faith reaches everyday life:
- **In trials:** will suffering make our faith grow steadfast (1:2–4, 12)?
- **In speech:** with the same mouth we bless God and curse people made in his likeness (3:9–10).
- **Between rich and poor:** will the church give the man in fine clothing a good seat and tell the poor man to stand to one side (2:1–4)? Have the rich kept back the wages of their laborers (5:4)?
- **Between faith and works:** if someone says he has faith, yet says "Go in peace" to a brother or sister without food or clothing, can that faith save him (2:14–17)?
- **In our plans and prayers:** we say, "Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a town," and forget "If the Lord wills" (4:13–15). When we suffer, are sick or have sinned, do we pray for one another (5:13–16)?

James is often called the "wisdom book" of the New Testament. It is much like Proverbs: short sayings, many images, one line after another, moving from one subject to the next. It is also like the prophets: it speaks very sharply to the rich who oppress the poor (5:1–6). Above all, it is like the Lord Jesus' Sermon on the Mount. That is no surprise, since James was the Lord's brother and later received and passed on Jesus' teaching as a leader of the Jerusalem church.

## Author and Setting

**1. Which James?** The New Testament mentions several men named James:
- **James the son of Zebedee:** one of the Twelve, John's brother. He was put to death by Herod around AD 44 (Acts 12:2), so he is generally thought unlikely to have written this letter.
- **James the son of Alphaeus:** also one of the Twelve (Matt 10:3), but the New Testament says little about him.
- **James the Lord's brother:** this is the church's traditional view and that of many scholars, especially evangelicals; even scholars who think the letter pseudonymous agree that the "James" it names is the Lord's brother. He was a leading figure in the Jerusalem church. When Peter was released from prison, he said, "Tell these things to James and to the brothers" (Acts 12:17). At the Jerusalem council, it was James who gave the decision (Acts 15:13–21). Paul calls him "James the Lord's brother" (Gal 1:19) and, with Peter and John, one of the "pillars" (Gal 2:9).

The writer calls himself only "a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ," without explaining who he is. That itself suggests he was well known in the church of his day: say "James," and everyone knew whom you meant. The letter's Greek is fairly polished, and some have doubted that a Galilean could write it; some scholars (for example, Martin Dibelius in Germany) have held that a later writer used James's name. Many others (for example, Douglas Moo, Peter Davids and Luke Timothy Johnson) point out that Greek was widely used in first-century Galilee, and that James may also have had help with the wording. The setting of the letter (the "assembly," literally "synagogue," 2:2; no sign yet of the church separated from the Jewish community) fits the early Jerusalem church well. This study follows the traditional view.

**2. What does "the Lord's brother" mean?** The Gospels mention Jesus' "brothers," and James is named first (Matt 13:55; Mark 6:3). The church has understood the term in different ways:
- **A son of Joseph and Mary:** this is the view of most Protestant scholars (held by Tertullian in the late second century and by Helvidius in the fourth): James was a son born to Mary later, Jesus' younger half-brother (Jesus having been conceived by the Holy Spirit). They note that the Gospels and Acts often place these brothers alongside Mary (Mark 3:31; Acts 1:14), and that Joseph "knew her not until she had given birth to a son" (Matt 1:25).
- **A son of Joseph by an earlier marriage:** this is the traditional Eastern Orthodox view (traceable to the second-century *Protoevangelium of James* and to Epiphanius in the fourth century): James was Jesus' older brother in name. Those who hold it believe Mary remained a virgin all her life, and note that from the cross Jesus entrusted his mother to John (John 19:26–27), which suggests she had no other sons.
- **A cousin of Jesus:** this view was proposed by Jerome and later widely adopted in the Catholic Church: "brother" means a relative. Its holders note that Hebrew and Aramaic often use "brother" for kin (as in Gen 13:8, where Abram calls his nephew Lot a "kinsman," literally "brothers").

On any of these views, James was a close relative who likely knew Jesus from childhood; on the first two, he grew up in the same household. This study follows the first view.

**3. From unbelief to "servant."** While Jesus was on earth, James did not believe in him. John says, "For not even his brothers believed in him" (John 7:5). Mark records that Jesus' family "went out to seize him, for they were saying, 'He is out of his mind,'" and then his mother and brothers came looking for him (Mark 3:21, 31). Yet before Pentecost, Jesus' brothers were already praying with the disciples (Acts 1:14). What happened in between? Paul tells us: the risen Jesus "appeared to James" (1 Cor 15:7). The Gospels do not record that meeting, but very likely it was this meeting that changed James's life. The man who once did not believe in his brother, whose family even said at one point that Jesus was out of his mind, later called that brother "our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory" (2:1), and called himself his "servant" (1:1).

**4. James's martyrdom.** The Jewish historian Josephus records that in AD 62, while the new Roman governor had not yet arrived, the high priest Ananus had "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James" handed over to be stoned (*Antiquities* 20.9.1, §200). In the second century, Hegesippus said that James knelt so often in prayer for the people that his knees grew hard like a camel's, and that he was called "James the Just" (in Eusebius, *Church History* 2.23). These details have legendary elements, but they show that James was known for prayer and uprightness.

**5. To whom and when?** The letter is addressed "to the twelve tribes in the Dispersion" (1:1).
- **Scattered Jewish believers:** many scholars take this to mean the Jewish Christians who were scattered from Jerusalem after Stephen's death (Acts 8:1; 11:19). Living away from home, they were mostly poor and oppressed by the rich (2:6; 5:4–6).
- **The whole church:** other scholars think "the twelve tribes" describes the church as God's new Israel, and "the Dispersion" means believers live as exiles in the world (see 1 Pet 1:1).

As for the date, James died in AD 62, so the letter was written before then. Some scholars (for example, Moo) date it to the mid-40s, before the Jerusalem council (around AD 49), which would explain why it says nothing about Gentile believers or the dispute over circumcision; it may then be the earliest book of the New Testament. Others date it to the late 50s or early 60s and think that in 2:14–26 James is correcting a misunderstanding of Paul's teaching on justification by faith.

**6. James in the canon.** James was accepted across the whole church more slowly than some books. In the first half of the third century, Origen was the earliest known church father to cite it explicitly by name as Scripture. In the early fourth century, Eusebius listed it among the "disputed" books, while noting that it was read publicly in most churches (*Church History* 2.23; 3.25). In AD 367, Athanasius's thirty-ninth Festal Letter listed it among the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, and churches East and West came to receive it as inspired Scripture.

At the Reformation, Martin Luther's 1522 preface to the New Testament compared James with John's Gospel and first letter, Paul's letters (especially Romans, Galatians and Ephesians) and 1 Peter, and called it "really an epistle of straw," because he could not find the gospel in it. In his preface to James he also called it a good book that "vigorously promulgates the law of God," but he did not regard it as the work of an apostle, and thought it ascribed justification to works against Paul; he placed it, unnumbered, at the end of his New Testament. Later editions dropped the "epistle of straw" sentence. The later Lutheran confessions (the Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article IV) did not follow him, and expound James 2:24 positively. Calvin, by contrast, accepted James fully, and in the *Institutes* explained that James and Paul do not conflict (Book 3, chapter 17).

## Key Verses: 1:2–4; 2:14–17; 4:6–8

**1. "Count it all joy."** "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness. And let steadfastness have its full effect, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing" (1:2–4). James begins with suffering. He does not say suffering itself is joy; he says God uses trials to complete our faith. Nor is this joy a matter of pretending it does not hurt. The same letter says, "Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray" (5:13), and points us to Job and to the Lord who is "compassionate and merciful" (5:11). The Greek word group for "perfect" (complete, mature) appears several times in the letter (1:4, 17, 25; 2:22, "completed"; 3:2). God does not only want us to get through trials; he wants us mature and whole. And that completeness, James says in 1:17, is itself a "perfect gift" coming down from the Father of lights.

**2. "Faith... if it does not have works, is dead."** "What good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him? If a brother or sister is poorly clothed and lacking in daily food, and one of you says to them, 'Go in peace, be warmed and filled,' without giving them the things needed for the body, what good is that? So also faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead" (2:14–17). This is the letter's best-known passage, and the most often misunderstood. James is not saying that people can earn salvation by works. He is saying that a "faith" that stays on the lips is not saving faith at all. James is not trying to make a struggling believer, grieved by his failures, doubt his salvation; he is speaking to those content with a profession on the lips, however they live. Grieving over one's coldness is itself a sign of living faith.

**3. "He gives more grace."** "But he gives more grace. Therefore it says, 'God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble.' Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-minded" (4:6–8). James has just used very strong words, calling his readers "adulterous people" (4:4), yet at once he says, "But he gives more grace." Our sins are many; God's grace is more. Every command in James is to be read beneath this sentence.

## Structure of the Book

James does not argue step by step the way Romans does. It circles around a few themes and keeps coming back to them. Scholars have proposed many outlines; one simple way to divide it is:
- **Greeting** (1:1).
- **Trials, wisdom and true religion** (1:2–27): joy in trials; whoever lacks wisdom should ask God; temptation does not come from God, but every good gift comes from above; be quick to hear and slow to speak; be doers of the word, not hearers only. This chapter works like a table of contents: nearly every later theme appears here.
- **No partiality; faith that works** (2:1–26): do not show favoritism; fulfill the royal law, "You shall love your neighbor as yourself"; faith without works is dead, as Abraham and Rahab show.
- **The tongue and two kinds of wisdom** (3:1–18): the tongue is small, but it is a fire; the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable.
- **Where quarrels come from, and the way of humility** (4:1–17): quarrels come from our passions; friendship with the world is enmity with God; but he gives more grace; do not speak evil against one another; do not say "Tomorrow we will..." and forget "If the Lord wills."
- **The rich, patience and prayer** (5:1–20): the rich who defraud their workers should weep; be patient until the Lord comes; do not swear; let the suffering pray and the sick call the elders; the prayer of a righteous person has great power; bring back the one who wanders.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Read it a little at a time, like Proverbs.** James is dense; a single verse can be pondered for a long time. Do not rush to finish. Read a short passage at a time and ask: where am I not yet living this out? And ask too: what does this verse show me about the God who gives?

**2. Listen for the voice of Jesus.** James never explicitly cites Jesus, but the Sermon on the Mount echoes throughout. For example:
- joy in trials (1:2; Matt 5:11–12);
- ask, and it will be given (1:5; Matt 7:7);
- God chose the poor to inherit his kingdom (2:5; Matt 5:3);
- do not judge (4:11–12; Matt 7:1);
- riches rotted, garments moth-eaten (5:2–3; Matt 6:19);
- do not swear; let your yes be yes and your no be no (5:12; Matt 5:34–37).

The Sermon on the Mount ends, "Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them will be like a wise man who built his house on the rock" (Matt 7:24). James could be called a long commentary on that sentence.

**3. Read James alongside Paul.** Paul says, "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Rom 3:28). James says, "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (2:24). The two sentences seem to collide head-on. Christians have explained them in different ways:
- **The Reformed and Lutheran traditions:** Paul speaks of how a person is counted righteous before God, on the ground of Christ alone, through faith alone; James speaks of whether that faith is real, since true faith always produces works. Calvin held that when James speaks of being "justified," he means righteousness being shown, not righteousness being imputed (*Institutes* 3.17.11–12). In his *Antidote to the Council of Trent* (1547) he wrote a line often quoted: it is faith alone that justifies, and yet the faith that justifies is not alone. Many evangelical interpreters (Moo, for example) take James's "justified" to refer to God's verdict at the final judgment, where works are the evidence of genuine faith, not its ground. And the "faith" James criticizes here is the bare assent even demons have (2:19), not the trusting faith in Christ that Paul means.
- **The Catholic tradition:** the Council of Trent teaches that initial justification is a free gift of grace (Session 6, chapter 8), and cites James 2:24 to teach that the justified, by keeping the commandments of God and of the Church and doing good works, "increase" in the righteousness they have received and "are further justified" (chapter 10).
- **Different opponents:** many interpreters also point out that Paul opposes those who think keeping the law (such as circumcision) saves a person before God, and he excludes not only rituals like circumcision but every human work as the ground of justification (Rom 4:4–5; Eph 2:9, "not a result of works"); while James opposes those who think that a profession on the lips is enough, however one lives. Paul's "works" are "works of the law" (Rom 3:28); James's works are the deeds of love that faith produces. Paul himself says that what counts in Christ is "faith working through love" (Gal 5:6), and that we are saved by grace through faith, yet "created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Eph 2:8–10).

This study looks at the question more closely in chapter 2.

**4. Behind the commands, see the God who gives grace.** James names Jesus Christ only twice (1:1; 2:1), so some feel its gospel is unclear. Yet the God James speaks of is the God "who gives generously to all without reproach" (1:5); the "Father of lights," from whom every good gift comes down (1:17); the God who "of his own will... brought us forth by the word of truth" (1:18); the Lord who is "compassionate and merciful" (5:11). James also calls Jesus "the Lord of glory" (2:1) and speaks of "the coming of the Lord" (5:8), applying to Jesus the title the Old Testament gives to God. He also says, "Mercy triumphs over judgment" (2:13), and "he gives more grace" (4:6). James's commands do not tell us to earn God's love by our own effort. They tell those whom God has already brought to birth what the new life looks like. If, as you read, you find you fall short at every turn, that is exactly when to humble yourself, grieve over your sin, and come back to God, as 4:6–10 says.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ" (1:1). Reading James, I am sometimes a little afraid. The book feels like a medical report, flagging one item after another: Do you count trials as joy? Do you bridle your tongue? Do you show partiality? Does your faith have works? By the end of a chapter the page is full of red marks. But James, who wrote this letter, was not someone who had always done well. He grew up in the same home as Jesus, yet did not believe in him; at one point his family even said Jesus was "out of his mind" (Mark 3:21), and James was very likely among them. He could later write these words, not because he tried harder than others, but because the risen Jesus came looking for him: "Then he appeared to James" (1 Cor 15:7). Scripture records no rebuke, no question like "Why did you not believe me before?" It says only that he "appeared to James," alive before him. So when James wrote, he did not call himself "the Lord's brother," the title he could most have boasted in. He called himself a "servant." Perhaps because he knew it was all grace. Every command in this letter, then, was written by someone whom grace had found. So when I reach the places where I fall short, I do not need to close the book. James himself says in 4:6, "But he gives more grace." He did not wait for James to improve before he came looking for him, and he has not waited for me. Whether I have believed for years and feel more exposed the more I read, or have never yet truly known him, the Lord who appeared to James speaks to me through this book too: he died for my sins and rose again, and he "gives grace to the humble" (4:6). What I can do is what James did: come to him humbly as a servant, confessing my sin. James's family once said this brother was out of his mind; later James called him "our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory" (2:1). As I begin this book, I want to call him that first: not only the doctor who reads out my results, not only the example I should copy, but the Lord of glory. Every command in these five chapters is his voice, and he himself is worth more than anything I could ever manage to do.
