## 本章脉络

第五章是全书的最后一章，可以分成四段。**警告欺压人的富人**（1-6节）：富足人应当哭泣、号咷；他们的财物坏了，金银长了锈；他们亏欠工人的工钱，那工钱有声音呼叫，收割之人的冤声已经入了万军之主的耳；他们定了义人的罪，把他杀害。**忍耐等候主来**（7-12节）：要像农夫等候秋雨春雨一样忍耐，坚固自己的心，因为主来的日子近了；不要彼此埋怨，审判的主站在门前了；要以先知和约伯为榜样，因为主是满心怜悯，大有慈悲；最要紧的是不可起誓，是就说是，不是就说不是。**祷告的群体**（13-18节）：受苦的要祷告，喜乐的要歌颂，有病的要请教会的长老来；要彼此认罪，互相代求；义人祈祷所发的力量是大有功效的，以利亚就是这样。**使失迷的人回转**（19-20节）：叫一个罪人从迷路上转回，便是救一个灵魂不死，并且遮盖许多的罪。

这一章把全书收拢起来。开头讲试炼中的忍耐（1:2-4），结尾又回到忍耐（5:7-11）；开头说缺少智慧的要求神（1:5），结尾说受苦的、有病的都要祷告（5:13-16）；开头讲卑微的和富足的（1:9-11），这里又对富人说话（5:1-6）。雅各书结尾没有一般书信常见的问安和祝福，而是以一个使命作结：去把迷路的人带回来。

## 深读 5:1-6 对富人的警告

**1. 「你们的财物坏了」。** 「嗐！你们这些富足人哪，应当哭泣、号咷，因为将有苦难临到你们身上。你们的财物坏了，衣服也被虫子咬了。你们的金银都长了锈；那锈要证明你们的不是，又要吃你们的肉，如同火烧。你们在这末世只知积攒钱财。」（5:1-3）
- **先知的口吻：** 这段话很像旧约先知对以色列富人的审判（参赛5:8-9；摩6:1-7）。雅各说的这些富人，多半不是教会里的人，因为他没有称他们为「弟兄」。可是他把这些话写给教会听，是要信徒看清楚：不要羡慕他们，也不要变成他们。
- **会坏的财宝：** 主耶稣说：「不要为自己积攒财宝在地上；地上有虫子咬，能锈坏。」（太6:19）金子其实不会生锈，银子也不会锈烂；雅各是用先知式的图画说：人以为最不会坏的财宝，在神的审判面前也是会朽坏的，那锈反倒成了控告他们的证据。
- **「在这末世」：** 新约说，从基督第一次来，就已经是末世了（来1:2）。在末世只知道积攒钱财的人，就像洪水已经到了门口，还在忙着盖仓房。

**2. 「亏欠他们的工钱」。** 「工人给你们收割庄稼，你们亏欠他们的工钱，这工钱有声音呼叫，并且那收割之人的冤声已经入了万军之主的耳了。你们在世上享美福，好宴乐，当宰杀的日子竟娇养你们的心。」（5:4-5）
- **律法的要求：** 律法吩咐：「雇工人的工价，不可在你那里过夜，留到早晨。」（利19:13）「要当日给他工价……恐怕他因你求告耶和华，罪便归你了。」（申24:15）先知也把「起假誓的、亏负人之工价的」放在一起（玛3:5）。
- **「入了万军之主的耳」：** 世人听不见欠薪工人的哭声，神却听见了，就像亚伯的血「有声音从地里向我哀告」（创4:10）。「万军之主」是一个很有分量的称呼：替这些工人伸冤的，是统管天上万军的神。
- **今天的样子：** 拖欠工资、克扣血汗钱、不给保险、欺负工人没有合法身份不敢声张而压低工钱，这些在今天并不少见，有时老板还是基督徒；在华人移民圈子里，同胞老板欠新移民工钱的事也时有所闻。被欠薪的人可以寻求法律保护，向劳工部门或法院申诉（罗13:4）；这不是不忍耐，而是寻求公义。若你是雇主，悔改就包括把欠的工钱补上；撒该是在主耶稣先到他家以后，才说「我若讹诈了谁，就还他四倍」（路19:5-8）。
- **「当宰杀的日子」：** 好像牲畜宰杀之前，先被养得肥肥的。富人以为自己在享福，其实是在为审判的日子养肥自己的心（参耶12:3「叫他们等候杀戮的日子」）。

**3. 「你们定了义人的罪」。** 「你们定了义人的罪，把他杀害，他也不抵挡你们。」（5:6）
- **「义人」是谁？** 多数解经家认为，这是泛指被富人欺压、无力抵抗的穷苦义人（参2:6「拉你们到公堂去」）。也有一些古代解经家认为，这里暗指主耶稣，「那义者」被定罪钉死，「他也不抵挡」（参徒7:52）；还有人认为，这句话也是对雅各自己将来殉道的预言。
- **「他也不抵挡」：** 这不是说不义的事无所谓，而是说，穷人无力抵挡，所以神要亲自为他们伸冤。这是多数解经家的读法；也有解经家把这句读成问句：「他（神）岂不抵挡你们吗？」因为这里的「抵挡」和4:6「神阻挡骄傲的人」的「阻挡」，原文是同一个词。

> 要点：神听见被欺压之人的冤声。会朽坏的财物不能救人，只知积攒钱财的人，是在为审判的日子养肥自己的心。可是在基督里，我们有一份「不能朽坏、不能玷污、不能衰残」的基业（彼前1:4），所以不必羡慕他们；若我亏欠了人，也可以像撒该一样补上，因为主先到了他家。

## 深读 5:7-12 忍耐等候主来

**1. 「看哪，农夫忍耐等候」。** 「弟兄们哪，你们要忍耐，直到主来。看哪，农夫忍耐等候地里宝贵的出产，直到得了秋雨春雨。你们也当忍耐，坚固你们的心，因为主来的日子近了。」（5:7-8）
- **「所以」：** 原文开头有「所以」（和合本没有译出）。因为神必为受欺压的人伸冤（1-6节），所以信徒不必自己报复，可以忍耐等候。
- **秋雨春雨：** 在巴勒斯坦，秋雨在十月、十一月来，叫刚撒的种子发芽；春雨在三月、四月来，叫庄稼饱满成熟。这是神在约中的应许：「他必按时降秋雨春雨在你们的地上。」（申11:14；参珥2:23）农夫不能叫雨早一点来，他能做的，就是撒了种，然后等候。
- **「主来的日子近了」：** 这里的「主」，多数解经家认为是指主耶稣的再来。新约的作者常说主来的日子「近了」（腓4:5；彼前4:7）。这不是说他们算错了日子，而是说，从基督复活升天以后，主的再来随时可能发生，信徒要天天带着这个盼望生活。主好像耽延，「其实不是耽延，乃是宽容你们」（彼后3:9）。

**2. 「审判的主站在门前了」。** 「弟兄们，你们不要彼此埋怨，免得受审判。看哪，审判的主站在门前了。」（5:9）
- **受苦的人容易埋怨：** 在外面受了欺压，回到弟兄姊妹中间，就容易彼此抱怨、迁怒，就像在外面受了气，回家拿家人出气。雅各提醒读者：伸冤是神的事，我们不可在彼此身上出气。
- **「站在门前」：** 主耶稣也说：「你们看见这一切的事，也该知道人子近了，正在门口了。」（太24:33）这是警告：我们彼此埋怨的话，那位审判的主都听见了；信徒也要站在基督台前（林后5:10）。可是这位审判者也正是那位对冷淡的教会说「看哪，我站在门外叩门」的主（启3:20），他来，是要我们悔改，与他一同坐席。

**3. 「主是满心怜悯，大有慈悲」。** 「弟兄们，你们要把那先前奉主名说话的众先知当作能受苦能忍耐的榜样。那先前忍耐的人，我们称他们是有福的。你们听见过约伯的忍耐，也知道主给他的结局，明显主是满心怜悯，大有慈悲。」（5:10-11）
- **先知的榜样：** 主耶稣说：「在你们以前的先知，人也是这样逼迫他们。」（太5:12）
- **约伯的「忍耐」：** 约伯不是一个默默不语的人，他哭诉，他质问，他甚至咒诅自己的生日（伯3:1）。可是他没有离开神。他起初说：「赏赐的是耶和华，收取的也是耶和华。耶和华的名是应当称颂的。」（伯1:21）他在痛苦中仍然向神说话；神最后也说，约伯议论他是对的（伯42:7）。所以雅各所说的忍耐，不是没有眼泪，而是在眼泪中仍然紧紧抓住神。
- **「主给他的结局」：** 「结局」原文也有目的、终点的意思。多数解经家认为，这是指神最后使约伯「从苦境转回」，赐给他加倍的福（伯42:10）。也有一些早期解经家（例如奥古斯丁）把它和主耶稣的受苦与复活连起来。无论哪一种，雅各的结论是：「主是满心怜悯，大有慈悲」，这是旧约对神的称颂（出34:6；诗103:8）。苦难不是故事的结局，神的怜悯才是。

**4. 「最要紧的是不可起誓」。** 「我的弟兄们，最要紧的是不可起誓；不可指着天起誓，也不可指着地起誓，无论何誓都不可起。你们说话，是，就说是；不是，就说不是，免得你们落在审判之下。」（5:12）
- **主耶稣的话：** 这几乎就是主耶稣在登山宝训中的话（太5:34-37）。
- **为什么放在这里？** 受苦的人可能在急躁中随口发誓，或者指着天地起誓来加重自己的话。华人常说「我对天发誓」「骗你天打雷劈」，好像不这样说，别人就不信。雅各要信徒说话诚实，一句「是」就够了，不需要靠起誓来叫人相信。
- **不同的理解：** 历代信徒对这节经文有不同的应用。重洗派传统（如1527年《施莱特海姆信条》第七条、门诺会）和贵格会认为，基督徒任何时候都不可起誓，包括在法庭上。多数教会传统（如《威斯敏斯德信条》第二十二章、《奥斯堡信条》第十六条、《天主教教理》2154条）认为，这里禁止的是日常生活中随便起誓、用誓言来逃避责任，并不禁止在法庭等庄严的场合合法地起誓；他们指出，神自己也起誓（来6:13），保罗也呼求神作见证（林后1:23）。两种看法都同意：基督徒的话，本身就要可信。

> 要点：神必为受欺压的人伸冤，所以我们可以像农夫一样忍耐等候。忍耐不是没有眼泪，而是在眼泪中仍然抓住那位「满心怜悯，大有慈悲」的主。

## 深读 5:13-18 祷告的群体

**1. 「受苦的……就该祷告」。** 「你们中间有受苦的呢，他就该祷告；有喜乐的呢，他就该歌颂。」（5:13）
- **无论什么光景，都转向神：** 受苦的时候，不要只是埋怨（5:9），要祷告；喜乐的时候，不要忘了神，要歌颂。整本诗篇就是这样的书：有哀歌，也有赞美。

**2. 「请教会的长老来」。** 「你们中间有病了的呢，他就该请教会的长老来；他们可以奉主的名用油抹他，为他祷告。出于信心的祈祷要救那病人，主必叫他起来；他若犯了罪，也必蒙赦免。」（5:14-15）
- **病人主动「请」：** 生病的人要请长老来，不必因为怕麻烦人、怕丢面子而独自承受。教会的长老也要把探望病人当作自己的职分。
- **「用油抹他」：** 历代教会对这里的抹油有不同的理解。有人认为，油在当时也是药物（参路10:34），所以这是祷告和医治并用。也有人认为，抹油是一个记号，表示把病人分别出来，交在神的手中（参可6:13）。天主教以这节经文为病人傅油圣事的根据之一，天特会议（第十四会期）称之为「终傅」；东正教也有类似的「圣傅」礼。新教的改革宗和路德宗传统一般不把它看作圣礼；加尔文认为，抹油是和初期教会医病的恩赐连在一起的，那恩赐已经过去，但为病人祷告仍然是教会的职责。许多五旬宗和灵恩派教会今天仍然照字面实行抹油祷告。
- **「出于信心的祈祷要救那病人」：** 「救」在这里主要是指病得医治，「叫他起来」就是叫他从病床上起来。这并不是说，只要信心够大，病就一定会好。雅各在这里说的，是那些祷告的长老的信心；他没有把重担压在病人身上。出于信心的祈祷，是把病人交在神手中、顺服他旨意的祷告（4:15「主若愿意」；约一5:14）。神有时在今生就叫病人起来；有时没有，像保罗三次求主除去身上的刺，主却说：「我的恩典够你用的。」（林后12:8-9）保罗也曾把生病的特罗非摩留在米利都（提后4:20）。我们不可以因为病没有好，就断定是病人或为他祷告的人信心不够。到了复活的日子，主必叫他所有的百姓都起来。为病人祷告，也不是叫人拒绝看医生、不吃药。
- **「他若犯了罪」：** 「若」字很重要。有些病和罪有关（参林前11:30），但不是所有的病都是因为犯罪（约9:3）。教会不可对病人说：「你一定是犯了什么罪。」

**3. 「你们要彼此认罪，互相代求」。** 「所以你们要彼此认罪，互相代求，使你们可以得医治。义人祈祷所发的力量是大有功效的。」（5:16）
- **彼此认罪：** 认罪首先是向神：「我们若认自己的罪，神是信实的，是公义的，必要赦免我们的罪。」（约一1:9）可是神也把我们放在一个群体里，叫我们可以向一两位可信任的弟兄姊妹说出自己的挣扎，请他们为我们祷告。这不是要人向所有人公开自己的一切，而是在安全、彼此相爱的关系中，不再独自隐藏。我们常把认罪看成丢脸；可是在神面前，隐藏才是真正的羞耻，认罪才是蒙怜悯的门。「彼此」是互相的；改教家不把这节经文理解为必须向神父私下告解（加尔文《基督教要义》第三卷第四章第六节）。
- **认罪不能代替法律：** 如果所认的罪涉及虐待、性侵或其他犯罪，教会不可只是私下处理、为他祷告了事，必须保护受害的人，并且向警察或有关部门举报（罗13:4）。认罪和得赦免，并不免除一个人在法律上的责任，也不等于他可以恢复职分，或再接触受害的人和孩子。
- **「义人」：** 雅各说的义人，是旧约意义上的义人：在神面前活得正直、敬畏他、肯认罪悔改的人（参箴15:29「耶和华远离恶人，却听义人的祷告」）。这样的义人不是从来不犯罪的完人，因为雅各刚刚才说「彼此认罪」。这样的生命，是从神白白的恩典来的：人先因信靠基督被称为义，才能活出义人的样子（参2:21-23）。他的祷告有力量，不是因为他自己有功劳，而是因为他所求告的神是信实的。

**4. 「以利亚与我们是一样性情的人」。** 「以利亚与我们是一样性情的人，他恳切祷告，求不要下雨，雨就三年零六个月不下在地上。他又祷告，天就降下雨来，地也生出土产。」（5:17-18）
- **为什么举以利亚？** 以利亚是旧约中最有能力的先知之一，可是雅各特意说，他「与我们是一样性情的人」。就在迦密山上求火、求雨以后，他因为害怕耶洗别逃到旷野，坐在罗腾树下求死：「耶和华啊，罢了！求你取我的性命。」（王上19:4）所以，祷告大有功效，不是因为祷告的人有什么过人之处。
- **「三年零六个月」：** 列王纪没有直接记载以利亚求不下雨。他对亚哈说「这几年我若不祷告，必不降露，不下雨」（王上17:1，原文直译是「若不照我的话」），又「屈身在地，将脸伏在两膝之中」求雨（王上18:42），可见他是一个恳切祷告的人。至于年数，列王纪说「到第三年」（王上18:1），可能是从以利亚住到撒勒法算起（王上17:8-9），而三年零六个月是旱灾的全长（路4:25也这样说）；三年半在圣经中也常是审判和苦难的时期（但7:25；启11:2-3、6）。
- **给灰心的人：** 若你像以利亚一样，累到不想活下去，不要独自承受。请告诉一位可信任的人，或者立即联系当地的危机热线、急救电话。神在罗腾树下没有丢下以利亚，他先让以利亚睡觉、吃东西（王上19:5-8）；累垮了或抑郁了去看医生，也是领受神的照顾；抑郁不等于信心不够。神没有丢下以利亚，也不会丢下你。

> 要点：教会是一个祷告的群体：受苦的祷告，喜乐的歌颂，有病的请长老来，彼此认罪，互相代求。祷告有力量，不是因为祷告的人了不起（以利亚和我们一样软弱），而是因为我们所求告的神是信实的。

## 深读 5:19-20 使失迷的人回转

**「我的弟兄们，你们中间若有失迷真道的，有人使他回转，这人该知道：叫一个罪人从迷路上转回便是救一个灵魂不死，并且遮盖许多的罪。」**（5:19-20）
- **「你们中间」：** 这是在教会里的人，是曾经认识真道、后来走迷了的人。雅各没有说「随他去吧」，而是说要使他回转。
- **怎样使人回转？** 保罗说：「若有人偶然被过犯所胜，你们属灵的人就当用温柔的心把他挽回过来；又当自己小心，恐怕也被引诱。」（加6:1）这需要温柔，需要耐心，也需要自己谦卑。
- **「救一个灵魂不死」：** 这里的「灵魂」，多数解经家认为是指那个迷路的人：他若不回转，就要面对永远的死亡；也有人认为，这也包括那个使人回转的人自己。神正是藉着弟兄姊妹「天天彼此相劝」（来3:13），保守属他的人到底。真正救人的是神，可是神使用一个人的爱和劝勉，把迷路的人带回来。
- **「遮盖许多的罪」：** 这里的「遮盖」，是旧约说神赦免罪的说法：「得赦免其过、遮盖其罪的，这人是有福的！」（诗32:1；参箴10:12）多数解经家认为，这是指那迷路的人回转，他的许多罪在神面前得了赦免；也有人认为，这也包括那使人回转的人自己的罪（参彼前4:8）。即使是后一种读法，也不是说我们靠挽回人来赚得赦免；赦免只因基督的血。这也不是说要替人掩盖罪行、不让人知道；凡是伤害了别人的罪，「遮盖」绝不等于隐瞒。
- **神自己就是这样：** 「失丧的，我必寻找；被逐的，我必领回。」（结34:16）主耶稣说，牧人会撇下九十九只羊，去找那失去的一只（路15:4）。我们去寻找迷路的人，是因为好牧人先来寻找了我们：「你们从前好像迷路的羊，如今却归到你们灵魂的牧人监督了。」（彼前2:25）如果你就是那个走远了的人，也许是因为在教会里受过伤，这句话也是对你说的：好牧人不是等你自己走回来，他是出来找你的。
- **这样结束全书：** 雅各从「要行道，不要单单听道」（1:22）开始，以一个行动结束：去把迷路的人带回来。被找回来的信心，最后也会去找别人，特别是那个走失了的人。

> 要点：雅各以一个使命结束他的书信：去把迷路的弟兄姊妹带回来。我们能去寻找人，是因为好牧人先寻找了我们。

## 默想

**默想：** 「义人祈祷所发的力量是大有功效的。以利亚与我们是一样性情的人。」（5:16-17）读到「义人祈祷」，我心里常常一沉：我不是那个义人。我的祷告很短，常常分心，有时候只是把一张清单念完就睡了。我想，大有功效的祷告，是那些属灵长辈的事，是那些一跪就是一个钟头的人的事。我也许会请别人为我代求，却不太相信自己的祷告算数。可是雅各偏偏选了以利亚，又偏偏说他「与我们是一样性情的人」。这个在迦密山上求火降下来的人，几天以后就坐在罗腾树下说：「耶和华啊，罢了！求你取我的性命。」（王上19:4）神没有因此不再听他的祷告，也没有骂他，只是叫天使拍醒他，说：「起来吃吧！」（王上19:5）原来祷告的力量，从来不在于以利亚自己。我也不是因为自己够属灵，才被算为「义人」：我能被神算为义，是因为基督；我能活出一点义人的样子，也是他的恩典。我常常不晓得当怎样祷告，可是「圣灵亲自用说不出来的叹息替我们祷告」（罗8:26）。雅各在这封信里，叫我们在试炼中喜乐，勒住舌头，不偏心待人，说「主若愿意」，去把迷路的人带回来；这一切，我都常常做不到，在神面前，我是亏欠的。可是正因为祷告的力量不在我，我今天就可以为一个人祷告，也许是正在生病的那位肢体，也许是好久没来聚会的那位朋友，不必等到我觉得自己够属灵。读完雅各书，我最后想说的，不是「我要更努力」，而是向这位神说：主啊，你是满心怜悯、大有慈悲的（5:11）。你在迦密山上听了以利亚，在罗腾树下也没有丢下他；你听我的祷告，不是因为我的祷告够长、够热切，而是因为我是奉你儿子的名来到你面前，他已经为我的罪死了，又复活了。你是那降秋雨春雨的神，我愿意在你面前慢慢地等候。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 5 is the letter's last chapter, and it falls into four parts. **A warning to rich oppressors** (vv. 1–6): you rich, weep and howl; your riches have rotted and your gold and silver have corroded; you kept back the wages of the laborers, and their cries have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts; you have condemned and murdered the righteous person. **Patience until the Lord comes** (vv. 7–12): be patient like the farmer waiting for the early and late rains, and establish your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is at hand; do not grumble against one another, for the Judge is standing at the door; take the prophets and Job as examples, for the Lord is compassionate and merciful; above all, do not swear, but let your yes be yes and your no be no. **A praying community** (vv. 13–18): let the suffering pray, the cheerful sing praise, and the sick call for the elders of the church; confess your sins to one another and pray for one another; the prayer of a righteous person has great power, as Elijah's did. **Bringing back the one who wanders** (vv. 19–20): whoever brings back a sinner from his wandering will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.

This chapter gathers the whole letter together. It began with steadfastness in trials (1:2–4) and ends with patience again (5:7–11). It began by telling those who lack wisdom to ask God (1:5) and ends by telling the suffering and the sick to pray (5:13–16). It began with the lowly and the rich (1:9–11), and here it speaks to the rich again (5:1–6). James's letter has no closing greetings or blessing; it ends with a mission: go and bring back the one who has wandered.

## Going Deeper: 5:1–6 A Warning to the Rich

**1. "Your riches have rotted."** "Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you. Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten. Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence against you and will eat your flesh like fire. You have laid up treasure in the last days" (5:1–3).
- **The prophets' voice:** this passage sounds very much like the Old Testament prophets' judgments on Israel's rich (see Isa 5:8–9; Amos 6:1–7). The rich James addresses here are probably not members of the church, since he does not call them "brothers." But he writes these words for the church to hear, so that believers will see clearly: do not envy them, and do not become like them.
- **Treasure that decays:** the Lord Jesus said, "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy" (Matt 6:19). Gold does not actually rust, and silver only tarnishes; James is using a prophetic picture: even the wealth people think cannot decay will rot before God's judgment, and its corrosion becomes evidence against them.
- **"In the last days":** the New Testament says that the last days began with Christ's first coming (Heb 1:2). Those who only pile up money in the last days are like someone still building barns as the flood arrives.

**2. "The wages of the laborers... which you kept back."** "Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts. You have lived on the earth in luxury and in self-indulgence. You have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter" (5:4–5).
- **What the law required:** "The wages of a hired servant shall not remain with you all night until the morning" (Lev 19:13). "You shall give him his wages on the same day... lest he cry against you to the LORD, and you be guilty of sin" (Deut 24:15). The prophet puts "those who swear falsely" beside "those who oppress the hired worker in his wages" (Mal 3:5).
- **"Reached the ears of the Lord of hosts":** the world does not hear the cries of unpaid workers, but God does, as when Abel's blood was "crying to me from the ground" (Gen 4:10). "The Lord of hosts" is a weighty title: the one who takes up these workers' cause is the God who commands the armies of heaven.
- **What it looks like today:** unpaid wages, docked pay, missing insurance, workers whose legal status is used to push their pay down: none of this is rare today, and sometimes the employer is a Christian. Those whose wages have been withheld may seek the protection of the law and complain to labor authorities or the courts (Rom 13:4). That is not impatience; it is seeking justice. If you are the employer, repentance includes paying back what is owed; Zacchaeus said "I restore it fourfold" after Jesus had first come to his house (Luke 19:5–8).
- **"A day of slaughter":** like livestock fattened before slaughter. The rich think they are enjoying life, but they are fattening their hearts for the day of judgment (see Jer 12:3, "set them apart for the day of slaughter").

**3. "You have condemned... the righteous person."** "You have condemned; you have murdered the righteous person. He does not resist you" (5:6).
- **Who is "the righteous person"?** Most interpreters take it generally, of the poor and righteous who are oppressed by the rich and cannot resist (see 2:6, "drag you into court"). Some ancient interpreters see a hint of the Lord Jesus, "the Righteous One" who was condemned and crucified, and "does not resist" (see Acts 7:52). Still others read it as foreshadowing James's own later martyrdom.
- **"He does not resist you":** this does not mean injustice does not matter. It means the poor cannot resist, and so God himself will take up their cause. This is the majority reading; some read it as a question, "Does he [God] not oppose you?", since the same verb is used in 4:6, "God opposes the proud."

> The point: God hears the cries of the oppressed. Riches that decay cannot save anyone, and those who only pile up money are fattening their hearts for the day of judgment. But in Christ we have an inheritance "imperishable, undefiled, and unfading" (1 Pet 1:4), so we need not envy them; and if I have wronged anyone, I can make it right as Zacchaeus did, because the Lord came to his house first.

## Going Deeper: 5:7–12 Patience Until the Lord Comes

**1. "See how the farmer waits."** "Be patient, therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord. See how the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient about it, until it receives the early and the late rains. You also, be patient. Establish your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is at hand" (5:7–8).
- **"Therefore":** because God will surely vindicate the oppressed (vv. 1–6), believers need not take revenge themselves; they can wait patiently.
- **The early and the late rains:** in Palestine the early rain comes in October and November, so newly sown seed can sprout; the late rain comes in March and April, so the crop can fill out and ripen. This was God's covenant promise: "he will give the rain for your land in its season, the early rain and the later rain" (Deut 11:14; see Joel 2:23). The farmer cannot make the rain come sooner. What he can do is sow, and then wait.
- **"The coming of the Lord is at hand":** most interpreters take "the Lord" here as the Lord Jesus and his return. New Testament writers often say the Lord's coming is "at hand" (Phil 4:5; 1 Pet 4:7). This does not mean they miscalculated the date. It means that since Christ's resurrection and ascension, the Lord's return could come at any time, and believers are to live every day in that hope. If the Lord seems slow, he "is patient toward you" (2 Pet 3:9).

**2. "The Judge is standing at the door."** "Do not grumble against one another, brothers, so that you may not be judged; behold, the Judge is standing at the door" (5:9).
- **Sufferers easily grumble:** when people are oppressed outside, they easily complain about and take it out on one another inside the church, like someone mistreated at work who takes it out on his family at home. James reminds his readers that vindication is God's work; it is not for us to vent on each other.
- **"Standing at the door":** the Lord Jesus also said, "So also, when you see all these things, you know that he is near, at the very gates" (Matt 24:33). This is a warning: the Judge hears our grumbling, and believers too will appear before the judgment seat of Christ (2 Cor 5:10). Yet this Judge is the same Lord who says to a lukewarm church, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (Rev 3:20), calling us to repent and to eat with him.

**3. "The Lord is compassionate and merciful."** "As an example of suffering and patience, brothers, take the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord. Behold, we consider those blessed who remained steadfast. You have heard of the steadfastness of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful" (5:10–11).
- **The prophets' example:** the Lord Jesus said, "for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you" (Matt 5:12).
- **Job's "steadfastness":** Job was not a man who suffered in silence. He wept, he protested, he even cursed the day of his birth (Job 3:1). But he did not leave God. At the start he said, "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21), and in his pain he kept speaking to God; in the end God said Job had spoken of him what is right (Job 42:7). So the steadfastness James means is not a life without tears, but holding fast to God through them.
- **"The purpose of the Lord":** the word also means "end" or "outcome." Most interpreters take it as the way God finally "restored the fortunes of Job" and gave him twice as much as before (Job 42:10). Some early interpreters (Augustine is usually cited) have linked it with the Lord Jesus' suffering and resurrection. Either way, James's conclusion is that "the Lord is compassionate and merciful," the Old Testament's own praise of God (Exod 34:6; Ps 103:8). Suffering is not the end of the story; God's mercy is.

**4. "Above all... do not swear."** "But above all, my brothers, do not swear, either by heaven or by earth or by any other oath, but let your 'yes' be yes and your 'no' be no, so that you may not fall under condemnation" (5:12).
- **The words of Jesus:** this is almost exactly what the Lord Jesus said in the Sermon on the Mount (Matt 5:34–37).
- **Why here?** Those who suffer may swear rashly in their impatience, or swear by heaven and earth to add weight to their words. Chinese speakers often say "I swear to heaven" or "may lightning strike me if I lie." James wants believers to speak honestly: a simple "yes" is enough; no oath is needed to be believed.
- **Different readings:** Christians have applied this verse differently. The Anabaptist tradition (for example, the Schleitheim Confession of 1527, article 7, and the Mennonites) and the Quakers hold that Christians should never swear an oath, including in court. Most church traditions (for example, the Westminster Confession, chapter 22; the Augsburg Confession, article 16; the Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2154) hold that what is forbidden here is casual swearing in daily life and using oaths to evade responsibility, not lawful oaths on solemn occasions such as in court; they note that God himself swears (Heb 6:13) and that Paul calls God as his witness (2 Cor 1:23). Both agree that a Christian's word should be trustworthy in itself.

> The point: God will vindicate the oppressed, so we can wait patiently like the farmer. Steadfastness is not a life without tears, but holding fast through them to the Lord who is "compassionate and merciful."

## Going Deeper: 5:13–18 A Praying Community

**1. "Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray."** "Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise" (5:13).
- **Whatever happens, turn to God:** in suffering, do not only grumble (5:9); pray. In joy, do not forget God; sing praise. The whole book of Psalms is like this: laments and praises together.

**2. "Let him call for the elders of the church."** "Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up. And if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven" (5:14–15).
- **The sick person "calls":** the sick are to ask the elders to come, not to bear it alone for fear of troubling anyone or of others finding out. The elders, for their part, should treat visiting the sick as part of their calling.
- **"Anointing him with oil":** the church has understood this anointing in different ways. Some note that oil was also used as medicine at the time (see Luke 10:34), so prayer and treatment go together. Others see the anointing as a sign that sets the sick person apart and places him in God's hands (see Mark 6:13). The Catholic Church takes this verse as one basis for the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick, which Trent called Extreme Unction (Council of Trent, Session 14); the Orthodox Church has a similar rite of Holy Unction. Protestant Reformed and Lutheran traditions generally do not treat it as a sacrament. Calvin held that the anointing was tied to the early church's gift of healing, which had passed, though praying for the sick remains the church's duty. Many Pentecostal and charismatic churches today still practice anointing with prayer as written.
- **"The prayer of faith will save the one who is sick":** "save" here refers mainly to healing; "raise him up" means raising him from his sickbed. This does not mean that if faith is big enough, the sick will always recover. The "faith" here is the faith of the elders who pray; the burden is not placed on the sick person. The prayer of faith entrusts the sick to God and submits to his will (4:15, "If the Lord wills"; 1 John 5:14). Sometimes God raises the sick up in this life; sometimes he does not, as when Paul pleaded three times for the thorn to be removed, and the Lord said, "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Cor 12:8–9), and when Paul left Trophimus ill at Miletus (2 Tim 4:20). We may not conclude from an unhealed illness that the sick person or those praying lacked faith. On the day of resurrection the Lord will raise up all his people. Nor does praying for the sick mean refusing doctors or medicine.
- **"If he has committed sins":** the "if" matters. Some illness is connected with sin (see 1 Cor 11:30), but not all illness comes from sin (John 9:3). The church must never tell a sick person, "You must have committed some sin."

**3. "Confess your sins to one another and pray for one another."** "Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working" (5:16).
- **Confessing to one another:** confession is first to God: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9). But God also places us in a community, so that we can tell one or two trustworthy brothers or sisters about our struggles and ask them to pray for us. This does not mean telling everyone everything; it means no longer hiding alone, within relationships that are safe and loving. We often treat confession as losing face; before God, hiding is the real shame, and confession is the door to mercy. "One another" is mutual; the Reformers did not read it as requiring private confession to a priest (Calvin, *Institutes* 3.4.6).
- **Confession does not replace the law:** if what is confessed involves abuse, sexual assault or another crime, the church must not simply deal with it privately and pray. It must protect those harmed and report to the police or proper authorities (Rom 13:4). Confession and forgiveness do not remove a person's legal responsibility, nor do they restore him to office or give him access to those harmed or to children.
- **"A righteous person":** James means "righteous" in the Old Testament sense: someone who lives uprightly before God, fears him and is quick to confess and repent (see Prov 15:29, "he hears the prayer of the righteous"). He is not someone who never sins, since James has just said "confess your sins to one another." Such a life comes from God's free grace: we are first counted righteous by trusting Christ, and only then begin to live as righteous people (see 2:21–23). His prayer has power not because of his own merit, but because the God he calls on is faithful.

**4. "Elijah was a man with a nature like ours."** "Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed fervently that it might not rain, and for three years and six months it did not rain on the earth. Then he prayed again, and heaven gave rain, and the earth bore its fruit" (5:17–18).
- **Why Elijah?** Elijah was one of the most powerful prophets in the Old Testament, yet James deliberately says he was "a man with a nature like ours." Right after he prayed down fire and rain on Mount Carmel, he fled in fear of Jezebel into the wilderness, sat under a broom tree and asked to die: "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life" (1 Kgs 19:4). So prayer has great power, not because those who pray are superhuman.
- **"Three years and six months":** Kings says "in the third year" (1 Kgs 18:1), while both the Lord Jesus and James say "three years and six months" (Luke 4:25). "The third year" may be counted from Elijah's move to Zarephath (1 Kgs 17:8–9), while three years and six months is the drought's full length; three and a half years is also a recurring period of judgment and distress in Scripture (Dan 7:25; Rev 11:2–3, 6). Kings also does not say outright that Elijah prayed for no rain, but he said there would be no dew or rain "except by my word" (1 Kgs 17:1), and he "bowed himself down on the earth and put his face between his knees" to pray for rain (1 Kgs 18:42). He was clearly a man of earnest prayer.
- **For the discouraged:** if, like Elijah, you are so worn out that you do not want to go on living, do not carry it alone. Tell someone you trust, or contact a local crisis line or emergency number now. God did not abandon Elijah under the broom tree; he first gave him sleep and food (1 Kgs 19:5–8), and seeing a doctor for exhaustion or depression is part of his care; depression is not a sign of weak faith. He will not abandon you.

> The point: The church is a praying community: the suffering pray, the cheerful sing, the sick call the elders, and we confess our sins to one another and pray for one another. Prayer has power not because those who pray are impressive (Elijah was as weak as we are), but because the God we call on is faithful.

## Going Deeper: 5:19–20 Bringing Back the One Who Wanders

**"My brothers, if anyone among you wanders from the truth and someone brings him back, let him know that whoever brings back a sinner from his wandering will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins"** (5:19–20).
- **"Among you":** this is someone in the church, who once knew the truth and later wandered. James does not say "let him go"; he says bring him back.
- **How do we bring someone back?** Paul says, "Brothers, if anyone is caught in any transgression, you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness. Keep watch on yourself, lest you too be tempted" (Gal 6:1). It takes gentleness, patience and humility.
- **"Will save his soul from death":** most interpreters take "his soul" as the wanderer's: if he does not turn back, he faces eternal death. Some think it also includes the one who brings him back. God uses brothers' and sisters' exhortation "every day" (Heb 3:13) as one means of keeping those who belong to him to the end. It is God who truly saves, but God uses one person's love and appeal to bring the wanderer home.
- **"Will cover a multitude of sins":** "cover" is Old Testament language for God's forgiveness: "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered" (Ps 32:1; see Prov 10:12). Most interpreters take it as the wanderer's many sins, forgiven when he turns back. Some think it also includes the sins of the one who brings him back (see 1 Pet 4:8). Even on that reading, no one earns forgiveness by restoring others; forgiveness rests on Christ's blood alone. Nor does it mean hiding wrongdoing from others; where sin has harmed people, covering never means concealing.
- **This is what God himself does:** "I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strayed" (Ezek 34:16). The Lord Jesus said the shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to go after the one that is lost (Luke 15:4). We go looking for those who have wandered because the good Shepherd first came looking for us: "For you were straying like sheep, but have now returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" (1 Pet 2:25). If you are the one who has wandered, perhaps because you were hurt in the church, this word is for you too: the good Shepherd does not wait for you to find your own way back; he comes looking.
- **How the letter ends:** James began with "be doers of the word, and not hearers only" (1:22), and he ends with an action: go and bring back the one who has wandered. Faith that has been found goes looking for others, especially the one who has gone astray.

> The point: James ends his letter with a mission: go and bring back the brother or sister who has wandered. We can go looking for others because the good Shepherd first came looking for us.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working. Elijah was a man with a nature like ours" (5:16–17). When I read "the prayer of a righteous person," my heart often sinks: I am not that righteous person. My prayers are short and often distracted; sometimes I just read through a list and go to sleep. I think powerful prayer belongs to the spiritual elders, to people who kneel for an hour at a time. I may ask others to pray for me, but I do not really believe my own prayers count. Yet James chose Elijah, and insisted that he was "a man with a nature like ours." The man who prayed down fire on Mount Carmel sat a few days later under a broom tree and said, "It is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life" (1 Kgs 19:4). God did not stop hearing him or scold him. He sent an angel to touch him and say, "Arise and eat" (1 Kgs 19:5). The power of prayer was never in Elijah himself. And I am not counted "righteous" because I am spiritual enough: I am counted righteous because of Christ, and whatever of a righteous life I live is his grace too. I often do not know what to pray for as I ought, but "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Rom 8:26). In this letter James has told us to count trials as joy, to bridle our tongues, to show no partiality, to say "If the Lord wills," to bring back those who wander. I fall short in all of it; before God I am in debt. But because the power of prayer is not in me, I can pray for someone today, perhaps the brother who is sick, or the friend who has not been to church in a long time, without waiting until I feel spiritual enough. Finishing James, the last thing I want to say is not "I will try harder," but this, to him: Lord, you are compassionate and merciful (5:11). You heard Elijah on Carmel, and you did not abandon him under the broom tree. You hear my prayers, not because they are long or fervent enough, but because I come in the name of your Son, who died for my sins and rose again. You are the God who sends the early and the late rain; I want to wait before you.
