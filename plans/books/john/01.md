## 本章脉络

第一章分三段。**序言**（1-18节）像一首诗，从永恒说到创造，从创造说到道成肉身。**施洗约翰的见证**（19-34节）把镜头拉到约旦河外的伯大尼，宗教领袖派人来问「你是谁」，约翰却一直把话题引向另一位。**最初的门徒**（35-51节）中，一个人带另一个人来见耶稣：安得烈带彼得，腓力带拿但业。全章的问题是「他是谁？」，答案从「道」「神」「光」一路到「神的羔羊」「弥赛亚」「以色列的王」「人子」，一章之内就给了耶稣十几个称号。

## 深读 1:1「太初有道，道与神同在，道就是神」

**1. 「太初」是有意的回声。** 「太初」希腊文是 *en archē*，正是七十士译本创世记1:1「起初神创造天地」的开头两个字。约翰要读者一开口就想起创世记，但他往后再推了一步：创世记从神造天地说起，约翰从天地被造以先说起。

**2. 「有」与「成了」的对比。** 这一节三次用「有/是」（*ēn*），这是未完成时，意思是「一直都在」。到第3节说万物「是藉着他造的」，换成了另一个动词 *egeneto*（成了、出现）。万物是「成了」的，道却是「本有」的。到了第14节，约翰又用这个「成了」：「道**成了**肉身」。那位本来就在的，进入了有开始的世界。整篇序言就靠这两个动词撑起来。

**3. 「与神同在」：面向着神。** 「同在」的希腊文是 *pros ton theon*。*pros* 加上宾格，常有「朝向」的意思，所以许多解经家把它理解为「面对面」，好像道在永恒中就转身朝向父，在相交之中。有学者提醒不要把一个介词读得太重，在当时的希腊文里它也可以只是「与……一起」。但约翰在18节又说子「在父怀里」，可见他确实想让我们看到一种亲密的关系，不只是位置上的并列。

**4. 「道就是神」：一个冠词的分量。** 希腊文是 *theos ēn ho logos*，字面是「神是那道」。「道」有冠词，「神」没有冠词，而且放在句首加强语气。这个文法很精确：如果「神」也加冠词，意思就成了「道等于神的全部」，好像道就是父本身，约翰刚说了道「与神同在」，二者有分别。但没有冠词也不是说「道是一位神」（某些译本这样翻），因为放在动词前、作表语的名词，通常表示性质或身份，不是泛指。有一本英文译本把它译成「神是什么，道也是什么」，很贴近原意：道与父有分别，却完全拥有神的本性。

**5. 为什么叫「道」？** 希腊文 *logos* 有「话语」「理性」的意思。约翰的根首先在旧约：神**说话**就有了世界（「神说，要有光」；「诸天藉耶和华的命而造」，诗33:6），箴言8章又把神的智慧描写成在创世以先就与神同在、参与创造的那一位。对希腊读者，*logos* 也是哲学家用来说明宇宙秩序背后那个理性原则的词，所以两边的读者都听得懂这个字。但约翰的意思要从旧约来理解，而他宣告的是：这道是一个位格，而且他来了。中文译者借用了「道」字，中国读者自然会联想到老子。但老子的「道」是非位格、不可名状的原则，约翰的「道」却是那位创造万物、与父相交、成了肉身的神。约翰不是在回答老子；这两个「道」只是同一个字，不是同一个意思。

> 要点：第一节用最少的字，同时说了道的永恒（太初有道）、道与父的分别与相交（与神同在）、道的神性（道就是神）。后面整卷书，都是在展开这三句。

## 深读 1:5「光照在黑暗里，黑暗却不接受光」

**1. 一个动词，两种译法。** 「接受」的希腊文是 *katelaben*，这个字可以是「抓住、领会」，也可以是「抓住、制服」。所以译本分成两派：和合本译「黑暗却不接受光」，英文钦定本译「黑暗却不明白它」，这是「领会」的意思；一些较新的中文译本译作「黑暗不能胜过光」，英文ESV、NIV译「黑暗没有胜过它」，这是「制服」的意思。

**2. 约翰自己用法的线索。** 同一个动词在12:35又出现：「免得黑暗临到你们」，那里显然是「追上、压倒」的意思。这是支持「胜过」的有力证据。但约翰很喜欢一字双关（第三章会看到更明显的例子），也许他有意两层都要：黑暗既不明白光，也压不住光。

**3. 时态的对比。** 「照」是现在时（*phainei*），光一直在照；「不接受/不胜过」是不定过去时，把整件事概括起来看。在序言里，这首先是在总结光从创造以来在黑暗世界中的历史。但读完整卷书再回来看这一节，我们难免会想到那次决定性的较量：十字架是黑暗最用力的一次，「那时候是夜间了」（13:30），可是第三天光依然照着。

> 要点：无论选哪一种译法，这一节都是在宣告结局：光一直在照，黑暗没有得胜。

## 深读 1:14「道成了肉身，住在我们中间」

**1. 「肉身」是很刺耳的字。** 约翰没有说道「成了人」或「取了身体」，而是说成了 *sarx*，就是肉体，人的软弱、会累、会饿、会死的那一面。同一卷书里，耶稣会走累了坐在井旁（4:6），会在拉撒路墓前哭（11:35），会在十字架上说「我渴了」（19:28）。第1节那位「就是神」的道，在第14节成了会渴的肉身。

**2. 「住」原文是「支帐棚」。** 「住」是 *eskēnōsen*，来自 *skēnē*（帐棚）。七十士译本称会幕就用这个字。读过出埃及记的人立刻会想起：会幕立好以后，「云彩遮盖会幕，耶和华的荣光就充满了帐幕」（出40:34）。所以约翰紧接着说：「我们也见过他的荣光。」神的同在从前住在旷野的帐幕里，现在住在一个人的身体里。

**3. 「恩典真理」是出埃及记34章的回声。** 在出埃及记33章，摩西求神：「求你显出你的荣耀给我看。」神说：「你不能看见我的面，因为人见我的面不能存活。」于是神从摩西面前经过，宣告自己是「有丰盛的慈爱和诚实」（出34:6），希伯来文 *ḥesed we'emet*。许多解经家认为，约翰的「充充满满地有恩典有真理」就是这句话的回声（七十士译本译 *ḥesed* 用的是另一个字，所以这是意思上的呼应，不是逐字的翻译）。他在17-18节接着说：「律法本是藉着摩西传的；恩典和真理都是由耶稣基督来的。从来没有人看见神，只有在父怀里的独生子将他表明出来。」原文在这两句之间没有「但是」：律法本身就是神的恩赐，约翰不是把律法与恩典对立，而是说律法所指向的，在基督里完全显明了。摩西在山上求而不得看见的，门徒在一张人的脸上看见了。

**4. 「独生子」与18节的异文。** 「独生」（*monogenēs*）的重点可能不在「生」，而在「独一、无可比拟」，希伯来书11:17称以撒为亚伯拉罕的 *monogenēs*，而亚伯拉罕还有以实玛利。（也有学者认为传统的「独生」译法仍然成立。无论如何，信经所认信的子「在万世之先为父所生」，并不只建立在这一个字上。）18节还有一个有名的异文：较早的抄本作「独一的神」（*monogenēs theos*），后来的抄本作「独生子」。和合本跟随后者，英文ESV作「那独一的神」，NIV（2011年版）作「那位独一的子，他本身就是神」。不论哪一种，意思都是：只有那位自己就是神的，才能把神表明出来。

> 要点：神不只是从远处向人说话，他搬进来住。会幕、荣光、恩典真理，旧约里神同在的每一个记号，约翰都放在这一个人身上。

## 深读 1:29「看哪，神的羔羊，除去世人罪孽的」

**1. 哪一只羔羊？** 约翰没有解释，因为他的读者心里已经有好几只：逾越节的羊羔（出12章），它的血使灭命的越过；以赛亚书53:7的羔羊，「他像羊羔被牵到宰杀之地」，默然不语、担当众人的罪孽；还有创世记22:8亚伯拉罕的那句话：「神必自己预备作燔祭的羊羔。」有些犹太启示文学（年代有争议）里还有一只得胜的羊羔，带领神的子民争战（启示录也这样描写基督）。施洗约翰说这话时，心里想的也许主要是那位要来施行审判、得胜的弥赛亚；写福音书的约翰回头看，看见了更完整的意义。还要注意：逾越节的羊羔严格来说不是赎罪祭，「除去罪孽」这一层，主要来自以赛亚书53章那位替众人担罪的仆人。这一个称号，把这几条线都收进来了。

**2. 约翰福音的结尾把耶稣的死与逾越节连在一起。** 约翰特别记下，耶稣被定罪是在「预备逾越节的日子，约有午正」（19:14）。有解经家认为这正是宰杀逾越节羊羔的那一天；也有人（如卡森）认为「预备日」指逾越节期间的星期五，这样就与另外三卷福音书的记载一致。无论哪一种，约翰都要我们把耶稣的死与逾越节连在一起：他死后兵丁没有打断他的腿，约翰引经说「他的骨头一根也不可折断」（19:36），这是出埃及记12:46对逾越节羊羔的规定（参诗34:20）。第一章的这句呼喊，到十九章才完全展开。

**3. 「罪孽」是单数。** 希腊文是 *tēn hamartian tou kosmou*，「世界的罪」，单数。约翰不是在列一张罪的清单，而是把世人在神面前全部的罪债与罪责总括起来。羔羊是祭牲，他除去罪的方法，是在十字架上替我们死、担当我们的罪，而且一次就完成了（19:30「成了」）。「除去」（*airōn*）是现在分词，说明他就是那位除去罪的；这不是说这工作还没有完成，而是说这位羔羊的身份与他所成就的救恩分不开。

## 深读 1:51「你们将要看见天开了，神的使者上去下来在人子身上」

**1. 雅各的梯子。** 这是创世记28:12的直接回声：雅各梦见一个梯子立在地上，梯子的头顶着天，「神的使者在梯子上，上去下来」。连顺序都一样，先「上去」再「下来」。雅各醒来说：「这不是别的，乃是神的殿，也是天的门。」（创28:17）耶稣说，那座连接天地的梯子，就是「人子」自己。

**2. 拿但业与雅各。** 这个回声不是偶然的。几节之前，耶稣称拿但业是「真是以色列人，他心里是没有诡诈的」（1:47）。「以色列」是雅各后来的名字，而雅各原本是那个「用诡计」（创27:35，七十士译本用的正是「诡诈」*dolos* 这个字）骗取祝福的人。耶稣好像在说：你是一个没有雅各诡诈的以色列人，你要看见比雅各所看见的更大的事。

**3. 为第二章作铺垫。** 雅各把那地方叫「伯特利」，意思是「神的殿」。下一章耶稣就要说：「你们拆毁这殿，我三日内要再建立起来。」（2:19）神与人相会的地方，不再是一个地点，而是一个人。

## 默想

**默想：** 第一章给了耶稣十几个称号：道、神、光、羔羊、弥赛亚、神的儿子、以色列的王、人子。哪一个称号是我今天最需要的？是那位「除去罪孽」的羔羊，还是那位「支帐棚住在我们中间」的道？约翰福音第一章里，一个人遇见耶稣之后，第一件事就是去找另一个人：「你来看！」（1:46）今天我可以对谁说这句话？

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 1 has three parts. **The Prologue** (vv. 1–18) reads like a poem, moving from eternity to creation and from creation to the incarnation. **John the Baptist's testimony** (vv. 19–34) pulls the camera to Bethany beyond the Jordan, where religious leaders send people to ask "Who are you?" and John keeps turning the subject to someone else. **The first disciples** (vv. 35–51) come one by one, each bringing another: Andrew brings Peter, and Philip brings Nathanael. The chapter's question is "Who is he?", and the answers run from "the Word," "God" and "the light" to "the Lamb of God," "Messiah," "King of Israel" and "Son of Man." Within one chapter Jesus is given more than a dozen titles.

## Going Deeper: 1:1 "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God"

**1. "In the beginning" is a deliberate echo.** The Greek *en archē* is exactly how the Septuagint opens Genesis 1:1, "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." John wants his readers to think of Genesis from the very first words, but then he goes one step further back. Genesis starts with God making the world; John starts before the world was made.

**2. "Was" versus "came to be."** This verse uses "was" (*ēn*) three times. It's the imperfect tense, meaning "was already there, continuously." When verse 3 says all things were made through him, John switches to a different verb, *egeneto* ("came to be," "came into existence"). All things *came to be*; the Word simply *was*. Then in verse 14 John uses that "came to be" verb again: "the Word *became* flesh." The one who always was entered a world of beginnings. The whole Prologue is held up by these two verbs.

**3. "With God": facing toward God.** "With" here is *pros ton theon*. *Pros* with the accusative often carries the sense of "toward," so many interpreters hear it as "face to face": the Word in eternity turned toward the Father in communion. Some scholars warn against overloading a preposition, since in the Greek of the day it could simply mean "with." But in verse 18 John adds that the Son is "at the Father's side" (literally, "in the bosom of the Father"), so he clearly wants us to see an intimate relationship, not just two beings side by side.

**4. "The Word was God": the weight of one article.** The Greek is *theos ēn ho logos*, literally "God was the Word." "Word" has the article; "God" doesn't, and it comes first for emphasis. The grammar is precise. If "God" also had the article, it would mean "the Word is the whole of God," as if the Word were the Father himself, yet John has just said the Word was *with* God, so the two are distinct. But the lack of an article doesn't mean "the Word was *a* god" (as one translation renders it), because a predicate noun placed before the verb typically describes nature or identity rather than one of a class. The New English Bible captures it well: "what God was, the Word was." The Word is distinct from the Father, yet fully shares the nature of God.

**5. Why "the Word"?** The Greek *logos* means both "word" and "reason." John's roots are first in the Old Testament: God *speaks* the world into being ("And God said, Let there be light"; "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made," Ps 33:6), and Proverbs 8 pictures God's Wisdom as present with him before creation and at work in it. For Greek readers *logos* was also the philosophers' term for the rational principle behind the order of the universe, so the word registered with both audiences. But John's meaning comes from the Old Testament, and what he announces is that the Word is a person, and he has come. Chinese Bibles translate it 道 (*dao*), and a Chinese reader naturally thinks of Laozi. But Laozi's Dao is an impersonal principle that cannot be named, while John's Word is the God who made all things, lives in communion with the Father, and became flesh. John wasn't answering Laozi. The two share a word, not a meaning.

> The point: In the fewest possible words, verse 1 states the Word's eternity ("in the beginning was the Word"), his distinction from and communion with the Father ("with God"), and his deity ("was God"). The rest of the book unfolds these three phrases.

## Going Deeper: 1:5 "The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it"

**1. One verb, two translations.** The verb is *katelaben*, which can mean "grasp" in the sense of understanding or "grasp" in the sense of seizing and overpowering. So translations split into two camps. The KJV has "the darkness comprehended it not," and the Chinese Union Version's "did not accept it" (接受) sits on the same side. The ESV and NIV have "the darkness has not overcome it," as do several newer Chinese translations.

**2. A clue from John's own usage.** The same verb appears again in 12:35, "lest darkness overtake you," where it clearly means "catch up with and overwhelm." That's strong evidence for "overcome." But John loves double meanings (chapter 3 has a clearer example), and he may intend both: the darkness neither understood the light nor could put it out.

**3. A contrast of tenses.** "Shines" is present tense (*phainei*): the light keeps on shining. "Has not overcome" is aorist, which views the action as a whole. In the Prologue it first sums up the light's history in a dark world since creation. But read the whole book and come back, and you can hardly help thinking of the decisive contest. The cross was darkness's greatest effort ("and it was night," 13:30), yet on the third day the light was still shining.

> The point: Whichever translation you choose, the verse announces the outcome in advance. The light keeps shining, and the darkness has not won.

## Going Deeper: 1:14 "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us"

**1. "Flesh" is a jarring word.** John doesn't say the Word "became a man" or "took a body." He says he became *sarx*, flesh: human weakness, the part of us that tires, hungers and dies. In this same book Jesus is worn out from walking and sits down by a well (4:6), weeps at Lazarus's tomb (11:35), and says "I thirst" on the cross (19:28). The Word who "was God" in verse 1 became, in verse 14, flesh that could thirst.

**2. "Dwelt" literally means "pitched his tent."** The verb is *eskēnōsen*, from *skēnē* (tent), the word the Septuagint uses for the tabernacle. Anyone who knew Exodus would recall that when the tabernacle was finished, "the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Ex 40:34). So John goes straight on: "and we have seen his glory." God's presence once dwelt in a tent in the wilderness; now it dwells in a human body.

**3. "Grace and truth" echoes Exodus 34.** In Exodus 33 Moses asks, "Please show me your glory." God answers, "You cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live." So God passes before Moses and proclaims himself "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Ex 34:6), in Hebrew *ḥesed we'emet*. Many interpreters hear John's "full of grace and truth" as an echo of that phrase (the Septuagint renders *ḥesed* with a different word, so it's an echo of meaning rather than a word-for-word match). In verses 17–18 he goes on: "the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known." There's no "but" between the two halves in Greek. The law was itself God's gift; John isn't setting law against grace but saying that what the law pointed to has been fully revealed in Christ. What Moses asked for on the mountain and couldn't see, the disciples saw in a human face.

**4. "Only Son" and the variant in verse 18.** The stress of *monogenēs* may fall less on "begotten" and more on "unique, one of a kind." Hebrews 11:17 calls Isaac Abraham's *monogenēs*, though Abraham also had Ishmael. (Some scholars still defend "only begotten." Either way, the creeds' confession that the Son is eternally begotten of the Father doesn't rest on this one word.) Verse 18 also has a famous textual variant. The earlier manuscripts read "the only God" (*monogenēs theos*), while later ones read "the only Son." The Chinese Union Version follows the latter; the ESV has "the only God," and the NIV (2011) "the one and only Son, who is himself God." Either way the meaning is that only one who is himself God can make God known.

> The point: God didn't just speak to us from a distance. He moved in. The tabernacle, the glory, the steadfast love and faithfulness, every Old Testament sign of God's presence, John places on this one person.

## Going Deeper: 1:29 "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!"

**1. Which lamb?** John doesn't explain, because his readers already had several lambs in mind. There was the Passover lamb (Ex 12), whose blood made the destroyer pass over. There was the lamb of Isaiah 53:7, "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter," silent, bearing the iniquity of many. And there was Abraham's word in Genesis 22:8, "God will provide for himself the lamb for a burnt offering." Some Jewish apocalyptic texts (their dating is debated) also picture a conquering lamb leading God's people in battle (Revelation portrays Christ this way too). When John the Baptist spoke, he may mainly have had in mind a Messiah who would come in judgment and victory; John the evangelist, looking back, saw the fuller meaning. Note too that the Passover lamb wasn't strictly a sin offering. The idea of "taking away sin" comes mainly from Isaiah 53's servant, who bears the sin of many. This one title gathers all these threads.

**2. The end of the Gospel ties Jesus' death to Passover.** John notes that Jesus was condemned on "the day of Preparation of the Passover, about the sixth hour" (19:14). Some interpreters see this as the day the Passover lambs were slaughtered; others (Carson, for example) take "Preparation" to mean the Friday of Passover week, in line with the other three Gospels. Either way, John wants us to connect Jesus' death with Passover. After his death the soldiers didn't break his legs, and John quotes, "Not one of his bones will be broken" (19:36), which was the rule for the Passover lamb in Exodus 12:46 (cf. Ps 34:20). The cry of chapter 1 is only fully unfolded in chapter 19.

**3. "Sin" is singular.** The Greek is *tēn hamartian tou kosmou*, "the sin of the world," in the singular. John isn't listing sins; he sums up the whole world's guilt before God. A lamb is a sacrifice, and Jesus takes away sin by dying in our place on the cross, bearing it himself, once for all ("It is finished," 19:30). "Takes away" (*airōn*) is a present participle, describing him as the one who takes away sin. That doesn't make the work unfinished; it tells us that who the Lamb is cannot be separated from what he has done.

## Going Deeper: 1:51 "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man"

**1. Jacob's ladder.** This directly echoes Genesis 28:12, where Jacob dreams of a ladder set on the earth with its top reaching heaven, "and behold, the angels of God were ascending and descending on it." Even the order matches: first ascending, then descending. Jacob wakes and says, "This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven" (Gen 28:17). Jesus says the ladder joining heaven and earth is the Son of Man himself.

**2. Nathanael and Jacob.** The echo isn't accidental. A few verses earlier Jesus calls Nathanael "an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit" (1:47). "Israel" was Jacob's later name, and Jacob was the man who came "deceitfully" to steal the blessing (Gen 27:35, where the Septuagint uses the same word for deceit, *dolos*). It's as if Jesus is saying: you are an Israelite without Jacob's deceit, and you will see greater things than Jacob saw.

**3. Setting up chapter 2.** Jacob named the place Bethel, "house of God." In the next chapter Jesus will say, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (2:19). The meeting place of God and humanity is no longer a location but a person.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Chapter 1 gives Jesus more than a dozen titles: Word, God, Light, Lamb, Messiah, Son of God, King of Israel, Son of Man. Which one do I most need today? The Lamb who takes away sin, or the Word who pitched his tent among us? In John 1, the first thing a person does after meeting Jesus is go and find someone else: "Come and see" (1:46). Who could I say that to today?
