## 本章脉络

第二章由两个场景组成，一个在加利利的婚筵上，一个在耶路撒冷的圣殿里。**变水为酒**（1-11节）是七个神迹的第一个，场面安静，只有用人和门徒知道发生了什么。**洁净圣殿**（12-22节）则是公开的冲突。**结尾的三节**（23-25节）像一个小注脚，告诉我们耶稣怎样看待那些因神迹而「信」的人。这两个场景放在一起，说的是同一件事：旧的秩序（洁净礼仪的石缸、献祭的圣殿）要被一个更新、更丰盛的东西取代，而那个新的东西就是耶稣自己。

还有一个小细节：「第三日」（2:1）。第一章用「次日」把日子一天天往下数（1:29、35、43），有学者算出来，迦拿的婚筵落在全书的第七天。这和创世记第一章遥相呼应：约翰福音以「太初」开始，在第一个「七天」的结尾，新创造的荣耀第一次显出来。这个算法不是每个解经家都接受，但它提醒我们，约翰很在意时间。

## 深读 2:4「母亲，我与你有什么相干？我的时候还没有到」

**1. 和合本的「母亲」原文是「妇人」。** 希腊文是 *gynai*，和合本译成「母亲」，并加小字注明原文作「妇人」。中文里称母亲为「妇人」显得冷淡，但在希腊文里这是一个有礼貌的称呼，耶稣也这样称呼撒玛利亚妇人（4:21）和抹大拉的马利亚（20:15）。不过，儿子这样称呼自己的母亲确实不寻常，这暗示一种距离：在这件事上，她不再只是以母亲的身份对他说话。

**2. 「我与你有什么相干」是一句闪族习语。** 字面是「这对我和对你是什么？」（*ti emoi kai soi*）。旧约里这句话常表示「这事不该由你来指挥我」（如士11:12，王上17:18）。语气不一定严厉，但意思是划出界线：他行事的时间表，不由母亲决定，也不由婚筵的需要决定，而由父决定。

**3. 「我的时候」是贯穿全书的一条线。** 这是约翰福音第一次提到耶稣的「时候」。接下来这个词会一再出现：「没有人下手，因为他的时候还没有到」（7:30；8:20）；然后在受难前夕突然转变：「人子得荣耀的时候到了」（12:23），「耶稣知道自己离世归父的时候到了」（13:1），「父啊，时候到了」（17:1）。所以在迦拿，耶稣说「我的时候还没有到」的时候，他想的不只是这场婚筵，而是十字架。

**4. 马利亚只出现两次，两次都被称为「妇人」。** 在约翰福音里，耶稣的母亲只出现在开头的迦拿和结尾的十字架下（19:25-27），而且两次耶稣都用 *gynai* 称呼她（和合本两处都译为「母亲」）。在迦拿，「时候还没有到」；在十字架下，时候到了。这两个场景像书挡一样，把耶稣的公开事奉夹在中间。

> 要点：马利亚的回应很值得学习。她没有争辩，只对用人说：「他告诉你们什么，你们就做什么。」（2:5）她不知道耶稣会怎样做，但她知道该把事情交在谁手里。

## 深读 2:6-10 六口石缸

**1. 数量惊人。** 「每口可以盛两三桶水」，这里的「桶」（*metrētēs*）约等于三十九公升。六口缸加起来大约四百五十到七百公升，相当于六七百瓶葡萄酒。一个乡村婚筵用不了那么多。这是故意的：弥赛亚时代的一个标志就是酒的丰盛。阿摩司书9:13说那日「大山要滴下甜酒」，以赛亚书25:6说万军之耶和华要在这山上为万民摆设「陈酒的筵席」。

**2. 石缸是做什么用的？** 约翰特别注明它们是「照犹太人洁净的规矩」摆在那里的。石器在犹太律法中被认为不会沾染不洁，所以用来盛洁净礼仪的水，比如饭前洗手。耶稣用的，正是旧约洁净礼仪的器皿。

**3. 「直到缸口」。** 用人把缸倒满了水，「直到缸口」（2:7）。不留空间，没有可以掺别的东西的余地。旧的器皿被装满了，然后被转变成新的东西。约翰没有说旧约的礼仪是错的，而是说它指向的东西现在来了，并且远远超过它。

**4. 管筵席的不知道，用人知道。** 管筵席的尝了，就说：「你倒把好酒留到如今！」（2:10）这句话的讽刺很深：他夸的是新郎，其实应该夸的是另一位，就是那位真正的新郎（3:29施洗约翰就这样称呼耶稣）。而知道真相的，是打水的用人。神迹的意义，常常向那些顺服地做了事的人显明。

## 深读 2:11「这是耶稣所行的头一件神迹……显出他的荣耀来」

**1. 「头一件」原文是「起头」。** 希腊文是 *archēn tōn sēmeiōn*，直译为「神迹的起头」。*archē* 正是1:1「太初」的那个字。这不只是「第一个」，而是「开端」，后面的所有神迹都从这里展开。

**2. 「神迹」是记号，不是表演。** 约翰从不用对观福音常用的 *dynameis*（异能）来称耶稣的神迹，而是用 *sēmeia*（记号）。一个路标的价值不在路标本身，而在它指向的地方。变水为酒的意义，不是耶稣能解决婚筵缺酒的尴尬，而是它「显出他的荣耀来」。这呼应1:14：「我们也见过他的荣光。」序言里宣告的事，在迦拿第一次被看见了。

**3. 神迹引出的反应。** 「他的门徒就信他了。」门徒在第一章就已经跟从了他，现在他们「信」，信是可以加深的。约翰的整卷书，就是这样一个信心一层层加深的过程。

## 深读 2:19「你们拆毁这殿，我三日内要再建立起来」

**1. 两个都译作「殿」的字。** 约翰在这段里用了两个不同的希腊字，和合本都译成「殿」。14-15节耶稣赶出牛羊的「殿」是 *hieron*，指整个圣殿区，包括外院；19-21节「你们拆毁这殿」的「殿」是 *naos*，指圣所本身，就是神同在的地方。耶稣说的不是整个建筑群，而是神居住的那个核心。约翰随即解释：「耶稣这话是以他的身体为殿。」（2:21）呼应1:14，神的同在已经「支帐棚」在他的身体里了。

**2. 又一个误解。** 「这殿是四十六年才造成的，你三日内就再建立起来吗？」（2:20）这是约翰福音第一个典型的误解：听的人只听到字面意思。希律从主前20年左右开始重建圣殿，四十六年后，大约是主后27-28年，与耶稣开始传道的时间吻合，这也是约翰的年代记载可靠的一个旁证。

**3. 门徒也是后来才明白。** 约翰很诚实地记下：「所以到他从死里复活以后，门徒就想起他说过这话，便信了圣经和耶稣所说的。」（2:22）有些话要等到复活之后才能懂。约翰在书中好几次这样说（12:16；14:26）。这对今天的读者也是一种安慰：今天读不懂的，不一定是白读。

**4. 「我为你的殿心里焦急，如同火烧」。** 门徒想起诗篇69:9（2:17）。七十士译本的诗篇用过去时（「已经吞噬了我」），约翰引用时却用将来时：「必吞噬我」。诗篇69篇是一首义人受苦的诗，约翰改成将来时，似乎是在暗示：耶稣为父殿的热心，最终会把他自己吞掉，这就是十字架。

**5. 为什么约翰把洁净圣殿放在开头？** 马太、马可、路加都把这件事放在受难周，约翰却放在事奉的开头。有学者认为耶稣洁净过两次圣殿；也有学者认为约翰是按主题而不是按时间来安排，把它放在前面作为整卷书的纲领。不论哪一种，约翰的用意都很清楚：从一开始，耶稣与圣殿之间的冲突就是关于「谁是神同在的地方」。

## 深读 2:24「耶稣却不将自己交托他们」

这里有一个中文看不出来的文字游戏。23节说许多人「信了他的名」，24节说耶稣「不将自己交托他们」。「信」和「交托」在希腊文里是同一个字 *pisteuō*。直译就是：「许多人信了他……耶稣却不信他们。」

他们的「信」是被神迹吸引的，耶稣知道这种信还没有扎根，「因他知道人心里所存的」（2:25）。约翰在这里第一次提醒我们：信有不同的深度。有一种信只停留在神迹上，另一种信却是信入他这个人。下一章的尼哥德慕，就是一个「因为看见神迹」来找耶稣的人（3:2）。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 2 has two scenes, one at a wedding feast in Galilee and one in the temple at Jerusalem. **Water into wine** (vv. 1–11) is the first of the seven signs, and it's quiet: only the servants and the disciples know what happened. **The cleansing of the temple** (vv. 12–22) is open confrontation. **The last three verses** (vv. 23–25) are a short footnote showing how Jesus regards people who "believe" because of signs. Together the two scenes say one thing: the old order, the stone jars of purification and the temple of sacrifice, is giving way to something newer and richer, and that new thing is Jesus himself.

There's also a small detail: "on the third day" (2:1). Chapter 1 counts the days forward with "the next day" (1:29, 35, 43), and some scholars calculate that the wedding at Cana falls on the book's seventh day. That would echo Genesis 1: John's Gospel opens with "in the beginning," and at the end of its first "seven days" the glory of a new creation appears for the first time. Not every interpreter accepts this count, but it reminds us that John pays close attention to time.

## Going Deeper: 2:4 "Woman, what does this have to do with me? My hour has not yet come"

**1. "Woman" is respectful in Greek.** The word is *gynai*. To modern ears it sounds cold for a son to call his mother "woman" (the Chinese Union Version softens it to "mother," with a note that the original says "woman"). But in Greek it was a polite form of address; Jesus uses it for the Samaritan woman (4:21) and Mary Magdalene (20:15). Still, it's unusual for a son to address his own mother this way, and it suggests some distance: in this matter she is no longer speaking to him simply as his mother.

**2. "What does this have to do with me?" is a Semitic idiom.** Literally it's "What is this to me and to you?" (*ti emoi kai soi*). In the Old Testament the phrase often means "this isn't yours to direct me in" (e.g., Judg 11:12; 1 Kgs 17:18). It isn't necessarily harsh, but it draws a line: his timetable isn't set by his mother or by the wedding's needs, but by the Father.

**3. "My hour" is a thread through the whole book.** This is the first mention of Jesus' "hour" in John. The word keeps returning: "no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come" (7:30; 8:20). Then, on the eve of the passion, it suddenly shifts: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified" (12:23); "Jesus knew that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the Father" (13:1); "Father, the hour has come" (17:1). So when Jesus says at Cana that his hour hasn't come, he's thinking not just of this wedding but of the cross.

**4. Mary appears only twice, and both times she is called "woman."** In John, Jesus' mother appears only at Cana at the start and at the cross at the end (19:25–27), and both times Jesus addresses her as *gynai*. At Cana, the hour has not yet come; at the cross, it has. The two scenes act like bookends around Jesus' public ministry.

> The point: Mary's response is worth learning from. She doesn't argue; she simply tells the servants, "Do whatever he tells you" (2:5). She doesn't know what Jesus will do, but she knows whose hands to leave it in.

## Going Deeper: 2:6–10 The Six Stone Jars

**1. The quantity is staggering.** "Each holding twenty or thirty gallons." Six jars hold roughly 120 to 180 gallons (450 to 700 liters), something like six or seven hundred bottles of wine. A village wedding could never drink that much. That's the point: one mark of the messianic age was an abundance of wine. Amos 9:13 says that in that day "the mountains shall drip sweet wine," and Isaiah 25:6 says the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples "a feast of well-aged wine."

**2. What were the stone jars for?** John notes that they stood there "for the Jewish rites of purification." Under Jewish law stone vessels were considered unable to contract impurity, so they held water for purification rituals such as washing hands before meals. Jesus used the very vessels of Old Testament purification.

**3. "Up to the brim."** The servants filled the jars "up to the brim" (2:7), leaving no room and no chance of adding anything else. The old vessels were filled full, then turned into something new. John isn't saying the Old Testament rites were wrong. He's saying that what they pointed to has now arrived, and it far surpasses them.

**4. The master of the feast doesn't know; the servants do.** The master tastes it and says, "You have kept the good wine until now" (2:10). The irony runs deep. He praises the bridegroom, but the one he should be praising is another, the true Bridegroom (which is what John the Baptist calls Jesus in 3:29). And the ones who know the truth are the servants who drew the water. The meaning of a sign is often revealed to those who simply did what they were told.

## Going Deeper: 2:11 "This, the first of his signs... manifested his glory"

**1. "The first" is literally "the beginning."** The Greek is *archēn tōn sēmeiōn*, "the beginning of the signs." *Archē* is the same word as "in the beginning" in 1:1. This isn't just "number one"; it's the origin from which all the later signs unfold.

**2. A sign is a pointer, not a performance.** John never calls Jesus' miracles *dynameis* (mighty works), as the other Gospels do, but *sēmeia* (signs). A signpost's value isn't in the post but in where it points. The meaning of water into wine isn't that Jesus could rescue a wedding from embarrassment, but that it "manifested his glory." This echoes 1:14: "we have seen his glory." What the Prologue announced is seen for the first time at Cana.

**3. The response the sign draws out.** "And his disciples believed in him." The disciples had already followed him in chapter 1; now they "believe," which shows that faith can deepen. John's whole book traces this deepening, layer by layer.

## Going Deeper: 2:19 "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up"

**1. Two words, both translated "temple."** John uses two different Greek words in this passage, though many translations render both as "temple." The temple Jesus clears of cattle and sheep in verses 14–15 is the *hieron*, the whole temple complex, outer courts included. The temple of "destroy this temple" in verses 19–21 is the *naos*, the sanctuary itself, the place of God's presence. Jesus isn't talking about the whole compound but about the core where God dwells. John then explains: "he was speaking about the temple of his body" (2:21). This echoes 1:14: God's presence has already "pitched its tent" in his body.

**2. Another misunderstanding.** "It has taken forty-six years to build this temple, and will you raise it up in three days?" (2:20). This is John's first classic misunderstanding: the hearers catch only the literal meaning. Herod began rebuilding the temple around 20 BC, so forty-six years later is about AD 27–28, which fits the start of Jesus' ministry and is one small confirmation of John's chronology.

**3. The disciples also understood only later.** John honestly records, "When therefore he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this, and they believed the Scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken" (2:22). Some words can only be understood after the resurrection; John says this several times (12:16; 14:26). That's a comfort for readers today too: what we can't understand now isn't wasted reading.

**4. "Zeal for your house will consume me."** The disciples remember Psalm 69:9 (2:17). The Septuagint has the verb in the past tense ("has consumed me"), but John quotes it in the future: "will consume me." Psalm 69 is a psalm of the suffering righteous one, and by shifting to the future John seems to hint that Jesus' zeal for his Father's house will finally consume him. That is the cross.

**5. Why does John put the temple cleansing at the start?** Matthew, Mark and Luke all place it in passion week; John places it at the beginning of the ministry. Some scholars think Jesus cleansed the temple twice. Others think John arranged his material by theme rather than chronology, putting this first as a key to the whole book. Either way John's intent is clear: from the beginning, the conflict between Jesus and the temple is about where God's presence dwells.

## Going Deeper: 2:24 "But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them"

There's wordplay here that most translations can't show. Verse 23 says many "believed in his name"; verse 24 says Jesus "did not entrust himself to them." "Believe" and "entrust" are the same Greek verb, *pisteuō*. Literally: "Many believed in him... but Jesus did not believe himself to them."

Their belief was drawn by the signs, and Jesus knew it hadn't taken root yet, "for he himself knew what was in man" (2:25). Here John first warns us that belief comes in different depths. One kind stops at the signs; another kind believes into the person himself. In the next chapter, Nicodemus is a man who comes to Jesus precisely because of the signs he's seen (3:2).

## Meditation

**Reflect:** The servants at Cana simply filled the stone jars with water as they were told, and they became the first to know about the sign. Is there some small thing I know the Lord has asked me to do, but have put off because I can't see the point of it? Jesus "knew what was in man." Does my faith in him stop at what he can do for me, or have I begun to entrust myself to him as a person?
