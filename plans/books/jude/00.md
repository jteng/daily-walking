## 这卷书在讲什么

犹大书只有二十五节，是新约中篇幅很短的一封信。犹大原本想写一封信，「论我们同得救恩」，后来却「不得不写信劝你们，要为从前一次交付圣徒的真道竭力地争辩」（1:3）。原因是：「有些人偷着进来」，他们「将我们神的恩变作放纵情欲的机会，并且不认独一的主宰－我们主耶稣基督」（1:4）。

所以这封信的大部分篇幅，是在描写这些人，并且提醒读者：神从来没有放过这样的人。犹大用了一连串旧约的例子：出埃及后不信的百姓、离开本位的天使、所多玛和蛾摩拉（1:5-7）；又说这些人走了该隐、巴兰、可拉的路（1:11）。他用了许多鲜明的图画：没有雨的云彩，秋天没有果子的树，海里的狂浪，流荡的星（1:12-13）。

可是犹大的信不是只有警告。信的开头和结尾，都在讲神怎样保守他的百姓：
- **开头：** 读者是「被召、在父神里蒙爱、为耶稣基督保守的人」（1:1）。
- **中间：** 信徒要「保守自己常在神的爱中」（1:21）。
- **结尾：** 神「能保守你们不失脚、叫你们无瑕无疵、欢欢喜喜站在他荣耀之前」（1:24）。
有趣的是，「保守」这个词（*tēreō*）在信中也用在那些离开本位的天使身上：他们「不守本位」，所以神把他们「拘留」（原文是同一个词）在黑暗里（1:6）；为那些流荡的星，「有墨黑的幽暗为他们永远存留」（1:13，也是同一个词）。不肯守住神所给的位分的，就被神「存留」到审判；被神保守的人，却要站在他荣耀之前。第24节用的是另一个词（*phylassō*，看守、护卫），但意思相近：信从头到尾讲的，都是神的保守。

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 「雅各的弟兄犹大」。** 作者自称是「耶稣基督的仆人，雅各的弟兄犹大」（1:1）。新约里叫犹大的人不少，但只有一位雅各有名到只要说「雅各的弟兄」，读者就知道是谁：耶路撒冷教会的领袖雅各，就是「主的兄弟」（加1:19；徒15:13）。福音书列出耶稣的弟兄，有「雅各、约西、西门、犹大」（太13:55；参可6:3）。所以传统认为，写这封信的是主耶稣的另一位兄弟犹大。新约也提到一位使徒「雅各的儿子犹大」（路6:16），有人认为作者是他；不过第17节似乎把作者和使徒们分开来说。

**2. 从不信到作仆人。** 耶稣在世的时候，约翰说，连他的弟兄也「不信他」（约7:5）。可是主复活以后，他的弟兄和门徒一同在楼上「同心合意地恒切祷告」（徒1:14）。保罗也提到「主的弟兄」带着妻子往来传道（林前9:5）。犹大没有称自己是「主的弟兄」，只称自己是「耶稣基督的仆人」。从前在耶稣身边长大的人，现在称他为「独一的主宰」（1:4）。

**3. 「主的弟兄」是什么意思？** 雅各书导读已经讲过这三种看法，这里简单列出：
- **约瑟和马利亚所生的儿子：** 这是大多数新教学者的看法，二世纪末的特土良和四世纪的赫尔维迪乌已经这样主张。他们指出，福音书常把这些弟兄和马利亚放在一起（可3:31；徒1:14），又说约瑟「没有和她同房，等她生了儿子」（太1:25）。
- **约瑟前妻所生的儿子：** 这是东正教的传统看法（可追溯到二世纪的《雅各原始福音》和四世纪的伊皮法纽）。他们指出，耶稣在十字架上把母亲交托给约翰（约19:26-27），可见她没有别的儿子。
- **耶稣的亲族：** 这是耶柔米提出、后来天主教普遍采用的看法。他们指出，希伯来文和亚兰文常用「弟兄」称呼亲戚（如创13:8）。《天主教教理》第500条说，这些人是耶稣的近亲。
无论哪一种看法，犹大都是在耶稣身边长大、亲眼见过他的人，后来却称他为主。

**4. 犹大的家人。** 二世纪的赫格西仆记载了一件事（优西比乌《教会史》第三卷第二十章引用）：罗马皇帝多米田在位时，有人把犹大的两个孙子带到皇帝面前，因为他们是大卫的后裔。皇帝问他们有多少产业，他们说只有一小块田地，靠自己耕种过活，并伸出手来给他看，手上满是做工磨出的茧。皇帝又问到基督的国，他们回答说，那不是地上的国，而是天上的国。皇帝看他们不足为患，就释放了他们。这个记载让我们看见，主在世上的亲属是靠双手劳作的穷苦农夫，并不是一个有财有势的家族。赫格西仆还说，他们后来因为是见证人、又是主的亲属，在教会中作领袖，一直活到图拉真在位的时候。

**5. 有人质疑作者吗？** 有些学者认为，这封信是后人托名犹大写的，理由之一是「从前一次交付圣徒的真道」（1:3）这句话，听起来像是使徒时代以后的说法；又说「要记念我们主耶稣基督之使徒从前所说的话」（1:17），好像使徒的时代已经过去了。不过包衡等学者指出，这封信满了犹太人解经的方式，很适合一位一世纪巴勒斯坦的犹太基督徒；而第17节可能只是说，使徒们当初建立这些教会时已经警告过他们。本研读采用传统的看法：作者是主的兄弟犹大。

**6. 写作的时间和收信人。** 信中没有说写给哪里的教会，也没有提到写作的时间。从信中大量引用旧约和犹太传统来看，读者很可能熟悉犹太人的经卷和传统。许多学者认为，信大约写于主后50至60年代。彼得后书导读讲过，彼得后书第2章和犹大书非常相似，多数学者认为是彼得后书采用了犹大书；也有人（如迈克尔·格林）认为是犹大书采用了彼得后书，或两封信用了共同的资料。若是前一种看法，犹大书就写在彼得后书以前。

**7. 被教会接纳的过程。** 犹大书很早就被一些教会接纳，例如一般认为写于二世纪末的《穆拉多利残篇》已经列入这封信。可是也有人迟疑，因为信中引用了《以诺一书》（1:14-15）。耶柔米说，犹大书因为引用了伪经《以诺书》，被许多人拒绝；但他接着说，这封信因年代久远、长期使用，已经有了权威，被列在圣经之中（《名人传》第四章）。优西比乌也把它列在有人质疑的书卷中（《教会史》第三卷第二十五章）。最后，教会还是普遍接纳了它。

## 钥匙经文：1:3；1:20-21；1:24-25

**1. 「为从前一次交付圣徒的真道竭力地争辩」。** 「亲爱的弟兄啊，我想尽心写信给你们，论我们同得救恩的时候，就不得不写信劝你们，要为从前一次交付圣徒的真道竭力地争辩。」（1:3）这里有三个重点：
- **「从前一次」：** 这真道是一次交付、不再更改的。信徒不必等候新的启示来补充它（参加1:6-8）。
- **「交付」：** 这是一个传承的词，就是把领受的东西交给下一代。真道不是我们发明的，是我们领受的。
- **「竭力地争辩」：** 原文是一个运动场上的词，指竭尽全力地奋斗，像赛跑或摔跤的人一样。但这不是叫人好辩，因为犹大在后面说，连天使长米迦勒也「不敢用毁谤的话」（1:9）。

**2. 「你们却要……」。** 「亲爱的弟兄啊，你们却要在至圣的真道上造就自己，在圣灵里祷告，保守自己常在神的爱中，仰望我们主耶稣基督的怜悯，直到永生。」（1:20-21）包衡认为，这两节加上第22-23节，才是全信要读者去做的事。犹大没有叫读者去攻击假教师，而是叫他们在真道上造就自己，在圣灵里祷告，常在神的爱中，等候基督的怜悯。圣灵、父神、主耶稣基督，三位都在这里。

**3. 「那能保守你们不失脚」的。** 「那能保守你们不失脚、叫你们无瑕无疵、欢欢喜喜站在他荣耀之前的我们的救主－独一的神，愿荣耀、威严、能力、权柄，因我们的主耶稣基督归与他，从万古以前并现今，直到永永远远。阿们！」（1:24-25）一封满了警告的信，最后却落在神的保守上。信徒「保守自己」（1:21），是因为神「能保守」他们（1:24）。这段颂赞是新约中最常被教会用来作祝祷和颂赞的经文之一。

## 全书结构

犹大书可以这样分：
- **问安**（1-2节）：写给被召、蒙爱、蒙保守的人；愿怜恤、平安、慈爱多多地加给你们。
- **写信的缘由**（3-4节）：要为真道竭力地争辩，因为有些人偷着进来。
- **神审判的鉴戒**（5-11节）：出埃及后不信的百姓、不守本位的天使、所多玛和蛾摩拉；天使长米迦勒；该隐、巴兰、可拉。
- **这些人的画像**（12-19节）：爱席上的礁石、没有雨的云彩、秋天没有果子的树、海里的狂浪、流荡的星；以诺的预言；使徒的预言。
- **对信徒的劝勉**（20-23节）：在真道上造就自己，在圣灵里祷告，常在神的爱中；怜悯存疑心的人，把人从火中抢出来。
- **颂赞**（24-25节）：那能保守你们不失脚的，愿荣耀归与他。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 和彼得后书对照着读。** 彼得后书第2章和犹大书4-16节有许多相同的内容：偷偷进来的人、犯罪的天使、所多玛、巴兰。彼得后书导读和第2章已经讲过两封信的关系、犯罪的天使和创世记6:1-4、巴兰的故事。读犹大书时，可以翻回彼得后书那里去看。两封信也有不同的地方：犹大用了更多犹太传统（例如米迦勒为摩西尸首的争辩、以诺的预言），又特别讲到该隐和可拉。

**2. 留意犹大引用的犹太传统。** 犹大引用了两段旧约以外的材料：
- **以诺的预言（1:14-15）：** 这段话出自《以诺一书》1.9。《以诺一书》是几部作品的合集；犹大引用的第一部分（第1-36章）大约写于主前三世纪，其余部分较晚，有的可能晚到主后一世纪。这本书在当时的犹太人中很流行。
- **米迦勒和魔鬼的争辩（1:9）：** 据俄利根说，这件事出自一本叫《摩西升天记》的书（《论首要原理》第三卷第二章第一节）；可惜这本书现存的抄本缺了结尾，看不到这一段。
犹大引用这些书，不等于说这些书都是圣经。保罗也称克里特的一位诗人为「本地先知」（多1:12），并不表示那人的书是圣经。犹大说以诺「曾预言」，是肯定这一句话是真的，不是肯定整本书。正像彼得后书第2章所说的，引用一本书，不等于把它当作圣经。（不过也有人看法不同：特土良就把《以诺书》当作圣经，埃塞俄比亚正教会的正典至今也包括这本书。）

**3. 争辩，却不毁谤。** 犹大书容易被人读成一封攻击人的信。可是犹大自己给了界线：天使长米迦勒和魔鬼争辩时，「尚且不敢用毁谤的话罪责他，只说：『主责备你吧！』」（1:9）。信的末了，他又吩咐读者怜悯存疑心的人，把人从火中抢出来（1:22-23）。为真道争辩，不是用恶言对待人，而是守住真道，也把人带回真道里。另外，犹大所说的是「偷着进来」的人，不是叫信徒怀疑每一位忠心的传道人；忠心的牧者是我们应当敬重的（帖前5:12-13；彼前5:2-3）。

**4. 留意「保守」的框架。** 读到第5-16节那些严厉的话，不要忘记信是怎样开始、怎样结束的：读者是「为耶稣基督保守的人」（1:1），神「能保守你们不失脚」（1:24）。警告是真的，但警告是写给被神保守的人的，叫他们不要走那些人的路。

## 默想

**默想：** 「因为有些人偷着进来……将我们神的恩变作放纵情欲的机会，并且不认独一的主宰－我们主耶稣基督。」（1:4）读到这些「偷着进来」的人，我很容易觉得这跟我无关：我没有传假道理，也不认识什么异端。可是「将我们神的恩变作放纵情欲的机会」，不一定要写成一套教导，也可以只是我心里的一个盘算：先上车，后补票。今晚我先由着自己，在网上看那些不该看的东西，或者和一个不是我配偶的人说一些越界的话；明天再认罪就是了，反正神会赦免。我甚至在犯罪以前，就已经把认罪的话预备好了。（如果我是被人逼迫、操控的一方，那不是这里说的罪；离开、求助，必要时报警，都是对的。）

德国牧师潘霍华在《作门徒的代价》里说，廉价的恩典，是没有作门徒的恩典，没有十字架的恩典，没有那位又真又活、道成肉身的耶稣基督的恩典。神的恩典是白白的，却不是廉价的。犹大说这是「我们神的恩」，又说那些人所不认的，是「独一的主宰－我们主耶稣基督」。我们得赎，「乃是凭着基督的宝血，如同无瑕疵、无玷污的羔羊之血」（彼前1:19）。我每次盘算着先犯罪、后认罪的时候，其实是在说：那血不算什么，这位主宰也管不着我今晚的事。

可是我也要小心，不要从这里跳到另一个结论：既然我一直这样，我大概根本不属于主，我就是犹大说的那种人。犹大写信，不是要把挣扎的信徒赶出去，而是要「提醒」他们（1:5）。保罗问：「我们可以仍在罪中、叫恩典显多吗？」他回答：「断乎不可！」（罗6:1-2）可是他也说过：「罪在哪里显多，恩典就更显多了。」（罗5:20）那叫我不愿再拿恩典当借口的，不是别的，正是同一个恩典：「神救众人的恩典已经显明出来，教训我们除去不敬虔的心和世俗的情欲。」（多2:11-12）

今天，我可以不再盘算明天的认罪，而是现在就把这件事带到主面前，也告诉一位可信的弟兄或姊妹，请他为我祷告，也定期问我。认罪从来不是我补的票。那张票，主耶稣早已用自己的血付清了；他付了这么重的价，是把我买回来归他自己。「你们不是自己的人，因为你们是重价买来的。」（林前6:19-20）所以我不能再把他的血当作一张随便用的通行证。他是我「独一的主宰」，也是为我付了全价的救主。

## What This Book Is About

Jude has only twenty-five verses; it is one of the shorter letters in the New Testament. Jude had meant to write "about our common salvation," but he "found it necessary to write appealing to you to contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (1:3). The reason: "certain people have crept in unnoticed," who "pervert the grace of our God into sensuality and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ" (1:4).

So most of the letter describes these people and reminds the readers that God has never let such people go unjudged. Jude gives a string of Old Testament examples: the people who did not believe after the exodus, the angels who left their position, Sodom and Gomorrah (1:5–7). He says these people have walked in the way of Cain, Balaam and Korah (1:11). He uses vivid pictures: waterless clouds, fruitless trees in late autumn, wild waves of the sea, wandering stars (1:12–13).

But Jude's letter is not only warning. Its beginning and its end are about how God keeps his people:
- **The beginning:** the readers are "called, beloved in God the Father and kept for Jesus Christ" (1:1).
- **The middle:** believers are to "keep yourselves in the love of God" (1:21).
- **The end:** God "is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy" (1:24).
Interestingly, the word "keep" (*tēreō*) is also used of the angels who left their position. They "did not stay within" (literally "did not keep") their own position, so God "has kept" them in chains under darkness (1:6, the same word). And for the wandering stars "the gloom of utter darkness has been reserved" (1:13, the same word again). Those who will not keep the place God gave them are kept by God for judgment; those whom God keeps will stand before his glory. Verse 24 uses a different verb (*phylassō*, "guard"), but the thought is the same: from first to last the letter is about God's keeping.

## Author and Setting

**1. "Jude, brother of James."** The writer calls himself "Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James" (1:1). There were many men named Judas (Jude) in the New Testament, but only one James was well known enough that "brother of James" would tell readers who the writer was: James, the leader of the church in Jerusalem, the Lord's brother (Gal 1:19; Acts 15:13). The Gospels list Jesus' brothers as "James and Joseph and Simon and Judas" (Matt 13:55; see Mark 6:3). So tradition holds that this letter was written by Judas, another brother of the Lord Jesus. The New Testament also mentions an apostle, "Judas the son of James" (Luke 6:16), and some have taken him as the author; but verse 17 seems to set the writer apart from the apostles.

**2. From unbelief to servant.** While Jesus was on earth, "not even his brothers believed in him" (John 7:5). But after the resurrection, his brothers were in the upper room with the disciples, "devoting themselves to prayer" (Acts 1:14). Paul also mentions "the brothers of the Lord" traveling with their wives in ministry (1 Cor 9:5). Jude does not call himself "the Lord's brother." He calls himself only "a servant of Jesus Christ." The one who grew up close to Jesus now calls him "our only Master and Lord" (1:4).

**3. What does "the Lord's brothers" mean?** The introduction to James set out three views; here they are in brief:
- **Sons of Joseph and Mary:** This is the view of most Protestant scholars (held by Tertullian in the late second century and by Helvidius in the fourth). They note that the Gospels often place these brothers alongside Mary (Mark 3:31; Acts 1:14), and that Joseph "knew her not until she had given birth to a son" (Matt 1:25).
- **Sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage:** This is the traditional Eastern Orthodox view (traceable to the second-century *Protoevangelium of James* and to Epiphanius in the fourth century). They note that from the cross Jesus entrusted his mother to John (John 19:26–27), which suggests she had no other sons.
- **Relatives of Jesus:** This view was proposed by Jerome and later widely adopted in the Catholic Church. Its holders note that Hebrew and Aramaic often use "brother" for kin (as in Gen 13:8). The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (500) calls them close relations of Jesus.
On any view, Jude grew up close to Jesus and saw him with his own eyes, and later came to call him Lord.

**4. Jude's family.** Hegesippus in the second century records a story (quoted by Eusebius, *Church History* 3.20). Under the emperor Domitian, two grandsons of Jude were brought before the emperor because they were descendants of David. He asked how much property they had. They said they had only a small piece of land, which they farmed themselves, and they showed him their hands, hard with calluses from work. He also asked them about Christ's kingdom, and they answered that it was not earthly but heavenly. The emperor saw they were no threat and let them go. The story shows that the Lord's relatives were poor farmers who lived by their own hands, not a wealthy dynasty. Hegesippus adds that they later led churches, as witnesses and relatives of the Lord, and lived into Trajan's reign.

**5. Is the authorship questioned?** Some scholars think the letter was written later in Jude's name. One reason is that "the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints" (1:3) sounds to them like language from after the apostles' time; another is that "remember... the predictions of the apostles" (1:17) seems to look back on the apostolic age as past. But Bauckham and others point out that the letter is full of Jewish ways of interpreting Scripture, which fit a first-century Palestinian Jewish Christian well, and that verse 17 may simply mean the apostles had warned these churches when they founded them. This study takes the traditional view that the author is Jude, the brother of the Lord.

**6. Date and readers.** The letter does not say which churches it was written to, or when. Since it draws so heavily on the Old Testament and Jewish traditions, its readers were probably familiar with Jewish writings and traditions. Many scholars date it to around the AD 50s or 60s. As the introduction to 2 Peter explained, 2 Peter 2 is very like Jude, and most scholars think 2 Peter used Jude, while others (for example Michael Green) think Jude used 2 Peter, or that both drew on common material. On the first view, Jude was written before 2 Peter.

**7. How the church received it.** Some churches accepted Jude early; the *Muratorian Fragment*, usually dated to the late second century, already lists it. Others hesitated, because the letter quotes *1 Enoch* (1:14–15). Jerome says Jude was rejected by many because it quoted the apocryphal book of Enoch; but he goes on to say that by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures (*Lives of Illustrious Men* 4). Eusebius also lists it among the books some disputed (*Church History* 3.25). In the end the church as a whole received it.

## Key Verses: 1:3; 1:20–21; 1:24–25

**1. "Contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints."** "Beloved, although I was very eager to write to you about our common salvation, I found it necessary to write appealing to you to contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints." (1:3) Three points stand out:
- **"Once for all":** This faith was delivered once and does not change. Believers need not wait for new revelation to add to it (see Gal 1:6–8).
- **"Delivered":** This is a word for handing on a tradition, passing on what one has received. The faith is not something we invented; we received it.
- **"Contend":** The Greek word comes from the athletic arena and means to strive with all one's strength, like a runner or a wrestler. But this is no call to be quarrelsome. Later Jude says that even the archangel Michael "did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment" (1:9).

**2. "But you..."** "But you, beloved, build yourselves up in your most holy faith; pray in the Holy Spirit; keep yourselves in the love of God, waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life." (1:20–21) Bauckham argues that these two verses, together with verses 22–23, are what the whole letter actually asks the readers to do. Jude does not tell them to attack the false teachers. He tells them to build themselves up in the faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep themselves in God's love, and wait for Christ's mercy. The Holy Spirit, God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ are all here.

**3. "Him who is able to keep you from stumbling."** "Now to him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy, to the only God, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, dominion, and authority, before all time and now and forever. Amen." (1:24–25) A letter full of warnings ends on God's keeping. Believers "keep themselves" (1:21) because God "is able to keep" them (1:24). This doxology is one of the New Testament passages the church most often uses as a benediction and in praise.

## Structure of the Book

Jude can be divided this way:
- **Greeting** (vv. 1–2): to those who are called, beloved and kept; may mercy, peace and love be multiplied to you.
- **The reason for writing** (vv. 3–4): contend for the faith, because certain people have crept in.
- **Examples of God's judgment** (vv. 5–11): the people who did not believe after the exodus, the angels who left their position, Sodom and Gomorrah; the archangel Michael; Cain, Balaam and Korah.
- **A portrait of these people** (vv. 12–19): hidden reefs at the love feasts, waterless clouds, fruitless trees in late autumn, wild waves, wandering stars; Enoch's prophecy; the apostles' predictions.
- **Appeal to believers** (vv. 20–23): build yourselves up in the faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in God's love; have mercy on those who doubt; snatch others out of the fire.
- **Doxology** (vv. 24–25): to him who is able to keep you from stumbling be glory.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Read it alongside 2 Peter.** 2 Peter 2 and Jude 4–16 share much of the same material: people who slip in, angels who sinned, Sodom, Balaam. The introduction to 2 Peter and its chapter 2 have already discussed how the two letters relate, the sinning angels and Genesis 6:1–4, and the story of Balaam. When reading Jude, you can turn back to 2 Peter for these. The two letters also differ: Jude uses more Jewish traditions (such as Michael's dispute over the body of Moses and Enoch's prophecy), and he speaks of Cain and Korah as well.

**2. Notice the Jewish traditions Jude quotes.** Jude quotes two passages from outside the Old Testament:
- **Enoch's prophecy (1:14–15):** These words come from *1 Enoch* 1.9. *1 Enoch* is a collection; the part Jude quotes (chs. 1–36) probably dates from the third century BC, and other parts are later, some perhaps from the first century AD. The book was popular among Jews at the time.
- **Michael's dispute with the devil (1:9):** According to Origen, this comes from a book called the *Assumption of Moses* (*On First Principles* 3.2.1). Unfortunately, the surviving manuscript of that work has lost its ending, so we cannot read this passage.
Quoting these books does not mean Jude counted them as Scripture. Paul calls a Cretan poet "a prophet of their own" (Titus 1:12) without making his book Scripture. When Jude says Enoch "prophesied," he affirms that this saying is true, not the whole book. As 2 Peter 2 noted, quoting a book does not make it Scripture. (Not everyone has agreed: Tertullian treated the book of Enoch as Scripture, and the Ethiopian Orthodox canon still includes it.)

**3. Contend, but do not slander.** Jude is easily read as a letter for attacking people. But Jude himself sets the limit: when the archangel Michael disputed with the devil, "he did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, 'The Lord rebuke you'" (1:9). At the end of the letter he tells his readers to have mercy on those who doubt and to snatch others out of the fire (1:22–23). Contending for the faith does not mean using harsh words against people. It means holding to the faith and bringing people back into it. And Jude is speaking about people who have "crept in," not telling believers to suspect every faithful preacher; faithful shepherds are to be respected (1 Thess 5:12–13; 1 Pet 5:2–3).

**4. Notice the frame of "keeping."** When you read the severe words of verses 5–16, do not forget how the letter begins and ends: the readers are "kept for Jesus Christ" (1:1), and God "is able to keep you from stumbling" (1:24). The warnings are real, but they are written to people God is keeping, so that they will not walk the way those others walk.

## Meditation

**Meditation:** "For certain people have crept in unnoticed... who pervert the grace of our God into sensuality and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ." (1:4) When I read about these people who "crept in," it is easy to think this has nothing to do with me: I do not teach false doctrine, and I do not know any heretics. But perverting the grace of our God into sensuality does not have to be written up as a teaching. It can be a quiet calculation in my heart, the kind the Chinese saying puts as "get on the bus now, buy the ticket later." Tonight I will let myself go, look at things online I should not look at, or say things that cross the line to someone who is not my spouse; tomorrow I will confess it, since God will forgive me anyway. I may even have my words of confession ready before I sin. (If I am the one being pressured or manipulated, that is not the sin described here; leaving, asking for help and, where needed, calling the police are right.)

The German pastor Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote in *The Cost of Discipleship* that cheap grace is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ, living and incarnate. God's grace is free, but it is not cheap. Jude calls it "the grace of our God," and says the one these people deny is "our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ." We were ransomed "with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19). Whenever I plan to sin first and confess later, what I am really saying is: that blood does not count for much, and this Master has no say over my evening.

But I must also be careful not to jump from here to another conclusion: since I keep doing this, I probably do not belong to the Lord at all; I am one of the people Jude describes. Jude writes, not to drive struggling believers out, but to "remind" them (1:5). Paul asks, "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?" and answers, "By no means!" (Rom 6:1–2). Yet he also says, "where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" (Rom 5:20). What makes me unwilling to use grace as an excuse any longer is nothing other than that same grace: "For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions" (Titus 2:11–12).

Today, instead of planning tomorrow's confession, I can bring this to the Lord now, and tell a trustworthy brother or sister, asking them to pray for me and to ask me about it regularly. My confession was never the ticket I buy later. The Lord Jesus paid long ago with his own blood, and he paid so great a price to buy me for himself: "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:19–20). So I cannot treat his blood as a pass to use however I like. He is my "only Master," and the Savior who paid the full price for me.
