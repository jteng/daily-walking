## 这卷书在讲什么

腓利门书是保罗书信中最短的一封，只有二十五节，也是最私人的一封。它不是写给一个城市的教会，而是主要写给一个人：歌罗西的信徒腓利门（也问候在他家的教会）。信里只为一件事：腓利门有一个奴仆叫阿尼西谋，离开了主人；他后来遇见了被囚的保罗，信了主。现在保罗要打发他回到腓利门那里去，并且求腓利门接纳他，「不再是奴仆，乃是高过奴仆，是亲爱的兄弟」（1:16）。

这封信没有讲很多教义，却让我们看见福音进到一个家里，会带来什么改变：
- **福音改变一个人：** 阿尼西谋「从前与你没有益处，但如今与你我都有益处」（1:11）。他的名字原文的意思就是「有益处」，从前名不副实，如今他在基督里成了一个新造的人（林后5:17）。
- **福音改变一段关系：** 主人和奴仆，在基督里成了弟兄。保罗没有叫腓利门假装什么都没有发生，而是请他在主里重新接纳这个人。
- **福音改变一个人求人的态度：** 保罗是使徒，本可以「放胆吩咐」（1:8），他却「宁可凭着爱心求你」（1:9）。他甚至说：「他若亏负你，或欠你什么，都归在我的帐上。」（1:18）

所以腓利门书虽然短，却是一幅福音的图画。马丁·路德在为这卷书写的序言里说，基督怎样在父面前为我们求情，保罗也怎样在腓利门面前为阿尼西谋求情。他又说：「我们若信，就都是他（基督）的阿尼西谋。」

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 被囚的保罗。** 保罗一开头就称自己为「为基督耶稣被囚的保罗」（1:1），信中又提到「捆锁」（1:10、13）。他写信的时候在监里，提摩太和他在一起。

**2. 写于何处？** 保罗被囚过好几次，这封信写于哪一次，学者有不同的看法：
- **罗马：** 这是传统的看法。保罗在罗马被软禁两年（徒28:30），信大约写于主后60-62年，和歌罗西书、以弗所书差不多同时。
- **以弗所：** 有些学者认为，保罗在以弗所工作的三年里（徒19:10；20:31）可能被囚过（参林后11:23）。以弗所离歌罗西不远，一个逃走的奴仆比较容易去到那里；保罗也请腓利门为他「预备住处」（1:22），好像很快就要去。若是这样，信大约写于主后54-55年。
- **该撒利亚：** 少数学者认为写于保罗在该撒利亚被囚的两年（徒24:27）。

路加这时和保罗在一起（1:24；西4:14），而使徒行传记载路加曾随保罗到罗马（徒27-28章），这也稍微支持罗马的看法（不过路加也可能曾在该撒利亚陪伴保罗）。本研读采用罗马的看法，但这个问题不影响我们读懂这封信。

**3. 腓利门和他家里的教会。** 腓利门住在歌罗西。那是小亚细亚吕卡斯河谷的一座小城（在今天土耳其西部），离老底嘉和希拉坡里不远。这一点是从歌罗西书看出来的：保罗说阿尼西谋「也是你们那里的人」（西4:9），又托歌罗西人对亚基布传话（西4:17）。
- **一个有家业的信徒：** 他有奴仆，家也够大，可以让教会在他家中聚会（1:2）。
- **保罗带他信主：** 保罗说「连你自己也是亏欠于我」（1:19），很可能是说腓利门藉着保罗信了主。保罗本人似乎没有到过歌罗西（西2:1），腓利门可能是在以弗所听见福音的（徒19:10）。歌罗西的教会很可能是以巴弗建立的（西1:7），他这时也和保罗在一起（1:23）。
- **亚腓亚和亚基布：** 「妹子亚腓亚」（1:2）就是姊妹亚腓亚，很可能是腓利门的妻子。亚基布可能是他们的儿子，也可能是教会中的一位同工；保罗称他为「与我们同当兵的」，歌罗西书也提到他有「从主所受的职分」（西4:17）。

**4. 阿尼西谋是怎样离开的？** 信里没有详细说，只说「他暂时离开你」（1:15），又说「他若亏负你，或欠你什么」（1:18）。学者大致有三种看法：
- **逃走的奴仆：** 这是从金口约翰以来最普遍的看法。阿尼西谋逃离了主人，可能还带走了一些钱财，后来在大城里遇见保罗。
- **求保罗说情：** 有些学者（如彼得·兰普）指出，罗马法学家的著作提到，一个得罪主人的奴仆，可以去找主人的朋友替他说情，这样的奴仆在法律上不算逃奴。阿尼西谋可能是特意去找保罗，求他在腓利门面前说情。
- **主人派去的：** 还有学者（如莎拉·温特）认为，阿尼西谋是歌罗西教会或腓利门派去服事被囚的保罗的人；不过信中「亏负你」「暂时离开你」这些话，用这个看法不太好解释。

无论是哪一种，阿尼西谋都是带着一笔没有了结的帐，走到了保罗面前；也是在保罗那里，他信了主，成了保罗「在捆锁中所生的儿子」（1:10）。

**5. 罗马的奴隶制度。** 当时罗马帝国的人口中，奴隶占了相当大的比例。他们不是按种族划分的，有的是战俘，有的是生下来就为奴，有的是因欠债卖身。有些奴隶做的是医生、教师、管家这样的工作，也有不少奴隶后来得了自由。但无论如何，奴隶在法律上是主人的财产，主人可以责打他、卖掉他。逃走的奴仆若被抓回，常被重重地责罚，有的还被打上烙印。所以阿尼西谋回到腓利门那里，是一件冒险的事。

**6. 一封可以对照的信。** 罗马作家小普林尼留下一封信（《书信集》9.21），是写给朋友萨比尼亚努斯，为一个已经得了自由、却得罪了旧主人的人（被释奴）求情。普林尼说，那人已经认错了，求主人宽恕他，这次就算了。两封信放在一起，可以看见保罗的不同：普林尼求的是主人的宽容，保罗求的却是主人把这个奴仆当作「亲爱的兄弟」（1:16）来接纳；而且保罗愿意亲自替他还债（1:18-19）。

## 钥匙经文：1:15-16；1:17-19

**1. 「或者是叫你永远得着他」。** 「他暂时离开你，或者是叫你永远得着他，不再是奴仆，乃是高过奴仆，是亲爱的兄弟。在我实在是如此，何况在你呢！这也不拘是按肉体说，是按主说。」（1:15-16）保罗说「或者」，没有断言；但他看见，在阿尼西谋离开的这件事背后，可能有神的手。约瑟对哥哥们说：「从前你们的意思是要害我，但神的意思原是好的。」（创50:20）神能把人的过错，变成福音进到一个家里的机会。

**2. 「收纳他，如同收纳我一样」。** 「你若以我为同伴，就收纳他，如同收纳我一样。他若亏负你，或欠你什么，都归在我的帐上；我必偿还，这是我－保罗亲笔写的。」（1:17-19）保罗把自己和阿尼西谋放在一起：接纳他，就是接纳我；他欠的，算我的。这是爱的代价，也让我们想起那更大的代价：神「使那无罪的，替我们成为罪，好叫我们在他里面成为神的义」（林后5:21）。

## 全书结构

腓利门书只有一章，可以这样分：
- **问安**（1-3节）：写给腓利门、亚腓亚、亚基布，和在他家的教会。
- **感恩和祷告**（4-7节）：为腓利门的爱心和信心感谢神；众圣徒的心从他得了畅快。
- **为阿尼西谋求情**（8-16节）：保罗凭着爱心求他；阿尼西谋从前没有益处，如今有益处；不再是奴仆，乃是亲爱的兄弟。
- **愿意替他还债**（17-20节）：收纳他，如同收纳我；他欠的，都归在我的帐上。
- **结语和问安**（21-25节）：深信你必过于我所说的；为我预备住处；同工们问安；愿主耶稣基督的恩常在你的心里。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 和歌罗西书一起读。** 这两封信很可能是同时写的，由同一批人带去：推基古和阿尼西谋一同到歌罗西（西4:7-9）。两封信末了问安的名单几乎一样：以巴弗、马可、亚里达古、路加、底马（1:23-24；西4:10-14）。歌罗西书说，在基督里「并不分……为奴的、自主的」（西3:11），又吩咐作仆人的和作主人的各尽本分（西3:22-4:1）。腓利门书让我们看见，这些话落在一个真实的家里是什么样子。

**2. 留意保罗怎样说话。** 这是一封求人的信，保罗用了很多心思。他先感谢腓利门的爱心（4-7节），再提出请求；他不用使徒的权柄压人，而是「凭着爱心求」（9节）；他说明自己本想留下阿尼西谋，却「不愿意」不经腓利门同意就这样做（13-14节）。读的时候，可以留意保罗怎样尊重对方，又怎样清楚地说出自己的请求。

**3. 诚实地面对奴隶制度的问题。** 今天的读者常会问：保罗为什么不直接叫腓利门释放阿尼西谋？为什么不谴责奴隶制度？对这个问题，我们要诚实：
- **保罗没有明说：** 他求腓利门接纳阿尼西谋为弟兄，又说「知道你所要行的必过于我所说的」（1:21）。有些解经家认为，这是暗示腓利门释放阿尼西谋，或让他回到保罗身边服事；但保罗没有明说。
- **福音的种子：** 但保罗确实把一个奴仆称为「我心上的人」（1:12），要主人把他当作「亲爱的兄弟」，甚至「如同收纳我一样」。他在别处也说，「若能以自由，就求自由更好」（林前7:21；这是多数译本的理解），又把「抢人口」的列在敌正道的罪中（提前1:10；参出21:16）。这些真理一旦在教会里扎根，奴隶制度就站不住了。
- **教会历史的教训：** 历史上，确实有基督徒用这封信为奴隶制度辩护，例如美国南方曾有人引用这封信，支持把逃奴送回主人那里；也有基督徒，像英国的威伯福斯，因着福音一生致力于废除奴隶贸易。我们读这封信，要看见保罗心中的方向：在基督里，人的尊贵不在于他的身份，而在于他是照神形象造的（创1:27），又是基督所爱的人。

**4. 看见福音的图画。** 阿尼西谋欠了债，没有办法回去；保罗站在他和腓利门中间，说：「收纳他，如同收纳我」，「都归在我的帐上」。这是一个人为另一个人说情、替他还债。保罗在这里没有讲这是基督的预表，但历代的信徒读到这里，都不禁想到那位「在神和人中间」的「中保」（提前2:5）。我们读这封信，可以先读懂保罗、腓利门、阿尼西谋之间真实的事，再从这件事里，看见那位更伟大的中保。

## 默想

**默想：** 「他若亏负你，或欠你什么，都归在我的帐上；我必偿还。」（1:18-19）我很能体会阿尼西谋回家路上的心情。我不是逃走的奴仆，却也有一段不敢面对的关系：一个被我得罪了、很久没有联系的家人；一笔借了没还、一直躲着不提的钱；一个我曾经辜负过的教会或同工。我想回去，又怕回去；我不知道对方会怎样看我，也不知道该说什么。阿尼西谋也带着一笔帐，一步一步走回歌罗西；可是他手里有一封信，信上写着：「收纳他，如同收纳我一样。」我在神面前也是这样。我欠的远比阿尼西谋多，我自己还不起；但基督没有只写一封信替我求情，他亲自「担当了我们的罪」（彼前2:24），把我的债都归到了他的帐上，又把他的义归给我（林后5:21）。而且，差他的儿子来替我还债的，正是父自己：「一切都是出于神；他藉着基督使我们与他和好」（林后5:18；参约一4:10）。所以我不是靠自己的表现回到父那里，而是因着基督被父「收纳」，就像保罗求腓利门「收纳他，如同收纳我一样」。今天，我可以先在祷告中向主承认我亏负人的地方，也承认我欠他的债，然后感谢他为我付清了帐。如果这段关系是安全的，我也可以求主给我勇气，迈出回去的第一步：打一个电话，或写一封信。如果那条回去的路已经走不通了，对方已经不在，或者不愿再见我，这笔帐也没有悬在半空：它在神面前已经归在基督的帐上了。若还有可以偿还的（比如还给对方的家人），我也要甘心去还，不是为了赎罪，而是因为我已经蒙了赦免。我不必等自己配得了才回去，因为替我说情的那一位，已经先替我还清了。

## What This Book Is About

Philemon is the shortest of Paul's letters, only twenty-five verses, and the most personal. It is not addressed to a city church but chiefly to one man: Philemon, a believer in Colossae (with greetings to the church in his house). The letter is about a single matter. Philemon had a slave named Onesimus, who had left his master. Onesimus later met Paul, who was in prison, and came to faith. Now Paul is sending him back to Philemon, and asking Philemon to receive him "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother" (1:16).

The letter teaches little doctrine directly, but it lets us see what happens when the gospel comes into a household:
- **The gospel changes a person:** "Formerly he was useless to you, but now he is indeed useful to you and to me" (1:11). The name Onesimus means "useful." Once he did not live up to his name; now in Christ he is a new creation (2 Cor 5:17).
- **The gospel changes a relationship:** master and slave have become brothers in Christ. Paul does not ask Philemon to pretend nothing happened; he asks him to receive this man again, in the Lord.
- **The gospel changes how one asks:** Paul was an apostle and could have been "bold enough... to command" (1:8), yet "for love's sake I prefer to appeal to you" (1:9). He even writes, "If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account" (1:18).

So, short as it is, Philemon is a picture of the gospel. In his preface to the letter Martin Luther wrote that as Christ intercedes for us with the Father, so Paul intercedes for Onesimus with Philemon: "we are all his [Christ's] Onesimi, if we believe."

## Author and Setting

**1. Paul the prisoner.** Paul opens by calling himself "a prisoner for Christ Jesus" (1:1), and he mentions his "imprisonment" again (1:10, 13). He writes from custody, with Timothy beside him.

**2. Written from where?** Paul was imprisoned more than once, and scholars differ on which imprisonment this was:
- **Rome:** the traditional view. Paul spent two years under house arrest in Rome (Acts 28:30), and the letter would date from about AD 60–62, around the same time as Colossians and Ephesians.
- **Ephesus:** some scholars think Paul may have been imprisoned during his three years in Ephesus (Acts 19:10; 20:31; see 2 Cor 11:23). Ephesus was not far from Colossae, an easier destination for a runaway slave, and Paul asks Philemon to "prepare a guest room" (1:22), as though he expects to come soon. On this view the letter dates from about AD 54–55.
- **Caesarea:** a few scholars place it in Paul's two years of custody in Caesarea (Acts 24:27).

Luke is with Paul at this time (1:24; Col 4:14), and Acts shows Luke going with Paul to Rome (Acts 27–28), which lends some support to the Roman view (though Luke may also have been near Paul in Caesarea). This study follows the Roman view, but the question does not affect our understanding of the letter.

**3. Philemon and the church in his house.** Philemon lived in Colossae, a small town in the Lycus valley in Asia Minor (western Turkey today), not far from Laodicea and Hierapolis. We learn this from Colossians: Paul says Onesimus is "one of you" (Col 4:9) and sends a message through the Colossians to Archippus (Col 4:17).
- **A believer of some means:** he owned slaves, and his house was large enough for the church to meet "in your house" (1:2).
- **Brought to faith through Paul:** Paul says Philemon owes him "even your own self" (1:19), most likely meaning that Philemon came to faith through Paul. Paul himself seems never to have visited Colossae (Col 2:1); Philemon may have heard the gospel in Ephesus (Acts 19:10). The Colossian church was most likely founded by Epaphras (Col 1:7), who is with Paul now (1:23).
- **Apphia and Archippus:** "Apphia our sister" (1:2) was most likely Philemon's wife. Archippus may have been their son, or a worker in the church; Paul calls him "our fellow soldier," and Colossians mentions "the ministry that you have received in the Lord" (Col 4:17).

**4. How did Onesimus come to leave?** The letter does not say in detail, only that "he was parted from you for a while" (1:15) and "if he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything" (1:18). Scholars hold roughly three views:
- **A runaway slave:** the most common view, since John Chrysostom. Onesimus fled his master, perhaps taking some money, and later met Paul in a large city.
- **Seeking Paul's intercession:** some scholars (such as Peter Lampe) point out that Roman jurists mention that a slave who had offended his master might go to a friend of the master to plead for him, and such a slave was not legally a runaway. Onesimus may have deliberately sought out Paul to intercede with Philemon.
- **Sent by his master:** others (such as Sara Winter) suggest that the Colossian church or Philemon had sent Onesimus to serve Paul in prison, though phrases like "wronged you" and "parted from you for a while" are hard to explain on this view.

Whichever it was, Onesimus came to Paul with an account still unsettled, and it was there, with Paul, that he believed and became the child "whose father I became in my imprisonment" (1:10).

**5. Roman slavery.** Slaves made up a large share of the Roman Empire's population. Slavery was not based on race: some slaves were prisoners of war, some were born into slavery, some had sold themselves to pay debts. Some slaves worked as doctors, teachers or household managers, and many were eventually freed. Even so, a slave was legally his master's property; the master could beat him or sell him. A runaway who was caught was often severely punished, and some were branded. So for Onesimus to go back to Philemon was a real risk.

**6. A letter for comparison.** The Roman writer Pliny the Younger left a letter (*Letters* 9.21) to his friend Sabinianus, pleading for a freedman who had offended him. Pliny says the man has admitted his fault and asks his friend to forgive him this time. Set side by side, the two letters show what is different about Paul's: Pliny asks the master for leniency, but Paul asks the master to receive the slave as "a beloved brother" (1:16), and Paul offers to pay the debt himself (1:18–19).

## Key Verses: 1:15–16; 1:17–19

**1. "That you might have him back forever."** "For this perhaps is why he was parted from you for a while, that you might have him back forever, no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother—especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord" (1:15–16). Paul says "perhaps"; he does not claim to know. But he sees that God's hand may lie behind Onesimus's leaving. Joseph told his brothers, "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Gen 50:20). God can turn human wrongs into an opening for the gospel to come into a home.

**2. "Receive him as you would receive me."** "So if you consider me your partner, receive him as you would receive me. If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account. I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it" (1:17–19). Paul puts himself beside Onesimus: to receive him is to receive me; what he owes, count as mine. This is the cost of love, and it reminds us of a far greater cost: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21).

## Structure of the Book

Philemon has a single chapter, which can be outlined like this:
- **Greeting** (vv. 1–3): to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the church in his house.
- **Thanksgiving and prayer** (vv. 4–7): thanks to God for Philemon's love and faith; the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through him.
- **The appeal for Onesimus** (vv. 8–16): Paul appeals for love's sake; Onesimus was once useless, now useful; no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother.
- **The offer to pay** (vv. 17–20): receive him as you would receive me; charge what he owes to my account.
- **Closing and greetings** (vv. 21–25): confident you will do even more than I say; prepare a guest room; greetings from fellow workers; the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Read it with Colossians.** The two letters were very likely written at the same time and carried by the same people: Tychicus and Onesimus traveled together to Colossae (Col 4:7–9). The greetings at the end of the two letters name almost the same people: Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Luke, Demas (1:23–24; Col 4:10–14). Colossians says that in Christ "there is not... slave, free" (Col 3:11), and it tells slaves and masters each their duty (Col 3:22–4:1). Philemon lets us see what those words look like when they land in a real household.

**2. Notice how Paul speaks.** This is a letter of request, and Paul writes it with great care. He first gives thanks for Philemon's love (vv. 4–7), and only then makes his request. He does not press his apostolic authority but appeals "for love's sake" (v. 9). He explains that he would have liked to keep Onesimus, but "preferred to do nothing without your consent" (vv. 13–14). As you read, notice how Paul honors Philemon and yet states his request plainly.

**3. Face the question of slavery honestly.** Readers today often ask: why does Paul not simply tell Philemon to free Onesimus? Why does he not condemn slavery? We should be honest about this:
- **Paul does not say it outright:** he asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as a brother, and says, "knowing that you will do even more than I say" (1:21). Some interpreters see here a hint that Philemon should free Onesimus, or send him back to serve with Paul; but Paul does not spell it out.
- **The seeds of the gospel:** yet Paul does call a slave "my very heart" (1:12), and asks the master to treat him as "a beloved brother," even "as you would receive me." Elsewhere he says, "if you can gain your freedom, avail yourself of the opportunity" (1 Cor 7:21, as most translations take it), and he lists "enslavers" among those contrary to sound doctrine (1 Tim 1:10; see Exod 21:16). Once these truths take root in the church, slavery cannot stand.
- **Lessons from church history:** in history some Christians did use this letter to defend slavery; in the American South, for example, some cited it to support returning runaway slaves to their masters. Other Christians, such as William Wilberforce in England, gave their lives, for the gospel's sake, to ending the slave trade. As we read this letter, we should see where Paul's heart points: in Christ, a person's worth lies not in his station but in being made in God's image (Gen 1:27) and loved by Christ.

**4. See the picture of the gospel.** Onesimus was in debt and had no way back. Paul stood between him and Philemon and said, "Receive him as you would receive me," "charge that to my account." One man pleads for another and pays his debt. Paul does not present this as a type of Christ, but believers through the ages, reading these lines, have not been able to help thinking of the "one mediator between God and men" (1 Tim 2:5). As we read, we can first understand the real events among Paul, Philemon and Onesimus, and then, through them, see the greater Mediator.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account... I will repay it" (1:18–19). I can understand how Onesimus felt on the road home. I am not a runaway slave, but I too have a relationship I am afraid to face: a family member I wronged and have not spoken to in a long time; money I borrowed, never repaid, and have avoided mentioning ever since; a church or a coworker I once let down. I want to go back, and I am afraid to go back; I do not know how they will look at me, or what I should say. Onesimus too walked back to Colossae, step by step, carrying an account still unsettled; but in his hand was a letter that said, "Receive him as you would receive me." Before God I am in the same place. I owe far more than Onesimus did, and I cannot repay it. But Christ did not merely write a letter on my behalf; he himself "bore our sins" (1 Pet 2:24), charged my debt to his own account, and counted his righteousness as mine (2 Cor 5:21). And it was the Father himself who sent his Son to pay: "All this is from God, who through Christ reconciled us to himself" (2 Cor 5:18; see 1 John 4:10). So I do not come back to the Father on the strength of my own record; I am received by the Father for Christ's sake, as Paul asked Philemon to "receive him as you would receive me." Today I can first confess to the Lord in prayer where I have wronged others, and the debt I owe him, and then thank him for paying the account in full. And if the relationship is a safe one, I can ask the Lord for courage to take the first step back: a phone call, or a letter. And if that road back is closed, if the person is gone or will not see me, the account is not left hanging; before God it has already been charged to Christ. Where something can still be repaid (to the person's family, say), I will gladly repay it, not to atone, but because I have been forgiven. I need not wait until I deserve it before I go back, because the one who pleads for me has already paid it all.
