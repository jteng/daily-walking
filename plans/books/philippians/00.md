## 这卷书在讲什么

腓立比书是保罗写给一个他深爱的教会的感谢信。他写信的时候正在坐监（1:7、13），前途未卜，可能被释放，也可能被处死（1:20-24）；可是全信「喜乐」「欢喜」这类词出现了十几次。保罗的喜乐不是来自顺境，而是来自基督：「你们要靠主常常喜乐。我再说，你们要喜乐」（4:4）。

这封信有几条线交织在一起：
- **感谢：** 腓立比教会从起头就和保罗同工，多次供给他的需要，这一次又打发以巴弗提带着馈送来到监里（2:25；4:10-18）。
- **合一：** 教会里有人彼此不和（2:2-4；4:2-3），保罗就把基督「虚己」「卑微」的心摆在他们面前（2:5-11）。
- **警戒：** 有人要信徒靠割礼和律法得义，保罗就用自己的见证说明，惟有「信基督的义」才是真义（3:2-11）。
- **盼望：** 信徒是「天上的国民」，等候主从天上降临（3:20-21）。

所以，腓立比书不只是叫人「开心一点」；它把喜乐、合一和盼望都牢牢地建立在基督身上。

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 作者。** 书信开头写着「基督耶稣的仆人保罗和提摩太」（1:1）。提摩太是同写信的人，但信中用「我」说话的是保罗。从初期教会到今天，几乎所有学者都接受这是保罗的书信。

**2. 腓立比城和教会的开始。** 腓立比在马其顿，是罗马的殖民地，城里有许多退伍的罗马军人和他们的后代，以有罗马公民的身份为荣（参徒16:21、37）。保罗在第二次旅行时看见马其顿人的异象，就过到欧洲来（徒16:9）。腓立比的教会是这样开始的：在河边祷告的地方，卖紫色布疋的吕底亚信了主（徒16:13-15）；一个被巫鬼所附的使女得了释放（徒16:16-18）；保罗和西拉被打、下在监里，半夜祷告唱诗，禁卒和他全家信了主（徒16:25-34）。一个富商，一个使女，一个罗马狱卒：这个教会从第一天起，就是一群原本不会坐在一起的人。

**3. 写作时间与地点。** 保罗是在监禁中写的，信里提到「御营全军」（1:13）和「凯撒家里的人」（4:22）。传统认为是在罗马的监禁（徒28:16、30），大约在主后60-62年。也有学者（如杰拉尔德·霍桑）认为是在该撒利亚（徒23:35；24:27），也有学者（如阿道夫·戴斯曼、乔治·邓肯）认为是在以弗所的一次监禁，大约在主后50年代中期，理由是腓立比和以弗所之间来往比较近，信中提到几次往返。「御营」和「凯撒家里的人」在罗马最自然，但在行省的总督府也可能有，所以这个问题没有定论，也不影响我们读懂这封信。

**4. 一封信还是几封信？** 3:1「我还有话说」（原文也可译作：最后）之后，3:2的语气突然变得很严厉，所以有学者认为现在的腓立比书是由两三封信合成的。不过，许多学者（如戈登·费、莫伊塞斯·席尔瓦）指出，这封信从头到尾用词和主题前后呼应（例如「思念」「同心」「喜乐」，以及2:6-8和3:7-10、20-21的对照），保罗写信时语气转变也不少见，所以把它看作一封完整的信最为合理。

## 钥匙经文：1:6

**1. 「我深信」。** 保罗在监里，前途不明，却「深信」。他的把握不是建立在腓立比信徒有多好，而是建立在神的信实上。

**2. 「那在你们心里动了善工的」。** 善工是神开始的。腓立比教会的起头，是「主就开导她的心」（徒16:14），是神在半夜震开了监门（徒16:26）。不是我们先找到神，而是神先找到我们。

**3. 「必成全这工」。** 神开始的事，他一定完成。他不会半途而废，也不会做到一半就把我们丢下。这是信徒得以坚忍到底的根基（参罗8:29-30）。

**4. 「直到耶稣基督的日子」。** 这工作要一直做到基督再来。所以信徒现在还没有完全（3:12），但我们不是在等一个不确定的结局，而是在等「主耶稣基督从天上降临」（3:20）。

## 全书结构

腓立比书是一封书信，不像论文那样一层层推论，但大致可以这样分：
- **问安、感谢和祷告**（1:1-11）：保罗为腓立比人从头一天就同心兴旺福音感谢神，又为他们的爱心多而又多祷告。
- **保罗在监里的景况**（1:12-26）：捆锁叫福音兴旺；「我活着就是基督，我死了就有益处」（1:21）。
- **与福音相称的生活**（1:27–2:18）：同心站立得稳；以基督耶稣的心为心（2:5-11）；恐惧战兢做成得救的工夫（2:12-13）；在弯曲悖谬的世代中好像明光照耀。
- **两位同工**（2:19-30）：提摩太和以巴弗提。
- **真义和假义**（3:1–4:1）：防备靠肉体的人；保罗把从前的一切当作有损的，「为要得着基督」；忘记背后，努力面前；我们是天上的国民。
- **劝勉、平安和感谢**（4:2-23）：友阿蝶和循都基要在主里同心；「应当一无挂虑」；凡是真实的……要思念；保罗学会了知足，并为腓立比人的馈送感谢神。

全书的中心是2:5-11。基督「虚己」「卑微」「顺服，以至于死」，神就将他「升为至高」。信中其他的劝勉，例如谦卑、同心、喜乐、不靠自己的义，都从这段经文流出来。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 留意「喜乐」。** 每次读到「喜乐」「欢喜」，问一问：保罗当时在什么处境里？他为什么喜乐？你会发现，他的喜乐几乎每一次都和基督、福音或弟兄姊妹有关。

**2. 留意「心」和「思念」。** 「以基督耶稣的心为心」（2:5）、「意念相同」（2:2）、「专以地上的事为念」（3:19）、「这些事你们都要思念」（4:8）：原文常用同一个动词，意思是思想、存心、看事情的眼光。腓立比书要改变的，不只是我们的行为，也是我们看事情的眼光。

**3. 留意保罗怎样谈基督。** 基督是保罗活着的目的（1:21），是我们谦卑的榜样和救主（2:5-11），是他要得着的至宝（3:8），也是加给他力量的那一位（4:13）。

**4. 读的时候想着一个真实的教会。** 这不是写给理想教会的信。里面有不和的同工，有病得几乎要死的弟兄，有要供给穷困使徒的普通信徒。读的时候，可以想着自己所在的教会。

## 默想

**默想：** 「我深信那在你们心里动了善工的，必成全这工，直到耶稣基督的日子」（1:6）。保罗为腓立比人「从头一天直到如今」（1:5）感谢神。我和主的故事，「头一天」是什么样子？也许是在一次聚会里，第一次听懂了耶稣为我的罪死；也许是在一段很黑的日子里，有人为我祷告，我第一次开口叫「主啊」。从那时到现在，我也许走过一段冷淡的路，也许有一些至今还没有改掉的老毛病，心里常常怀疑：神会不会对我失去耐心？腓立比教会的起头，是神开导了吕底亚的心，是神在半夜震开了监门。我的起头也一样：是他先找到我，是基督先为我舍了自己。这工作是他开始的，所以也是他来完成，不是靠我自己撑到最后。今天，我能不能写下我信主的「头一天」，以及从那时到现在神保守我的几件事，为每一件感谢他？然后把我还没有完成的那部分，就是那些我改不掉、想不通、看不到结局的事，交在那位「必成全这工」的神手中。基督已经开始了，他必做成。

## What This Book Is About

Philippians is Paul's thank-you letter to a church he deeply loved. He wrote it from prison (1:7, 13), not knowing whether he would be released or put to death (1:20–24), yet words for "joy" and "rejoice" occur more than a dozen times. Paul's joy did not come from good circumstances but from Christ: "Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, Rejoice" (4:4).

Several threads run through the letter:
- **Thanks:** the Philippians had been Paul's partners from the start, supplied his needs more than once, and had now sent Epaphroditus with a gift to the prison (2:25; 4:10–18).
- **Unity:** some in the church were at odds (2:2–4; 4:2–3), so Paul set before them the mind of Christ, who "made himself nothing" and "humbled himself" (2:5–11).
- **Warning:** some wanted believers to be righteous by circumcision and the law, so Paul used his own story to show that the only true righteousness is "that which comes through faith in Christ" (3:2–11).
- **Hope:** believers are citizens of heaven, waiting for the Lord to come from heaven (3:20–21).

So Philippians does not simply tell people to "cheer up." It grounds joy, unity and hope firmly in Christ.

## Author and Setting

**1. Author.** The letter opens, "Paul and Timothy, servants of Christ Jesus" (1:1). Timothy joins in sending it, but the one who speaks as "I" is Paul. From the early church until today, nearly all scholars accept it as Paul's letter.

**2. The city and the beginning of the church.** Philippi was in Macedonia and was a Roman colony. Many retired Roman soldiers and their descendants lived there, proud of their Roman citizenship (see Acts 16:21, 37). On his second journey Paul saw a vision of a man of Macedonia and crossed into Europe (Acts 16:9). The church in Philippi began like this: at a place of prayer by the river, Lydia, a seller of purple goods, came to faith (Acts 16:13–15); a slave girl with a spirit of divination was set free (Acts 16:16–18); Paul and Silas were beaten and thrown into prison, prayed and sang hymns at midnight, and the jailer and his household believed (Acts 16:25–34). A wealthy merchant, a slave girl and a Roman jailer: from its first day this church was made of people who would never otherwise have sat together.

**3. Date and place.** Paul wrote while in custody, and he mentions "the whole imperial guard" (1:13) and "those of Caesar's household" (4:22). Tradition places the letter in his Roman imprisonment (Acts 28:16, 30), around AD 60–62. Some scholars (such as Gerald Hawthorne) suggest Caesarea (Acts 23:35; 24:27); others (such as Adolf Deissmann and George Duncan) suggest an imprisonment in Ephesus in the mid-50s, because Ephesus is nearer to Philippi and the letter mentions several journeys back and forth. "Imperial guard" and "Caesar's household" fit Rome most naturally, but both could also be found at a governor's headquarters in a province. The question is open, and it does not affect our understanding of the letter.

**4. One letter or several?** After "Finally" in 3:1, the tone of 3:2 turns sharply severe, so some scholars think our Philippians was put together from two or three letters. But many scholars (such as Gordon Fee and Moisés Silva) point out that words and themes echo from beginning to end (for example "think," "same mind" and "joy," and the parallels between 2:6–8 and 3:7–10, 20–21), and that shifts of tone are common in Paul's letters. It is most reasonable to read it as one complete letter.

## Key Verse: 1:6

**1. "I am sure of this."** Paul is in prison with an uncertain future, yet he is "sure." His confidence does not rest on how good the Philippians are but on the faithfulness of God.

**2. "He who began a good work in you."** God began the good work. The Philippian church began when "the Lord opened her heart" (Acts 16:14) and when God shook open the prison doors at midnight (Acts 16:26). We did not find God first; he found us.

**3. "Will bring it to completion."** What God begins, he will finish. He does not give up halfway or leave us half-made. This is the ground on which believers persevere to the end (see Rom 8:29–30).

**4. "At the day of Jesus Christ."** This work goes on until Christ returns. So believers are not yet perfect (3:12), but we are not waiting for an uncertain ending; we "await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ" (3:20).

## The Shape of the Book

Philippians is a letter, not a treatise that argues step by step, but it can be roughly divided like this:
- **Greeting, thanksgiving and prayer** (1:1–11): Paul thanks God for the Philippians' partnership in the gospel from the first day, and prays that their love may abound more and more.
- **Paul's situation in prison** (1:12–26): his imprisonment has advanced the gospel; "to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (1:21).
- **A life worthy of the gospel** (1:27–2:18): standing firm in one spirit; having the mind of Christ Jesus (2:5–11); working out salvation with fear and trembling (2:12–13); shining as lights in a crooked and twisted generation.
- **Two fellow workers** (2:19–30): Timothy and Epaphroditus.
- **True and false righteousness** (3:1–4:1): beware of those who put confidence in the flesh; Paul counts everything as loss "in order that I may gain Christ"; forgetting what lies behind and straining forward; our citizenship is in heaven.
- **Exhortation, peace and thanks** (4:2–23): Euodia and Syntyche are to agree in the Lord; "do not be anxious about anything"; whatever is true… think about these things; Paul has learned contentment and thanks God for the Philippians' gift.

The center of the letter is 2:5–11. Christ "made himself nothing," "humbled himself," became "obedient to the point of death," and so God "highly exalted him." The other exhortations of the letter (humility, unity, joy, not trusting one's own righteousness) flow from this passage.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Watch for "joy."** Each time you read "joy" or "rejoice," ask: what situation was Paul in, and why was he rejoicing? You will find that his joy almost always has to do with Christ, the gospel or his brothers and sisters.

**2. Watch for "mind" and "think."** "Have this mind among yourselves" (2:5), "being of the same mind" (2:2), "with minds set on earthly things" (3:19), "think about these things" (4:8): the Greek often uses the same verb, meaning to think, to set one's mind, to have a way of seeing. Philippians aims to change not only how we act but how we see.

**3. Watch how Paul speaks of Christ.** Christ is the purpose of Paul's life (1:21), our example of humility and our Savior (2:5–11), the treasure Paul wants to gain (3:8), and the one who strengthens him (4:13).

**4. Read with a real church in mind.** This letter was not written to an ideal church. In it are co-workers who cannot agree, a brother who nearly died of illness, and ordinary believers supplying a poor apostle. As you read, think of the church you belong to.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "And I am sure of this, that he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ" (1:6). Paul thanks God for the Philippians "from the first day until now" (1:5). What was the "first day" of my story with the Lord like? Perhaps at a meeting I understood for the first time that Jesus died for my sins; perhaps in a very dark season someone prayed for me and I said "Lord" for the first time. Between then and now I may have walked through cold stretches, and I may still have old habits I have not overcome, so I often wonder whether God will lose patience with me. The Philippian church began when God opened Lydia's heart and shook open the prison doors at midnight. My beginning was the same: he found me first, and Christ gave himself for me first. He began this work, so he is the one who will complete it; it does not depend on my holding on to the end by my own strength. Today, can I write down the "first day" of my faith, and a few of the ways God has kept me from then until now, and thank him for each one? Then can I place the unfinished part (what I cannot change, cannot understand, cannot see the end of) in the hands of the God who "will bring it to completion"? Christ has begun it, and he will finish it.
