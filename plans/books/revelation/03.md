## 本章脉络

第三章是七封信的后三封，格式和第二章相同（见第二章开头的本章脉络）：基督取用第一章异象中的称号，说「我知道」，然后称赞或责备、呼召、应许得胜的人。

**本章的三封信：**
- **撒狄**（1-6节）：「按名你是活的，其实是死的。」几乎没有称赞，只有「几名」未曾污秽衣服的人。
- **非拉铁非**（7-13节）：「你略有一点力量」，却遵守了主的道。这封信和士每拿一样，只有称赞，没有责备。
- **老底嘉**（14-22节）：「我是富足……一样都不缺」，主却一句称赞也没有，只有劝告和恳切的邀请。

**七封信合起来看：** 许多学者注意到一个对称的排列：第一封和第七封（以弗所、老底嘉）都在严重的危险中；第二封和第六封（士每拿、非拉铁非）都是受逼迫的小教会，没有责备；中间三封好坏参半。七封信的应许，几乎都在全书末了的新天新地里实现：白衣（19:8）、生命册（21:27）、神的名写在额上（22:4）、新耶路撒冷（21:2）。

## 深读 3:1-6 撒狄：按名是活的，其实是死的

**1. 撒狄这座城。** 撒狄曾是吕底亚王国的首都，以富有闻名的克罗伊斯就是这里的王。卫城建在三面悬崖的山上，被认为攻不破，却两次因为无人看守而失陷：一次被波斯王古列攻下（希罗多德《历史》1.84），一次被安提阿古三世攻下（波利比乌斯《历史》7.15-18）。有学者（如威廉·拉姆齐、科林·赫默）认为，到了约翰的时代，撒狄是一个靠过去名声过日子的城市。

**2. 「那有神的七灵和七星的」。** 基督向这个属灵上死了的教会，自称有「神的七灵」（这七灵是指圣灵还是七位天使，见第一章）。许多学者认为这是指圣灵的丰满，正是撒狄所需要的：只有赐生命的灵能使死人复活。

**3. 「按名你是活的，其实是死的」。** 「我知道你的行为，按名你是活的，其实是死的。」（3:1）撒狄教会也许聚会照常，活动很多。奇怪的是，主没有提到逼迫，也没有提到假教师；一个和周围世界相安无事的教会，可能正因为这样才睡着了。他们的行为「没有一样是完全的」（3:2），有头无尾。人给的名声和主所记的名（4-5节），可能完全不同。

**4. 「你要警醒」。** 「你要警醒，坚固那剩下将要衰微的……所以要回想你是怎样领受、怎样听见的，又要遵守，并要悔改。若不警醒，我必临到你那里，如同贼一样。」（3:2-3）这座城两次因为没有看守而失陷，城里的人读到这警告，大概格外刺耳。
- 「如同贼一样」：耶稣和使徒都用这个比喻讲主的再来（16:15；太24:43；帖前5:2）。有人认为这里是指主的最后再来；也有人认为，既然说「若不警醒」，这是指主在历史中对这个教会的审判。两种读法都提醒我们：警醒的人盼望他来；沉睡的人却要被他的来临惊醒。
- 药方是回到起初所领受的福音。

**5. 「他们要穿白衣与我同行」。** 「然而在撒狄，你还有几名是未曾污秽自己衣服的，他们要穿白衣与我同行，因为他们是配得过的。」（3:4）
- 「污秽自己衣服」大概是指与周围的罪恶和偶像崇拜妥协。
- 「配得过」不是说他们靠自己赚得了白衣。在启示录里，白衣是「用羔羊的血」洗白的（7:14），细麻衣是「蒙恩得穿」的，而这细麻衣「就是圣徒所行的义」（19:8）：这义是白白赐下的，又在行为上显出来。
- 在一个死了的教会里，主仍然认得「几名」。

**6. 生命册。** 「凡得胜的必这样穿白衣，我也必不从生命册上涂抹他的名；且要在我父面前，和我父众使者面前，认他的名。」（3:5）旧约也有「你所写的册」（出32:32）。「必不」在原文是很强的否定。这句话怎样理解，信徒之间有不同的看法：
- **改革宗**的读法（如《威斯敏斯德信条》第十七章论圣徒的坚忍；当代学者如毕尔）认为这是加强语气的应许：真正名字写在生命册上的人，绝不会被涂抹。启示录17:8说到名字「从创世以来」记在（或没有记在）生命册上，可见名字是神预先记下的（参13:8，那一节的语法可以有两种读法）。
- **阿民念派和卫斯理宗**的读法（如约翰·卫斯理1751年论圣徒坚忍的文章）认为，既然主说必不涂抹，就暗示一个人若不得胜，名字就可能被涂抹（参出32:33；启22:19），所以这节经文同时是安慰，也是警告。

两种读法都同意：这节经文是写给得胜的人的安慰，不是要叫信徒活在恐惧里；而得胜的路，就是持守所领受的福音。主还要在父面前「认他的名」（参太10:32）。

> 要点：一个教会可能名声在外，其实已经睡着；主呼召我们回到所领受的福音，也认得每一个穿着他所赐白衣的人。

## 深读 3:7-13 非拉铁非：敞开的门

**1. 非拉铁非这座城。** 非拉铁非是七城中最年轻的一个，城名的意思是「爱弟兄的」。它位于通往内陆弗吕家的大路上。主后17年大地震之后，余震多年不断，按斯特拉波的记载，城墙天天出现裂缝，许多居民宁可住到城外的田野里（斯特拉波《地理志》12.8.18；13.4.10）。城为了感谢皇帝的援助，一度改名为新该撒利亚，后来又称弗拉维亚。

**2. 「圣洁、真实」与「大卫的钥匙」。** 「那圣洁、真实、拿着大卫的钥匙、开了就没有人能关、关了就没有人能开的，说……」（3:7）
- 「圣洁、真实」在启示录6:10是殉道者对神的称呼：「圣洁真实的主啊」。基督取用神的称号，可见他与神同等。
- 这句话出自以赛亚书：神把以利亚敬立为王宫的总管，「我必将大卫家的钥匙放在他肩头上。他开，无人能关；他关，无人能开」（赛22:22）。总管决定谁可以进王宫；如今基督决定谁可以进神的国。非拉铁非的信徒也许被当地的会堂赶了出来（参本章9节），主却说，开门关门的是他。

**3. 敞开的门与「一点力量」。** 「我知道你的行为，你略有一点力量，也曾遵守我的道，没有弃绝我的名。看哪，我在你面前给你一个敞开的门，是无人能关的。」（3:8）这门最可能是进入神国的门：别人关了门，主却开了一个谁也关不上的门；也有人认为是传福音的门（参林前16:9）。两种读法可以并存。主没有责备这个小教会力量小，反而称赞他们在软弱中持守。在主眼中，教会的大小和预算，不是衡量忠心的尺度。

**4. 「知道我是已经爱你了」。** 「那撒但一会的……我要使他们来，在你脚前下拜，也使他们知道我是已经爱你了。」（3:9）「撒但一会的」该怎样小心理解，第二章士每拿的信已经讲过。这里有一个惊人的反转：以赛亚曾应许，苦待以色列的列国「都要在你脚下跪拜」（赛60:14）；神也对以色列说：「又因我爱你。」（赛43:4）如今这些话却用在一个大概以外邦人为主、被会堂排斥的小教会身上。这不是说神已经弃绝了他的古民（罗11:1-2、28-29）。「下拜」不是指敬拜信徒，而是承认神与他们同在。有人认为这里带着对方归信的盼望；也有人认为这是末日被迫的承认。

**5. 「保守你免去你的试炼」。** 「你既遵守我忍耐的道，我必在普天下人受试炼的时候，保守你免去你的试炼。」（3:10）这节经文在末世论中常被讨论：
- **时代论灾前被提**的读法（如约翰·华尔伏）认为，这是指教会在大灾难之前被提，离开世界；他们强调，主应许保守他们免去的是那「时候」本身，不只是试炼。他们也常把4:1「你上到这里来」看作被提的预表。
- **历史前千禧年派和许多无千禧年派**的读法（如乔治·赖德、毕尔、格兰特·奥斯本）认为，这是指在试炼中被保守，不被它所害。他们指出，耶稣在约翰福音17:15用了同样的动词和介词：「不求你叫他们离开世界，只求你保守他们脱离那恶者。」
- **过去派**（如肯尼斯·詹特里）则把这试炼和第一世纪罗马世界的大动荡连在一起。

各种读法都同意：主不会让属他的人被那试炼吞灭。无论是被接去，还是在苦难中被保守，保守他们的都是主自己。

**6. 神殿中的柱子与新名。** 「得胜的，我要叫他在我神殿中作柱子，他也必不再从那里出去。」（3:12）这城的人常常因为地震逃到城外，这应许对他们大概是很深的安慰；被会堂赶出去的人，却在神的同在中有了永远的位置（参21:22）。城可以为了皇帝改名，主却把他自己的名写在信徒身上：他们是属他的。

> 要点：力量小的教会不必羡慕大教会；那拿着大卫钥匙的主为他们开了谁也关不上的门，要他们知道「我是已经爱你了」。

## 深读 3:14-18 老底嘉：不冷也不热

**1. 老底嘉这座城。** 老底嘉在吕家河谷，靠近歌罗西和希拉坡里（西4:13），保罗也提到一封「从老底嘉来的书信」（西4:16）。这城是富裕的商业和金融中心：西塞罗提到可以在这里兑换汇票（《致友人书信》3.5.4）；斯特拉波记载这里出产乌黑发亮的羊毛（《地理志》12.8.16），城附近还有一所医学院（《地理志》12.8.20），古代文献也提到一种弗吕家眼药，但是否在老底嘉制造，学者看法不一。主后60年左右地震以后，这城没有接受罗马的援助，靠自己的财力重建（塔西佗《编年史》14.27）。

本地缺乏好水，要用石管从南边几公里外引水进城，水到时已经是温的，含有大量矿物质；北边的希拉坡里有温泉，东边的歌罗西有清凉的冷水。

**2. 「那为阿们的」。** 「那为阿们的，为诚信真实见证的，在神创造万物之上为元首的，说……」（3:14）
- 「阿们」的意思是：是的，这是真实的。以赛亚书65:16两次说「真实的神」，希伯来原文直译就是阿们的神。保罗说：「神的应许，不论有多少，在基督都是是的。」（林后1:20）
- 「诚信真实见证的」（参1:5）：老底嘉人不愿听真话，主却是那说真话的见证人。
- 「在神创造万物之上为元首的」：「元首」这个词在原文可以指开端、源头或统治者。古代的亚流派曾用七十士译本的箴言8:22说基督是被造的，今天的耶和华见证人也这样说；但这不合启示录的用法：神被称为「我是初，我是终」（21:6），基督也这样自称（22:13）。这里与歌罗西书1:15-18相近，也合约翰福音所说：「万物是藉着他造的。」（约1:3）基督是万物的源头和主宰，不是受造物之一。

**3. 不冷也不热。** 「我知道你的行为，你也不冷也不热；我巴不得你或冷或热。你既如温水，也不冷也不热，所以我必从我口中把你吐出去。」（3:15-16）有两种主要读法：
- **传统的读法：**「热」是火热爱主，「冷」是公开敌对，「温」是半心半意。可是这样读，主为什么会「巴不得」他们是冷的呢？
- **从当地水源来读：**热水能治病，冷水能解渴，温水却叫人想吐。按这个读法（鲁德威克和格林在1958年提出，后来许多学者采用），问题是他们对主毫无用处。这和3:19并不冲突：主在那里所开的药方，正是重新为他火热，变得有用。

也有学者（如克雷格·柯斯特）提醒，水管的细节不宜推得太远：宴席上的饮料或热或冷，温的叫人反胃，这个比喻本来就很好懂。

**4. 「一样都不缺」。** 「你说：我是富足，已经发了财，一样都不缺；却不知道你是那困苦、可怜、贫穷、瞎眼、赤身的。」（3:17）
这话很像何西阿所责备的以法莲（何12:8）。这城靠自己重建的骄傲，可能已经进了教会。最可怕的是「却不知道」：撒狄是名不副实；老底嘉更糟，连自己的光景都看不见。

**5. 向我买。** 「我劝你向我买火炼的金子，叫你富足；又买白衣穿上，叫你赤身的羞耻不露出来；又买眼药擦你的眼睛，使你能看见。」（3:18）主所开的药方，正对着这城出名的东西（其中眼药一项最不确定）：
- 城里有银行，主却要他们来买「火炼的金子」，就是经过试炼的真信心（参彼前1:7）；
- 城里有出名的黑羊毛，主却要他们买「白衣」，遮盖赤身的羞耻；
- 这一带和一种弗吕家眼药有关，主却说他们是瞎眼的，要买他的眼药。

可是，一个「贫穷」的人用什么来买？以赛亚早就邀请：「没有银钱的也可以来。你们都来，买了吃；不用银钱，不用价值，也来买酒和奶。」（赛55:1）向基督「买」，就是承认自己什么都付不起，空着手来领受。

> 要点：自以为一样都不缺，是最深的贫穷；主邀请我们空手到他那里，领受他的金子、白衣和眼药。

## 深读 3:19-22 凡我所疼爱的

**1. 「凡我所疼爱的，我就责备管教他」。** 「凡我所疼爱的，我就责备管教他；所以你要发热心，也要悔改。」（3:19）在最严厉的话以后，主说出他为什么这样说。这句话来自箴言：「因为耶和华所爱的，他必责备，正如父亲责备所喜爱的儿子。」（箴3:12；参来12:6）
- 对一个一句称赞也没有得到的教会，主说的却是「我所疼爱的」。箴言3:12的七十士译本用的是 *agapaō*，这里约翰用的是 *phileō*；有人认为这更带亲密的情感，不过这两个词的意思大量重叠，不宜推得太远。无论如何，他的责备不是厌弃，而是父亲的爱。
- 「发热心」这个词，和前面的「热」在原文里声音相近，或许是有意呼应。许多人认为，「发热心」用现在式的命令，带有持续的意思；同时主也呼召他们悔改。

**2. 「我站在门外叩门」。** 「看哪，我站在门外叩门，若有听见我声音就开门的，我要进到他那里去，我与他，他与我一同坐席。」（3:20）
- 这节经文常被用来向未信的人传福音，这样用并不错，因为主确实邀请每一个人。不过在上下文中，这话首先是对教会说的：主站在他自己教会的门外。一个教会可以有聚会、有财富，主却在门外。
- 「若有……的」：主对整个教会说话，却向个人发出邀请。即使整个教会都冷淡，任何一个人仍可以开门。
- 他怎样叩门？「若有听见我声音」：他的叩门就是他的声音，就是他的话（约10:27；参徒16:14）。
- 「一同坐席」：在古代，一同吃饭表示亲密的相交。这也指向「羔羊之婚筵」（启19:9）。主耶稣也说过，主人回来，看见仆人警醒，「必叫他们坐席，自己束上带，进前伺候他们」（路12:37）。

**3. 与我同坐宝座。** 「得胜的，我要赐他在我宝座上与我同坐，就如我得了胜，在我父的宝座上与他同坐一般。」（3:21）这是七封信中最大的一个应许，而且是给最不像样的老底嘉的。基督自己也是先「得了胜」才坐上宝座；他的得胜就是十字架和复活。到了第五章，约翰要看见这位得胜的，原来是「像是被杀过的」羔羊（5:5-6）。信徒的得胜，也是靠着他的血，跟随羔羊的路（12:11）。

> 要点：主最严厉的责备，是从他的疼爱而来的；他站在门外叩门，邀请每一个人与他一同坐席。

## 默想

**默想：** 「你说：我是富足，已经发了财，一样都不缺。」（3:17）这几年我们家过得还算不错。在城里买了房，贷款也快还完了；孩子进了好学校，父母身体还硬朗，工作也稳定。教会这几年也兴旺，刚盖了新堂，奉献够用。我照常聚会、照常奉献，可是我已经很久没有迫切地祷告了。不是我不信祷告，而是好像没有什么特别需要求的。读到老底嘉这一段，我第一个反应是：这是说那些有钱人，不是说我。

可是「一样都不缺」不只是银行里的数字。我从小听的是「求人不如求己」，大人教我们凡事靠自己，不要欠人情。这份要强帮我考上了大学，在工作上站稳了脚跟。可是不知不觉，我也这样对待神：我知道自己是罪人，可是在生活里，我很少真的觉得需要他。我对圣经很熟，祷告的话也很会说，属灵上好像也「一样都不缺」。

主对老底嘉说的话很重：「困苦、可怜、贫穷、瞎眼、赤身的。」最可怕的是前面那几个字：「却不知道」。我最大的问题，也许不是缺少什么，而是看不见自己缺少什么。

主没有说：你们既然这么有本事，就自己去补足吧。他说：「我劝你向我买。」老底嘉人有的是钱，可是主所要卖给他们的，是钱买不到的东西。向主买，就是承认我付不起，承认我靠着那份自足的骄傲，在他面前其实是赤身露体的。保罗说：「他本来富足，却为你们成了贫穷，叫你们因他的贫穷，可以成为富足。」（林后8:9）那真正一样都不缺的一位，为了我成了贫穷；我买他的金子，付款的是他。

可是读到「我必从我口中把你吐出去」，我的心一沉：我已经这样温吞地过了好多年。年轻时也曾经火热，后来工作、成家、买房，信仰就成了生活的一部分，不再是中心。主会不会已经把我吐出去了？他今天让我读到这段经文，让我心里不安，这本身就是他的爱，不是他的厌弃。他没有等我先热起来才爱我；他是因为爱我，才叫我醒过来。

我想，我可以从一件很小的事开始：今天在祷告里，先诚实地对主说，我需要你，我不知道自己有多贫穷，求你给我眼药，让我看见。这样的祷告，不需要先有什么属灵的成绩。

主劝老底嘉人买的第二样东西，是「白衣穿上，叫你赤身的羞耻不露出来」。那城里的人以乌黑发亮的羊毛自豪，主却说，他们在神面前是赤身的。这白衣不是我可以织出来的。以赛亚早就欢然说：「我因耶和华大大欢喜……因他以拯救为衣给我穿上，以公义为袍给我披上」（赛61:10）。为了给我穿上这件白衣，羔羊自己被剥去衣服（约19:23-24），挂在十字架上，担当了我的羞耻。有一天，我也要穿着这件衣服站在他面前，不是因为我曾经富足，也不是因为我终于够热心，而是因为他对我这样的人说过：「凡我所疼爱的」（启3:19）。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 3 holds the last three of the seven letters, which follow the same pattern as chapter 2 (see the shape of chapter 2): Christ takes a title from the vision of chapter 1, says "I know," then praises or rebukes, calls, and gives a promise to the one who conquers.

**The three letters in this chapter:**
- **Sardis** (vv. 1–6): "You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead." There is almost no praise, only "a few names" who have not soiled their garments.
- **Philadelphia** (vv. 7–13): "You have but little power," yet it has kept the Lord's word. Like Smyrna, it receives praise and no rebuke.
- **Laodicea** (vv. 14–22): "I am rich … and I need nothing," yet the Lord has not one word of praise, only counsel and an earnest invitation.

**The seven letters together:** many scholars notice a symmetrical arrangement: the first and the seventh (Ephesus, Laodicea) are in grave danger; the second and the sixth (Smyrna, Philadelphia) are small, persecuted churches with no rebuke; the middle three are mixed. The promises of the seven letters are nearly all fulfilled in the new creation at the end of the book: white garments (19:8), the book of life (21:27), God's name on their foreheads (22:4), the new Jerusalem (21:2).

## Going Deeper: 3:1–6 Sardis: A Name for Being Alive

**1. The city.** Sardis had been the capital of the kingdom of Lydia, ruled by the proverbially rich Croesus. Its citadel stood on a hill with cliffs on three sides and was thought impregnable, yet it fell twice because no one was on watch: to the Persian king Cyrus (Herodotus, *Histories* 1.84) and to Antiochus III (Polybius, *Histories* 7.15–18). Some scholars (e.g. William Ramsay, Colin Hemer) think that by John's day Sardis was a city living on its past.

**2. "Him who has the seven spirits of God and the seven stars."** To a church that is spiritually dead, Christ presents himself as the one who has "the seven spirits of God" (on whether these are the Holy Spirit or seven angels, see chapter 1). Many scholars take them as the fullness of the Holy Spirit, just what Sardis needs: only the life-giving Spirit can raise the dead.

**3. "You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead."** "I know your works. You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead" (3:1). Perhaps the church's meetings went on and it was busy. Strikingly, the Lord mentions no persecution and no false teachers; a church at peace with the world around it may fall asleep for that very reason. Its works were not "complete" (3:2), begun and never carried through. The name people give and the name the Lord records (vv. 4–5) may be quite different.

**4. "Wake up."** "Wake up, and strengthen what remains and is about to die … Remember, then, what you received and heard. Keep it, and repent. If you will not wake up, I will come like a thief" (3:2–3). For a city that had twice fallen because no one was on watch, this warning must have stung.
- "Like a thief": Jesus and the apostles use this image of the Lord's return (16:15; Matt 24:43; 1 Thess 5:2). Some take it here as the final coming; others, because it says "if you will not wake up," take it as the Lord's judgment on this church within history. Either way, those who are awake long for his coming; those who sleep will be startled awake by it.
- The remedy is a return to the gospel they first received.

**5. "They will walk with me in white."** "Yet you have still a few names in Sardis, people who have not soiled their garments, and they will walk with me in white, for they are worthy" (3:4).
- "Soiled their garments" probably means compromise with the surrounding sin and idolatry.
- "Worthy" does not mean they earned their white clothes. In Revelation, robes are made white "in the blood of the Lamb" (7:14), and the bride's fine linen is "granted her," yet "the fine linen is the righteous deeds of the saints" (19:8): a righteousness freely given that shows itself in deeds.
- In a dead church, the Lord still knows "a few names."

**6. The book of life.** "The one who conquers will be clothed thus in white garments, and I will never blot his name out of the book of life. I will confess his name before my Father and before his angels" (3:5). The Old Testament speaks of "your book that you have written" (Exod 32:32). "Never" is a strong double negative in the Greek. Believers read the promise in different ways:
- **The Reformed** reading (e.g. the *Westminster Confession* 17 on the perseverance of the saints, and today Beale) takes it as an emphatic promise: no one whose name is truly written in the book of life will ever be blotted out. Revelation 17:8 speaks of names written (or not written) "in the book of life from the foundation of the world," implying that the names were recorded by God beforehand (cf. 13:8, whose syntax can be read two ways).
- **The Arminian and Wesleyan** reading (e.g. John Wesley's 1751 *Serious Thoughts upon the Perseverance of the Saints*) holds that "I will never blot his name out" implies that a name could be blotted out if one does not conquer (cf. Exod 32:33; Rev 22:19), so the verse is both comfort and warning.

Both agree that the verse is written to comfort those who conquer, not to make believers live in fear, and that the way to conquer is to keep the gospel received. And the Lord will "confess his name" before the Father (cf. Matt 10:32).

> The point: A church may have a name for being alive and yet be asleep. The Lord calls us back to the gospel we received, and he knows each one who wears the white garments he gives.

## Going Deeper: 3:7–13 Philadelphia: An Open Door

**1. The city.** Philadelphia was the youngest of the seven cities; its name means "brotherly love." It stood on the main road into inland Phrygia. In the years after the earthquake of AD 17 the aftershocks went on and on; according to Strabo, the walls cracked daily and many residents preferred to live in the fields outside the city (Strabo, *Geography* 12.8.18; 13.4.10). In thanks for imperial help, the city was renamed for a time Neocaesarea, and later Flavia.

**2. "The holy one, the true one, who has the key of David."** "The words of the holy one, the true one, who has the key of David, who opens and no one will shut, who shuts and no one opens" (3:7).
- The martyrs address God in 6:10 as "O Sovereign Lord, holy and true." Christ takes God's own title, a sign of his deity.
- The words come from Isaiah. God installed Eliakim as steward of the palace: "I will place on his shoulder the key of the house of David. He shall open, and none shall shut; and he shall shut, and none shall open" (Isa 22:22). The steward decided who could enter the king's house; now Christ decides who enters God's kingdom. The believers in Philadelphia may have been put out of the local synagogue (see v. 9); the Lord says that he is the one who opens and shuts.

**3. An open door and "little power."** "I know your works. Behold, I have set before you an open door, which no one is able to shut. I know that you have but little power, and yet you have kept my word and have not denied my name" (3:8). The door is most likely the door into God's kingdom: others shut the door, but the Lord has opened one no one can shut; some take it as a door for the gospel (cf. 1 Cor 16:9). The two can stand together. The Lord does not rebuke this small church for being weak; he praises them for holding on while weak. In his eyes, a church's size and budget are not the measure of faithfulness.

**4. "They will learn that I have loved you."** "Behold, I will make those of the synagogue of Satan … come and bow down before your feet and they will learn that I have loved you" (3:9). How carefully to read "synagogue of Satan" was set out in the letter to Smyrna in chapter 2. Here there is a startling reversal. Isaiah promised that the nations who afflicted Israel would "bow down at your feet" (Isa 60:14), and God said to Israel, "I love you" (Isa 43:4). Now those words are applied to a small, probably largely Gentile church shut out by the synagogue. This does not mean God has cast off his ancient people (Rom 11:1–2, 28–29). "Bow down" does not mean worshiping the believers but acknowledging that God is with them. Some see hope of conversion here, others a final acknowledgment.

**5. "I will keep you from the hour of trial."** "Because you have kept my word about patient endurance, I will keep you from the hour of trial that is coming on the whole world, to try those who dwell on the earth" (3:10). This verse is often discussed in debates about the end times:
- **The dispensational pre-tribulation** reading (e.g. John Walvoord) takes "keep you from" to mean that the church will be raptured before the great tribulation. They stress that the promise is to keep them from the *hour*, the period itself, not only from the trial. They often see 4:1, "Come up here," as a picture of the rapture.
- **The historic premillennial and many amillennial** readings (e.g. George Eldon Ladd, Beale, Grant Osborne) take it as protection within the trial, not removal from it. They point out that Jesus uses the same verb and preposition in John 17:15: "I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one."
- **Preterists** (e.g. Kenneth Gentry) relate the trial to the upheavals of the first-century Roman world.

All agree that the Lord will not let his own be swallowed by that trial. Whether by being taken or by being kept through suffering, it is the Lord himself who keeps them.

**6. A pillar in God's temple and a new name.** "The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never shall he go out of it" (3:12). For a city whose people often fled outside after earthquakes, this must have been a deep comfort; those put out of the synagogue have a permanent place in God's presence (cf. 21:22). A city might change its name for an emperor; the Lord writes his own name on believers: they belong to him.

> The point: A church with little power need not envy larger ones. The Lord who holds the key of David has opened a door no one can shut for them and wants them to know "I have loved you."

## Going Deeper: 3:14–18 Laodicea: Neither Cold nor Hot

**1. The city.** Laodicea lay in the Lycus valley, near Colossae and Hierapolis (Col 4:13), and Paul mentions "the letter from Laodicea" (Col 4:16). It was a wealthy center of commerce and finance: Cicero mentions cashing bills of exchange there (*Letters to Friends* 3.5.4); Strabo records its glossy black wool (*Geography* 12.8.16) and a school of medicine nearby (*Geography* 12.8.20); ancient writers mention a Phrygian eye remedy, though scholars differ on whether it was made in Laodicea. After an earthquake around AD 60 the city refused help from Rome and rebuilt by its own resources (Tacitus, *Annals* 14.27).

Lacking good water of its own, it piped water through stone conduits from springs several kilometers south; it arrived tepid and heavy with minerals, while Hierapolis to the north had hot springs and Colossae to the east had cold, fresh water.

**2. "The Amen."** "The words of the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's creation" (3:14).
- "Amen" means *yes, truly*. Isaiah 65:16 twice speaks of "the God of truth," literally in Hebrew *the God of Amen*. Paul writes, "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20).
- "The faithful and true witness" (cf. 1:5): the Laodiceans did not want the truth, but the Lord is the witness who tells it.
- "The beginning of God's creation": the word can mean beginning, source or ruler (the CUV has "ruler"). The ancient Arians used the Greek Old Testament of Proverbs 8:22 to say that Christ was created, and Jehovah's Witnesses say the same today; but that does not fit Revelation's usage: God is called "the beginning and the end" (21:6), and Christ calls himself the same (22:13). The verse is close to Colossians 1:15–18 and to John's own Gospel: "All things were made through him" (John 1:3). Christ is the source and ruler of all creation, not one of its creatures.

**3. Neither cold nor hot.** "I know your works: you are neither cold nor hot. Would that you were either cold or hot! So, because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I will spit you out of my mouth" (3:15–16). There are two main readings:
- **The traditional reading:** "hot" is zealous love for Christ, "cold" is open hostility, "lukewarm" is half-heartedness. But then why would the Lord wish they were cold?
- **A reading from the local water supply:** hot water heals, cold water refreshes, lukewarm water makes you want to spit it out. On this reading (proposed by Rudwick and Green in 1958 and taken up by many scholars), the problem is their uselessness to the Lord. This does not clash with 3:19: there the Lord's remedy is renewed zeal for him that makes them useful again.

Some scholars (e.g. Craig Koester) caution against pressing the details of the pipes: drinks at banquets were served hot or cold, and a lukewarm one was nauseating, an image any ancient reader would grasp.

**4. "I need nothing."** "For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing, not realizing that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked" (3:17).
The words echo Hosea's rebuke of Ephraim (Hos 12:8). The city's pride in rebuilding without help may have entered the church. The most frightening words are "not realizing": Sardis had a name it did not live up to; Laodicea was worse, unable to see its own condition at all.

**5. "Buy from me."** "I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, so that you may be rich, and white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen, and salve to anoint your eyes, so that you may see" (3:18). The Lord's prescription targets things the city was known for (the eye remedy is the least certain of the three):
- the city had banks, but the Lord tells them to buy "gold refined by fire," a faith proved genuine in testing (cf. 1 Pet 1:7);
- the city had famous black wool, but the Lord tells them to buy "white garments" to cover the shame of their nakedness;
- the region was linked with a Phrygian eye remedy, but the Lord says they are blind and must buy his salve.

But with what can a "poor" person buy? Isaiah had long since invited: "he who has no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price" (Isa 55:1). To "buy" from Christ is to admit that we can pay for nothing and to come with empty hands to receive.

> The point: To think we need nothing is the deepest poverty. The Lord invites us to come to him empty-handed and receive his gold, his white garments and his salve.

## Going Deeper: 3:19–22 Those Whom I Love

**1. "Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline."** "Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline, so be zealous and repent" (3:19). After the sternest words, the Lord tells why he spoke them. The line comes from Proverbs: "for the LORD reproves him whom he loves, as a father the son in whom he delights" (Prov 3:12; cf. Heb 12:6).
- To a church that received not one word of praise, the Lord says "those whom I love." Where the Greek Old Testament of Prov 3:12 has *agapaō*, John writes *phileō*; some see extra warmth in the change, though the two verbs overlap so widely that the point should not be pressed. Either way, his rebuke is not rejection; it is a father's love.
- "Be zealous" sounds like the Greek word for "hot" earlier, perhaps a deliberate echo. Many take the present imperative "be zealous" as calling for ongoing zeal, alongside the summons to repent.

**2. "I stand at the door and knock."** "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me" (3:20).
- It is often used to invite unbelievers to Christ, and that is not wrong, for the Lord does invite everyone. But in context the words are first spoken to a church: the Lord stands outside his own church. A church can have meetings and money while the Lord stands outside.
- "If anyone": the Lord addresses the whole church but invites individuals. Even if the whole church is cold, any one person can open.
- How does he knock? "If anyone hears my voice": his knock is his voice, his word (John 10:27; cf. Acts 16:14).
- "Eat with him": in the ancient world, sharing a meal meant close fellowship. It also points to "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Rev 19:9). Jesus himself said that when the master returns and finds his servants awake, "he will dress himself for service and have them recline at table, and he will come and serve them" (Luke 12:37).

**3. Sitting with me on my throne.** "The one who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my throne, as I also conquered and sat down with my Father on his throne" (3:21). This is the greatest promise of the seven letters, and it is given to unpromising Laodicea. Christ himself "conquered" before he sat down on the throne; his victory was the cross and the resurrection. In chapter 5 John will see that the one who has conquered is "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (5:5–6). The believer's conquering is the same: by his blood, following the Lamb's way (12:11).

> The point: The Lord's sternest rebuke comes from his love. He stands at the door and knocks, inviting anyone to share his table.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing" (3:17). These last few years our family has done quite well. We bought a flat in the city, and the mortgage is nearly paid off; the children got into good schools; my parents are still in good health, and my job is steady. Our church has grown too: a new building, enough in the offering. I still go to church and still give, but I have not prayed urgently for a long time. It is not that I don't believe in prayer; there just seems to be nothing in particular to ask for. When I read this letter to Laodicea, my first thought was: this is about rich people, not me.

But "I need nothing" is not only a figure in the bank. I grew up on the saying that asking others is worse than relying on yourself. We were taught to depend on ourselves and not to owe favors. That drive got me into university and helped me stand on my own feet at work. But without noticing, I have treated God the same way. I know I am a sinner, yet in daily life I rarely feel that I need him. I know the Bible well, I can pray fluent prayers, and spiritually, too, I seem to "need nothing."

The Lord's words to Laodicea are heavy: "wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked." Most frightening are the words before them: "not realizing." My deepest problem may not be what I lack, but that I cannot see what I lack.

The Lord does not say, "Since you are so capable, make up the lack yourselves." He says, "I counsel you to buy from me." The Laodiceans had plenty of money, but what the Lord offers them cannot be bought with it. To buy from him is to admit that I cannot pay, that for all my self-sufficient pride I stand naked before him. Paul writes: "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). The one who truly needed nothing became poor for me; when I buy his gold, he is the one who pays.

But when I reach "I will spit you out of my mouth," my heart sinks: I have lived lukewarm like this for many years. When I was young I burned for him, but then came work, marriage, a flat, and faith became one part of life instead of its center. Has the Lord already spit me out? That he lets me read this passage today, and lets it unsettle me, is itself his love, not his rejection. He did not wait for me to warm up before loving me; it is because he loves me that he wakes me.

I think I can begin with something very small. Today, when I pray, I will first tell the Lord honestly: I need you; I do not know how poor I am; give me your salve so that I can see. A prayer like that needs no spiritual record to stand on.

The second thing the Lord counsels Laodicea to buy is "white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen." The city was proud of its glossy black wool, but the Lord says that before God they are naked. These white garments are not something I can weave. Isaiah had long ago sung for joy: "I will greatly rejoice in the LORD … for he has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness" (Isa 61:10). To clothe me in white, the Lamb himself was stripped of his garments (John 19:23–24) and hung on the cross, bearing my shame. One day I too will stand before him wearing that robe, not because I was once rich, nor because I finally became zealous enough, but because he has said to people like me, "Those whom I love" (Rev 3:19).
