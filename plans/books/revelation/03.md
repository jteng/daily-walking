## 本章脉络

第三章是七封信的后三封，格式和第二章相同（见第二章开头的本章脉络）：基督取用第一章异象中的称号，说「我知道」，然后称赞或责备、呼召、应许得胜的人。

**本章的三封信：**
- **撒狄**（1-6节）：「按名你是活的，其实是死的。」几乎没有称赞，只有「几名」未曾污秽衣服的人。
- **非拉铁非**（7-13节）：「你略有一点力量」，却遵守了主的道。这封信和士每拿一样，只有称赞，没有责备。
- **老底嘉**（14-22节）：「我是富足……一样都不缺」，主却一句称赞也没有，只有劝告和恳切的邀请。

**七封信合起来看：** 许多学者注意到七封信的排列有一个对称的结构：
- 第一封和第七封（以弗所、老底嘉）的教会都在严重的危险中：一个要被挪去灯台，一个要被「吐出去」；
- 第二封和第六封（士每拿、非拉铁非）都是软弱、受逼迫的小教会，没有责备，两处都提到「撒但一会的」和「冠冕」；
- 中间三封（别迦摩、推雅推喇、撒狄）好坏参半，都面对内部的妥协或沉睡。

还有一个细节：前三封信先说「凡有耳的，就应当听」，再说给得胜者的应许；后四封信次序倒过来，先说应许，再呼召人听。七封信的应许，几乎都在全书末了的新天新地里实现：白衣（19:8），生命册（20:12、15；21:27），神的名写在额上（22:4），新耶路撒冷（21:2），与基督同坐宝座（20:4；22:3）。所以七封信是全书的门口：后面的一切异象，都是为了让这些真实的教会听见主的话，并且得胜。

## 深读 3:1-6 撒狄：按名是活的，其实是死的

**1. 撒狄这座城。** 撒狄曾是吕底亚王国的首都，以富有著称，传说中的富豪克罗伊斯就是这里的王。城的卫城建在险峻的山上，三面是悬崖，被认为攻不破。可是它两次在大意中失陷：主前546年左右，波斯王古列的士兵看见一个吕底亚兵从悬崖小路下来拾头盔，就循着那条路爬了上去（希罗多德《历史》1.84）；主前214年左右，安提阿古三世的军队又趁夜从无人看守的悬崖爬上城墙（波利比乌斯《历史》7.15-18）。主后17年，撒狄在大地震中毁坏得最严重，皇帝提庇留拨款帮助重建（塔西佗《编年史》2.47）。到了约翰的时代，撒狄仍然繁荣，却常被人说是靠过去的名声过日子。

**2. 「那有神的七灵和七星的」。** 基督向这个属灵上死了的教会，自称有「神的七灵」（参1:4；4:5；5:6，这七灵是指圣灵还是七位天使，见第一章）。许多学者认为这是指圣灵的丰满。这正是撒狄所需要的：只有赐生命的灵能使死人复活。他也「有七星」，众教会仍在他手中。

**3. 「按名你是活的，其实是死的」。** 「我知道你的行为，按名你是活的，其实是死的。」（3:1）撒狄教会有名声：也许聚会照常，活动很多，在别的教会眼中是一个兴旺的教会。奇怪的是，主没有提到逼迫，也没有提到假教师。一个不受逼迫、也不受异端搅扰的教会，可能正因为和周围的世界相安无事，才慢慢睡着了。
- 「没有一样是完全的」（3:2）：他们的行为不是全错，而是有头无尾，没有做完。
- 「名」在这封信里出现了好几次：按名是活的（1节）；还有「几名」未曾污秽衣服的（4节）；主必不从生命册上涂抹「他的名」，还要认「他的名」（5节）。人给的名声和主所记的名，可能完全不同。

**4. 「你要警醒」。** 「你要警醒，坚固那剩下将要衰微的……所以要回想你是怎样领受、怎样听见的，又要遵守，并要悔改。若不警醒，我必临到你那里，如同贼一样。」（3:2-3）对这座两次因为没有看守而失陷的城市来说，「警醒」和「如同贼一样」的警告，读起来大概格外刺耳。
- 「如同贼一样」：耶稣和使徒都用这个比喻讲主的再来（16:15；帖前5:2）。这里有人认为是指主的最后再来；也有人认为，既然说「若不警醒」，这是指主在历史中对这个教会的审判。两种读法都提醒我们：主的来临对警醒的人是盼望，对沉睡的人却是突然的。
- 药方仍然是回到起初：「回想你是怎样领受、怎样听见的」，就是回到当初所领受的福音。

**5. 「他们要穿白衣与我同行」。** 「然而在撒狄，你还有几名是未曾污秽自己衣服的，他们要穿白衣与我同行，因为他们是配得过的。」（3:4）
- 「污秽自己衣服」大概是指与周围的罪恶和偶像崇拜妥协。
- 「配得过」不是说他们靠自己赚得了白衣。在启示录里，白衣是「用羔羊的血」洗白的（7:14），细麻衣是「蒙恩得穿」的（19:8）。他们配得过，是因为他们一直穿着主所赐的衣服，没有把它弄脏。
- 在一个死了的教会里，主仍然认得「几名」。没有一个忠心的人是被他忽略的。

**6. 生命册。** 「凡得胜的必这样穿白衣，我也必不从生命册上涂抹他的名；且要在我父面前，和我父众使者面前，认他的名。」（3:5）古代城市有公民名册，人死了或犯了重罪，名字就会被除去；有学者认为撒狄的读者会想到这一点。旧约也有「你所写的册」（出32:32）和「凡名录在册上的，必得拯救」（但12:1）。「必不」在原文是很强的否定。这句话怎样理解，信徒之间有不同的看法：
- **改革宗**的读法（如《威斯敏斯德信条》第十七章论圣徒的坚忍，今天如毕尔）认为这是加强语气的应许：真正名字写在生命册上的人，绝不会被涂抹；13:8也说这些名字是「从创世以来」记在羔羊生命册上的。
- **阿民念派和卫斯理宗**的读法（如约翰·卫斯理1751年论圣徒坚忍的文章）认为，既然主说必不涂抹，就暗示一个人若不得胜，名字有被涂抹的可能，所以这节经文同时是安慰，也是警告。

两种读法都同意：这节经文是写给得胜的人的安慰，不是要叫信徒活在恐惧里；而得胜的路，就是持守所领受的福音。主还要在父面前「认他的名」，正如他说：「凡在人面前认我的，我在我天上的父面前也必认他。」（太10:32）

> 要点：一个教会可能名声在外，其实已经睡着；主呼召我们醒过来，回到所领受的福音。他认得每一个穿着他所赐白衣的人，也必在父面前认他们的名。

## 深读 3:7-13 非拉铁非：敞开的门

**1. 非拉铁非这座城。** 非拉铁非是七城中最年轻的一个，主前二世纪由别迦摩的亚达罗王朝建立，城名的意思是「爱弟兄的」，据说是纪念亚达罗二世对兄长的忠诚。它位于通往内陆弗吕家的大路上，威廉·拉姆齐称它为把希腊文化传到内陆的门户。主后17年的大地震之后，余震持续了很久，按斯特拉波的记载，城墙天天出现裂缝，许多居民宁可搬到城外的田野里住（斯特拉波《地理志》12.8.18；13.4.10）。城后来为了感谢皇帝的援助，一度改名为新该撒利亚。这些背景都有助于读这封信，不过我们不必把每一个细节都和经文对应起来。

**2. 「拿着大卫的钥匙」。** 「那圣洁、真实、拿着大卫的钥匙、开了就没有人能关、关了就没有人能开的，说……」（3:7）这句话出自以赛亚书：神把以利亚敬立为王宫的总管，「我必将大卫家的钥匙放在他肩头上。他开，无人能关；他关，无人能开」（赛22:22）。总管有权决定谁可以进王宫。如今基督拿着这把钥匙，他决定谁可以进神的国。非拉铁非的信徒也许被当地的会堂赶了出来（3:9），被宣告不属于神的百姓；主却说，开门关门的是他。

**3. 「敞开的门」是什么门？** 「看哪，我在你面前给你一个敞开的门，是无人能关的。」（3:8）有两种主要读法：
- **进入神国的门：**这最合上下文。别人关了门，主却给他们开了一个谁也关不上的门，就是进入神同在、进入新耶路撒冷的门。
- **传福音的门：**保罗也说「有宽大又有功效的门为我开了」（林前16:9）。非拉铁非是通往内陆的门户，主可能也给他们传福音的机会。

两种读法可以并存：被主接纳进来的人，也被差派出去。

**4. 「你略有一点力量」。** 「我知道你的行为……你略有一点力量，也曾遵守我的道，没有弃绝我的名。」（3:8）这是一个人数少、没有势力的教会。主没有责备他们力量小，反而称赞他们在力量小的时候仍然持守。在主眼中，教会的大小、影响力和预算，不是衡量忠心的尺度。

**5. 「知道我是已经爱你了」。** 「那撒但一会的……我要使他们来，在你脚前下拜，也使他们知道我是已经爱你了。」（3:9）「撒但一会的」该怎样小心理解，第二章士每拿的信已经讲过，这里不再重复。这里要注意的是一个惊人的反转：以赛亚曾应许，苦待以色列的列国，「都要在你脚下跪拜」（赛60:14）；神也对以色列说：「因我看你为宝为尊；又因我爱你。」（赛43:4）如今这些话却用在一个以外邦人为主、被会堂排斥的小教会身上。「下拜」不是指敬拜信徒，而是承认神与他们同在。许多学者认为，「使他们知道」也带着盼望：那些反对的人终有一天会知道，这群人是神所爱的。

**6. 「保守你免去你的试炼」。** 「你既遵守我忍耐的道，我必在普天下人受试炼的时候，保守你免去你的试炼。」（3:10）这节经文在末世论中常被讨论：
- **时代论灾前被提**的读法（如约翰·华尔伍德）认为，「保守你免去」是指教会在大灾难之前被提，离开世界；他们也常把4:1「你上到这里来」看作被提的预表。
- **历史前千禧年派和许多无千禧年派**的读法（如乔治·赖德、毕尔、奥斯本）认为，这是指在试炼中被保守，不被它所害。他们指出，耶稣在约翰福音17:15用了同样的动词和介词：「不求你叫他们离开世界，只求你保守他们脱离那恶者。」

两种读法都同意：主不会让属他的人被那试炼吞灭。无论是被接去，还是在苦难中被保守，保守他们的都是主自己。

**7. 神殿中的柱子与新名。** 「得胜的，我要叫他在我神殿中作柱子，他也必不再从那里出去。我又要将我神的名和我神城的名……并我的新名，都写在他上面。」（3:12）
- 对一个常常因地震逃出城外的城市来说，「必不再从那里出去」大概是很深的安慰；被会堂赶出去的人，却在神的殿中有了永远的位置。
- 新耶路撒冷没有殿，「因主神－全能者和羔羊为城的殿」（21:22）。所以作「柱子」不是变成建筑物，而是永远住在神的同在中。
- 城可以为了讨好皇帝而改名，主却把他自己的名写在信徒身上：他们是属他的。

> 要点：力量小的教会不必羡慕大教会；那拿着大卫钥匙的主为他们开了谁也关不上的门，要他们知道「我是已经爱你了」，并且应许他们永远住在神的同在里。

## 深读 3:14-18 老底嘉：不冷也不热

**1. 老底嘉这座城。** 老底嘉在吕家河谷，靠近歌罗西和希拉坡里（西4:13）。保罗的同工以巴弗曾为这三个教会劳苦，保罗也提到一封「从老底嘉来的书信」（西4:16）。这城位于交通要道上，是富裕的商业和金融中心：
- **银行：**罗马政治家西塞罗曾提到，他可以在老底嘉兑换汇票（西塞罗《致友人书信》3.5.4）。
- **羊毛：**斯特拉波记载，这里出产柔软、乌黑发亮的羊毛，远近闻名（《地理志》12.8.16）。
- **医学：**城附近有一所著名的医学学校（《地理志》12.8.20），古代文献也提到一种弗吕家的眼药；至于这眼药是否就在老底嘉制造，学者看法不一。
- **自给自足：**主后60年左右，老底嘉在地震中被毁，塔西佗特别记下，这城没有接受罗马的援助，靠自己的财力重建起来（塔西佗《编年史》14.27）。

**2. 「那为阿们的」。** 「那为阿们的，为诚信真实见证的，在神创造万物之上为元首的，说……」（3:14）
- 「阿们」的意思是：是的，这是真实的。以赛亚书65:16两次说「真实的神」，希伯来原文直译就是阿们的神。基督自己就是神一切应许的「阿们」。
- 「诚信真实见证的」（参1:5）：老底嘉人不愿听真话，主却是那说真话的见证人。
- 「在神创造万物之上为元首的」：和合本译作「元首」，也可以译作开端。这个词可以指开端、源头或统治者。今天的耶和华见证人用它来说基督是第一个被造的，但这不合启示录的用法：神被称为「我是初，我是终」（21:6），基督也这样自称（22:13）。这里与歌罗西书1:15-18相近：基督是万物的源头和主宰，不是受造物之一。

**3. 不冷也不热。** 「我知道你的行为，你也不冷也不热；我巴不得你或冷或热。你既如温水，也不冷也不热，所以我必从我口中把你吐出去。」（3:15-16）这句话有两种主要读法：
- **传统的读法：**「热」是火热爱主，「冷」是公开敌对，「温」是半心半意。可是这样读，主为什么会「巴不得」他们是冷的呢？
- **从当地水源来读：**老底嘉本地缺乏好的水源，要用石管从南边几公里外的泉源引水进城，水到城里时已经是温的，而且含有大量矿物质，古水管里至今留有厚厚的沉积。北边的希拉坡里有可以治病的温泉，东边的歌罗西有清凉的冷水。按这个读法（鲁德威克和格林在1958年提出，后来许多学者采用），热水和冷水都有用处，温水却叫人想吐。所以问题不是热情的程度，而是他们对主毫无用处。

也有学者（如克雷格·柯斯特）提醒，水管的细节不宜推得太远，宴席上献给客人的饮料或热或冷，温的叫人反胃，这个比喻在当时已经很好懂。无论哪一种背景，意思是一致的：老底嘉教会已经失去了它的味道和用处。「吐出去」是很强的词，表示主对这种光景的厌恶。

**4. 「一样都不缺」。** 「你说：我是富足，已经发了财，一样都不缺；却不知道你是那困苦、可怜、贫穷、瞎眼、赤身的。」（3:17）
- 这话很像先知何西阿所责备的以法莲：「我果然成了富足，得了财宝。」（何12:8）
- 这个城市地震后不求援助、靠自己重建的骄傲，可能已经进了教会。他们的富足可能是物质的，也可能是属灵的：自以为什么都懂，什么都有。
- 最可怕的是「却不知道」。撒狄还有一个活着的名声；老底嘉连自己的光景都看不见。

**5. 向我买。** 「我劝你向我买火炼的金子，叫你富足；又买白衣穿上，叫你赤身的羞耻不露出来；又买眼药擦你的眼睛，使你能看见。」（3:18）主针对这城引以为傲的三样东西开出药方：
- 城里有银行，主却要他们来买「火炼的金子」，就是经过试炼的真信心（参彼前1:7）；
- 城里有出名的黑羊毛，主却要他们买「白衣」，遮盖赤身的羞耻；
- 城里有眼药，主却说他们是瞎眼的，要买他的眼药。

可是，一个「贫穷」的人用什么来买？这里让人想起以赛亚的邀请：「没有银钱的也可以来。你们都来，买了吃；不用银钱，不用价值，也来买酒和奶。」（赛55:1）向基督「买」，就是承认自己什么都付不起，空着手来领受。只有知道自己贫穷的人，才买得起。

> 要点：自以为一样都不缺，是最深的贫穷，因为这样的人看不见自己需要基督；主不是叫我们靠自己补足，而是邀请我们空手到他那里，领受他的金子、白衣和眼药。

## 深读 3:19-22 凡我所疼爱的

**1. 「凡我所疼爱的，我就责备管教他」。** 「凡我所疼爱的，我就责备管教他；所以你要发热心，也要悔改。」（3:19）在对老底嘉说了最严厉的话以后，主说出了他为什么这样说。这句话来自箴言：「因为耶和华所爱的，他必责备，正如父亲责备所喜爱的儿子。」（箴3:12；参来12:6）
- 对一个一句称赞也没有得到的教会，主说的却是「我所疼爱的」。这里的「疼爱」，原文用的是表达亲密情感的词。他的责备不是厌弃，而是父亲的爱。
- 「发热心」这个词，和前面的「热」在原文里声音相近，许多人认为是有意呼应：主要他们从温水变成热的。它在原文是现在式的命令，意思是要持续地火热；「悔改」则是一次决断的转向。

**2. 「我站在门外叩门」。** 「看哪，我站在门外叩门，若有听见我声音就开门的，我要进到他那里去，我与他，他与我一同坐席。」（3:20）这是圣经中最常被引用的经文之一。
- 这节经文常被用来向未信的人传福音，这样用并不错，因为主确实邀请每一个人。不过在上下文中，这话首先是对教会说的：主站在他自己教会的门外。一个教会可以有聚会、有财富，主却在门外。
- 「若有……的」：主对整个教会说话，却向「个人」发出邀请。即使整个教会都冷淡，任何一个人仍可以开门。
- 「一同坐席」：在古代，一同吃饭表示亲密的相交。这也让人想到主应许的「羔羊之婚筵」（19:9），以及雅歌中良人敲门的声音（歌5:2）。主耶稣也说过，主人回来，看见仆人警醒，「必叫他们坐席，自己束上带，进前伺候他们」（路12:37）。
- 英国画家亨特有一幅名画《世界之光》，画中的门外面没有门把手，表示门只能从里面开。这幅画常被用来讲这节经文：主不破门而入，他叩门，等人开门。

**3. 与我同坐宝座。** 「得胜的，我要赐他在我宝座上与我同坐，就如我得了胜，在我父的宝座上与他同坐一般。」（3:21）这是七封信中最大的一个应许，而且是给最不像样的老底嘉的。基督自己也是先「得了胜」才坐上宝座；他的得胜就是十字架和复活。到了第五章，约翰要看见这位「已得胜」的，原来是「像是被杀过的」羔羊（5:5-6）。信徒的得胜也是这样，是跟随羔羊的路。

**4. 通往宝座的门。** 七封信以「宝座」结束，第四章就以约翰看「见有一个宝座安置在天上」开始（4:2）。七个在地上挣扎的教会，有的受逼迫，有的在妥协，有的睡着了，有的自以为富足；可是在它们上面，有一个宝座。那在灯台中间行走的主，就是将要在宝座上与父同坐的主。

> 要点：主最严厉的责备，是从他的疼爱而来的；他站在冷淡的教会门外叩门，邀请每一个人与他一同坐席，并且要让得胜的人与他同坐宝座。

## 默想

**默想：** 「你说：我是富足，已经发了财，一样都不缺。」（3:17）这几年我们家过得还算不错。在城里买了房，贷款也快还完了；孩子进了好学校，父母身体还硬朗，工作也稳定。教会这几年也兴旺，刚盖了新堂，奉献够用，聚会人越来越多。我照常聚会、照常奉献，可是我已经很久没有迫切地祷告了。不是我不信祷告，而是好像没有什么特别需要求的。读到老底嘉这一段，我第一个反应是：这是说那些有钱人，不是说我。

可是「一样都不缺」不只是银行里的数字。我从小听的是「求人不如求己」，大人教我们凡事靠自己，不要麻烦别人，不要欠人情。这份要强帮我考上了大学，在工作上站稳了脚跟。可是不知不觉，我也这样对待神：我知道自己是罪人，可是在生活里，我很少真的觉得需要他。我对圣经很熟，祷告的话也很会说，属灵上好像也「一样都不缺」。

主对老底嘉说的话很重：「困苦、可怜、贫穷、瞎眼、赤身的。」最可怕的是前面那几个字：「却不知道」。我最大的问题，也许不是缺少什么，而是看不见自己缺少什么。

主没有说：你们既然这么有本事，就自己去补足吧。他说：「我劝你向我买。」这是一个奇怪的邀请。老底嘉人有的是钱，可是主要卖给他们的，是钱买不到的东西。以赛亚早就说过：「没有银钱的也可以来……不用银钱，不用价值。」（赛55:1）向主买，就是承认我付不起，承认我那一切自足的骄傲，在他面前是赤身露体的。保罗说：「他本来富足，却为你们成了贫穷，叫你们因他的贫穷，可以成为富足。」（林后8:9）那真正一样都不缺的一位，为了我成了贫穷；我买他的金子，付款的是他。

也许有人读到这里，心里是另一种难过：我已经这样温吞地过了好多年。年轻时也曾经火热，后来工作、成家、买房，信仰就成了生活的一部分，不再是中心。主会不会已经把我吐出去了？请看他接着说的话：「凡我所疼爱的，我就责备管教他。」（3:19）对一个一句称赞也没有得到的教会，他说的是「我所疼爱的」。他今天让我读到这段经文，让我心里不安，这本身就是他的爱，不是他的厌弃。他没有等我先热起来才爱我；他是因为爱我，才叫我醒过来。

我想，我可以从一件很小的事开始：今天在祷告里，不急着感谢，也不急着为别人代求，先诚实地对主说，我需要你，我不知道自己有多贫穷，求你给我眼药，让我看见。这样的祷告，不需要先有什么属灵的成绩。

主劝老底嘉人买的第二样东西，是「白衣穿上，叫你赤身的羞耻不露出来」。那城里的人以乌黑发亮的羊毛自豪，主却说，他们在神面前是赤身的。这白衣不是我可以织出来的。启示录说，那些从大患难中出来的人，「曾用羔羊的血把衣裳洗白净了」（7:14）。为了给我穿上这件白衣，羔羊自己被剥去衣服，赤身挂在十字架上，担当了我的羞耻。有一天，我也要穿着这件衣服站在他面前，不是因为我曾经富足，也不是因为我终于够热心，而是因为他说：「我是已经爱你了。」（3:9）这件白衣，就是被他爱着的人所穿的衣服。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 3 holds the last three of the seven letters, which follow the same pattern as chapter 2 (see the shape of chapter 2): Christ takes a title from the vision of chapter 1, says "I know," then praises or rebukes, calls, and gives a promise to the one who conquers.

**The three letters in this chapter:**
- **Sardis** (vv. 1–6): "You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead." There is almost no praise, only "a few names" who have not soiled their garments.
- **Philadelphia** (vv. 7–13): "You have but little power," yet it has kept the Lord's word. Like Smyrna, it receives praise and no rebuke.
- **Laodicea** (vv. 14–22): "I am rich … and I need nothing," yet the Lord has not one word of praise, only counsel and an earnest invitation.

**The seven letters together:** many scholars notice a symmetrical arrangement:
- the first and the seventh (Ephesus, Laodicea) are in grave danger: one may have its lampstand removed, the other may be spit out;
- the second and the sixth (Smyrna, Philadelphia) are small, weak, persecuted churches with no rebuke, and both letters mention a "synagogue of Satan" and a "crown";
- the middle three (Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis) are mixed, facing compromise or sleep from within.

One more detail: in the first three letters "He who has an ear, let him hear" comes before the promise to the one who conquers; in the last four the order is reversed, promise first and then the call to hear. The promises of the seven letters are nearly all fulfilled in the new creation at the end of the book: white garments (19:8), the book of life (20:12, 15; 21:27), God's name on their foreheads (22:4), the new Jerusalem (21:2), sitting with Christ on his throne (20:4; 22:3). So the seven letters are the doorway to the whole book: all the visions that follow are given so that these real churches will hear the Lord and conquer.

## Going Deeper: 3:1–6 Sardis: A Name for Being Alive

**1. The city.** Sardis had been the capital of the kingdom of Lydia, famous for wealth; the legendary rich king Croesus ruled there. Its citadel stood on a steep hill, with cliffs on three sides, and was thought impregnable. Yet it fell twice through carelessness. Around 546 BC a soldier of the Persian king Cyrus saw a Lydian climb down a cliff path to fetch a fallen helmet, and the Persians climbed up the same way (Herodotus, *Histories* 1.84). Around 214 BC the troops of Antiochus III scaled an unguarded cliff by night (Polybius, *Histories* 7.15–18). In AD 17 Sardis suffered worst in a great earthquake, and the emperor Tiberius gave money to rebuild it (Tacitus, *Annals* 2.47). In John's day Sardis was still prosperous, but it was often thought of as living on its past.

**2. "Him who has the seven spirits of God and the seven stars."** To a church that is spiritually dead, Christ presents himself as the one who has "the seven spirits of God" (cf. 1:4; 4:5; 5:6; on whether these are the Holy Spirit or seven angels, see chapter 1). Many scholars take them as the fullness of the Holy Spirit. That is just what Sardis needs: only the life-giving Spirit can raise the dead. He also has "the seven stars": the churches are still in his hand.

**3. "You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead."** "I know your works. You have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead" (3:1). The church in Sardis had a name: perhaps its meetings went on and it was busy, and other churches thought it was thriving. Strikingly, the Lord mentions no persecution and no false teachers. A church untroubled by persecution or heresy may fall asleep precisely because it is at peace with the world around it.
- "I have not found your works complete" (3:2): their works were not all wrong, but unfinished, begun and never carried through.
- The word "name" runs through this letter: a name for being alive (v. 1), "a few names" who have not soiled their garments (v. 4), the Lord who will not blot out "his name" and will confess "his name" (v. 5). The name people give and the name the Lord records may be quite different.

**4. "Wake up."** "Wake up, and strengthen what remains and is about to die … Remember, then, what you received and heard. Keep it, and repent. If you will not wake up, I will come like a thief" (3:2–3). For a city that had twice fallen because no one was on watch, "wake up" and "like a thief" must have stung.
- "Like a thief": Jesus and the apostles use this image of the Lord's return (1 Thess 5:2; Rev 16:15). Some take it here as the final coming; others, because it says "if you will not wake up," take it as the Lord's judgment on this church within history. Either way, the Lord's coming is hope to those who are awake and sudden to those who sleep.
- The remedy is again a return to the beginning: "remember … what you received and heard," the gospel they first received.

**5. "They will walk with me in white."** "Yet you have still a few names in Sardis, people who have not soiled their garments, and they will walk with me in white, for they are worthy" (3:4).
- "Soiled their garments" probably means compromise with the surrounding sin and idolatry.
- "Worthy" does not mean they earned their white clothes. In Revelation, robes are made white "in the blood of the Lamb" (7:14), and the bride's fine linen is something "granted her" (19:8). They are worthy because they have kept wearing the clothes the Lord gave and have not soiled them.
- In a dead church, the Lord still knows "a few names." No faithful person escapes his notice.

**6. The book of life.** "The one who conquers will be clothed thus in white garments, and I will never blot his name out of the book of life. I will confess his name before my Father and before his angels" (3:5). Ancient cities kept registers of citizens, and a name could be struck off at death or for a serious crime; some scholars think readers in Sardis would think of this. The Old Testament speaks of "your book that you have written" (Exod 32:32) and of deliverance for "everyone whose name shall be found written in the book" (Dan 12:1). "Never" is a strong double negative in the Greek. Believers read the promise in different ways:
- **The Reformed** reading (e.g. the *Westminster Confession* 17 on the perseverance of the saints, and today Beale) takes it as an emphatic promise: no one whose name is truly written in the book of life will ever be blotted out; 13:8 says these names were written "before the foundation of the world."
- **The Arminian and Wesleyan** reading (e.g. John Wesley's 1751 *Serious Thoughts upon the Perseverance of the Saints*) holds that "I will never blot his name out" implies that a name could be blotted out if one does not conquer, so the verse is both comfort and warning.

Both agree that the verse is written to comfort those who conquer, not to make believers live in fear, and that the way to conquer is to keep the gospel received. And the Lord will "confess his name" before the Father, as he said: "everyone who acknowledges me before men, I also will acknowledge before my Father who is in heaven" (Matt 10:32).

> The point: A church may have a name for being alive and yet be asleep. The Lord calls us to wake up and return to the gospel we received. He knows each one who wears the white garments he gives, and he will confess their names before his Father.

## Going Deeper: 3:7–13 Philadelphia: An Open Door

**1. The city.** Philadelphia was the youngest of the seven cities, founded in the second century BC by the Attalid kings of Pergamum; its name means "brotherly love," said to honor Attalus II's loyalty to his brother. It stood on the main road into inland Phrygia, and William Ramsay called it a gateway for carrying Greek culture inland. After the great earthquake of AD 17 the aftershocks went on for a long time; according to Strabo, the walls cracked daily and many residents preferred to live outside the city in the fields (Strabo, *Geography* 12.8.18; 13.4.10). Later, in thanks for imperial help, the city was renamed for a time Neocaesarea. This background helps in reading the letter, though we need not match every detail to the text.

**2. "Who has the key of David."** "The words of the holy one, the true one, who has the key of David, who opens and no one will shut, who shuts and no one opens" (3:7). The words come from Isaiah. God installed Eliakim as steward of the palace: "I will place on his shoulder the key of the house of David. He shall open, and none shall shut; and he shall shut, and none shall open" (Isa 22:22). The steward decided who could enter the king's house. Now Christ holds this key, and he decides who enters God's kingdom. The believers in Philadelphia may have been put out of the local synagogue (3:9) and declared not to belong to God's people; the Lord says that he is the one who opens and shuts.

**3. What is the "open door"?** "Behold, I have set before you an open door, which no one is able to shut" (3:8). There are two main readings:
- **A door into God's kingdom:** this fits the context best. Others shut the door; the Lord has opened one no one can shut, the door into God's presence and the new Jerusalem.
- **A door for the gospel:** Paul speaks of "a wide door for effective work" (1 Cor 16:9). Philadelphia stood at a gateway to the interior, and the Lord may also have given them opportunity to preach.

The two can stand together: those the Lord brings in, he also sends out.

**4. "You have but little power."** "I know your works … I know that you have but little power, and yet you have kept my word and have not denied my name" (3:8). This was a small church without influence. The Lord does not rebuke them for being weak; he praises them for holding on while weak. In his eyes, a church's size, influence and budget are not the measure of faithfulness.

**5. "They will learn that I have loved you."** "Behold, I will make those of the synagogue of Satan … come and bow down before your feet and they will learn that I have loved you" (3:9). How carefully to read "synagogue of Satan" was set out in the letter to Smyrna in chapter 2 and is not repeated here. What stands out here is a startling reversal. Isaiah promised that the nations who afflicted Israel would "bow down at your feet" (Isa 60:14), and God said to Israel, "you are precious in my eyes, and honored, and I love you" (Isa 43:4). Now those words are applied to a small, largely Gentile church shut out by the synagogue. "Bow down" does not mean worshiping the believers but acknowledging that God is with them. Many scholars think "they will learn" also carries hope: the opponents will one day know that these people are loved by God.

**6. "I will keep you from the hour of trial."** "Because you have kept my word about patient endurance, I will keep you from the hour of trial that is coming on the whole world, to try those who dwell on the earth" (3:10). This verse is often discussed in debates about the end times:
- **The dispensational pre-tribulation** reading (e.g. John Walvoord) takes "keep you from" to mean that the church will be raptured out of the world before the great tribulation; it often sees 4:1, "Come up here," as a picture of the rapture.
- **The historic premillennial and many amillennial** readings (e.g. George Eldon Ladd, Beale, Osborne) take it as protection within the trial, not removal from it. They point out that Jesus uses the same verb and preposition in John 17:15: "I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one."

Both agree that the Lord will not let his own be swallowed by that trial. Whether by being taken or by being kept through suffering, it is the Lord himself who keeps them.

**7. A pillar in God's temple and a new name.** "The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God. Never shall he go out of it, and I will write on him the name of my God, and the name of the city of my God … and my own new name" (3:12).
- For a city whose people often fled outside after earthquakes, "never shall he go out of it" must have been a deep comfort; those put out of the synagogue have a permanent place in God's temple.
- The new Jerusalem has no temple, "for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" (21:22). So being a "pillar" does not mean becoming part of a building; it means living forever in God's presence.
- A city might change its name to please an emperor; the Lord writes his own name on believers: they belong to him.

> The point: A church with little power need not envy larger ones. The Lord who holds the key of David has opened a door no one can shut for them, wants them to know "I have loved you," and promises them a home forever in God's presence.

## Going Deeper: 3:14–18 Laodicea: Neither Cold nor Hot

**1. The city.** Laodicea lay in the Lycus valley, near Colossae and Hierapolis (Col 4:13). Paul's co-worker Epaphras had labored for all three churches, and Paul mentions "the letter from Laodicea" (Col 4:16). The city stood at a junction of trade routes and was a wealthy center of commerce and finance:
- **Banking:** the Roman statesman Cicero mentions that he could cash bills of exchange at Laodicea (Cicero, *Letters to Friends* 3.5.4).
- **Wool:** Strabo records that the region produced soft, glossy black wool, famous far and wide (*Geography* 12.8.16).
- **Medicine:** a well-known school of medicine stood near the city (*Geography* 12.8.20), and ancient writers mention a Phrygian eye remedy; scholars differ on whether it was made in Laodicea itself.
- **Self-sufficiency:** after an earthquake around AD 60, Tacitus notes that Laodicea refused help from Rome and recovered by its own resources (Tacitus, *Annals* 14.27).

**2. "The Amen."** "The words of the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's creation" (3:14).
- "Amen" means *yes, truly*. Isaiah 65:16 twice speaks of "the God of truth," literally in Hebrew *the God of Amen*. Christ himself is the "Amen" to all God's promises.
- "The faithful and true witness" (cf. 1:5): the Laodiceans did not want the truth, but the Lord is the witness who tells it.
- "The beginning of God's creation": the CUV renders it "ruler" (元首). The word can mean beginning, source or ruler. Today's Jehovah's Witnesses use it to say that Christ was the first created being, but that does not fit Revelation's usage: God is called "the beginning and the end" (21:6), and Christ calls himself the same (22:13). The verse is close to Colossians 1:15–18: Christ is the source and ruler of all creation, not one of its creatures.

**3. Neither cold nor hot.** "I know your works: you are neither cold nor hot. Would that you were either cold or hot! So, because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I will spit you out of my mouth" (3:15–16). There are two main readings:
- **The traditional reading:** "hot" is zealous love for Christ, "cold" is open hostility, "lukewarm" is half-heartedness. But then why would the Lord wish they were cold?
- **A reading from the local water supply:** Laodicea lacked good water of its own and piped it through stone conduits from springs several kilometers south. It arrived tepid and heavy with minerals; the ancient pipes still carry thick deposits. Hierapolis to the north had hot springs used for healing, and Colossae to the east had cold, fresh water. On this reading (proposed by Rudwick and Green in 1958 and taken up by many scholars), hot and cold water are both useful; lukewarm water makes you want to spit it out. The problem is not the temperature of their zeal but their uselessness to the Lord.

Some scholars (e.g. Craig Koester) caution against pressing the details of the pipes, noting that drinks at banquets were served either hot or cold and that a lukewarm one was nauseating, an image any ancient reader would grasp. Whatever the background, the point is the same: the church in Laodicea had lost its taste and its usefulness. *Spit out* is a strong word, showing the Lord's disgust at this condition.

**4. "I need nothing."** "For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing, not realizing that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked" (3:17).
- The words echo the prophet Hosea's rebuke of Ephraim: "Ah, but I am rich; I have found wealth for myself" (Hos 12:8).
- The city's pride in rebuilding without help may have entered the church. Their wealth may have been material, or spiritual: thinking they understood everything and had everything.
- The most frightening words are "not realizing." Sardis at least had a reputation for life; Laodicea could not see its own condition at all.

**5. "Buy from me."** "I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, so that you may be rich, and white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen, and salve to anoint your eyes, so that you may see" (3:18). The Lord's prescription targets the three things the city was proud of:
- the city had banks, but the Lord tells them to buy "gold refined by fire," a faith proved genuine in testing (cf. 1 Pet 1:7);
- the city had famous black wool, but the Lord tells them to buy "white garments" to cover the shame of their nakedness;
- the city had an eye remedy, but the Lord says they are blind and must buy his salve.

But with what can a "poor" person buy? Here we remember Isaiah's invitation: "he who has no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without price" (Isa 55:1). To "buy" from Christ is to admit that we can pay for nothing and to come with empty hands to receive. Only those who know they are poor can afford it.

> The point: To think we need nothing is the deepest poverty, because it blinds us to our need of Christ. The Lord does not tell us to make up the lack ourselves; he invites us to come to him empty-handed and receive his gold, his white garments and his salve.

## Going Deeper: 3:19–22 Those Whom I Love

**1. "Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline."** "Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline, so be zealous and repent" (3:19). After the sternest words to Laodicea, the Lord tells why he spoke them. The line comes from Proverbs: "for the LORD reproves him whom he loves, as a father the son in whom he delights" (Prov 3:12; cf. Heb 12:6).
- To a church that received not one word of praise, the Lord says "those whom I love." The Greek word here expresses warm affection. His rebuke is not rejection; it is a father's love.
- "Be zealous" sounds like the Greek word for "hot" earlier, and many see a deliberate echo: the Lord wants them to turn from lukewarm to hot. It is a present imperative, suggesting ongoing zeal, while "repent" is a decisive turn.

**2. "I stand at the door and knock."** "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me" (3:20). This is one of the most quoted verses in the Bible.
- It is often used to invite unbelievers to Christ, and that is not wrong, for the Lord does invite everyone. But in context the words are first spoken to a church: the Lord stands outside his own church. A church can have meetings and money while the Lord stands outside.
- "If anyone": the Lord addresses the whole church but invites individuals. Even if the whole church is cold, any one person can open.
- "Eat with him": in the ancient world, sharing a meal meant close fellowship. It also points to "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (19:9), and recalls the beloved knocking in the Song of Songs (Song 5:2). Jesus himself said that when the master returns and finds his servants awake, "he will dress himself for service and have them recline at table, and he will come and serve them" (Luke 12:37).
- The English painter Holman Hunt's well-known *The Light of the World* shows a door with no handle on the outside, meaning it can only be opened from within. The painting is often used to illustrate this verse: the Lord does not break the door down; he knocks and waits.

**3. Sitting with me on my throne.** "The one who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my throne, as I also conquered and sat down with my Father on his throne" (3:21). This is the greatest promise of the seven letters, and it is given to unpromising Laodicea. Christ himself "conquered" before he sat down on the throne; his victory was the cross and the resurrection. In chapter 5 John will see that the one who "has conquered" is "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (5:5–6). The believer's conquering is the same: following the Lamb's way.

**4. The doorway to the throne.** The seven letters end with a throne, and chapter 4 begins, "a throne stood in heaven" (4:2). Seven struggling churches on earth: some persecuted, some compromising, some asleep, some thinking themselves rich. Yet above them stands a throne. The Lord who walks among the lampstands is the Lord who sits with his Father on the throne.

> The point: The Lord's sternest rebuke comes from his love. He stands at the door of a lukewarm church and knocks, inviting anyone to share his table, and he will seat those who conquer with him on his throne.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "For you say, I am rich, I have prospered, and I need nothing" (3:17). These last few years our family has done quite well. We bought a flat in the city, and the mortgage is nearly paid off; the children got into good schools; my parents are still in good health, and my job is steady. Our church has grown too: a new building, enough in the offering, more people every Sunday. I still go to church and still give, but I have not prayed urgently for a long time. It is not that I don't believe in prayer; there just seems to be nothing in particular to ask for. When I read this letter to Laodicea, my first thought was: this is about rich people, not me.

But "I need nothing" is not only a figure in the bank. I grew up on the saying that asking others is worse than relying on yourself. We were taught to depend on ourselves, not to trouble anyone, not to owe favors. That drive got me into university and helped me stand on my own feet at work. But without noticing, I have treated God the same way. I know I am a sinner, yet in daily life I rarely feel that I need him. I know the Bible well, I can pray fluent prayers, and spiritually, too, I seem to "need nothing."

The Lord's words to Laodicea are heavy: "wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked." Most frightening are the words before them: "not realizing." My deepest problem may not be what I lack, but that I cannot see what I lack.

The Lord does not say, "Since you are so capable, make up the lack yourselves." He says, "I counsel you to buy from me." It is a strange invitation. The Laodiceans had plenty of money, but what the Lord offers them cannot be bought with it. Isaiah had said long before: "he who has no money, come … without money and without price" (Isa 55:1). To buy from him is to admit that I cannot pay, that all my proud self-sufficiency stands naked before him. Paul writes: "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9). The one who truly needed nothing became poor for me; when I buy his gold, he is the one who pays.

Some may read this with another sorrow: I have lived lukewarm like this for many years. When I was young I burned for him, but then came work, marriage, a flat, and faith became one part of life instead of its center. Has the Lord already spit me out? Look at what he says next: "Those whom I love, I reprove and discipline" (3:19). To a church that received not one word of praise, he says "those whom I love." That he lets me read this passage today, and lets it unsettle me, is itself his love, not his rejection. He did not wait for me to warm up before loving me; it is because he loves me that he wakes me.

I think I can begin with something very small. Today, when I pray, I will not hurry to give thanks or to pray for others. First I will tell the Lord honestly: I need you; I do not know how poor I am; give me your salve so that I can see. A prayer like that needs no spiritual record to stand on.

The second thing the Lord counsels Laodicea to buy is "white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen." The city was proud of its glossy black wool, but the Lord says that before God they are naked. These white garments are not something I can weave. Revelation says that those who come out of the great tribulation "have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb" (7:14). To clothe me in white, the Lamb himself was stripped and hung naked on the cross, bearing my shame. One day I too will stand before him wearing that robe, not because I was once rich, nor because I finally became zealous enough, but because he has said, "I have loved you" (3:9). That white robe is what the beloved wear.
