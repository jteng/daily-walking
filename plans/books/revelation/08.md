## 本章脉络

第7章是一段插曲：神的众仆人额上受了印，从各国来的许多人站在宝座和羔羊面前。现在羔羊揭开了最后一印，读者以为结局马上就要来到，天上却忽然安静下来。第8章可以分成四段：
- **8:1，第七印和寂静：** 「羔羊揭开第七印的时候，天上寂静约有二刻。」
- **8:2-6，七枝号和金香炉：** 七位天使领受了七枝号；另一位天使在金坛前把众圣徒的祈祷和香一同献上，又把坛上的火倒在地上。
- **8:7-12，前四枝号：** 雹子与火、火烧的大山、名叫「茵陈」的大星、日月星辰的黑暗，各击打受造之物的三分之一。
- **8:13，鹰的警告：** 一只鹰飞在空中，宣告后面还有三样「祸哉」。

这一章最奇特的地方，是号声响起以前，先有寂静和祷告。七枝号已经交在天使手中，可是要等到众圣徒的祈祷升到神面前，号才吹响。神在历史中施行审判，和他百姓的祷告连在一起。

## 深读 8:1-2 天上寂静约有二刻

**1. 寂静是什么意思？** 第四、五章的天上满了声音：活物昼夜不住地说「圣哉」（4:8），千千万万的天使大声唱歌（5:11-12）。现在天上却完全安静下来。释经者主要有三种理解：
- **在审判以前肃敬静默：** 旧约的先知多次这样说：「你要在主耶和华面前静默无声，因为耶和华的日子快到。」（番1:7）「全地的人都当在他面前肃敬静默。」（哈2:20）「凡有血气的都当在耶和华面前静默无声；因为他兴起，从圣所出来了。」（亚2:13）许多释经者（例如格兰特·奥斯本）认为，天上的寂静是神起来审判以前那叫人屏息的一刻。
- **为了听见圣徒的祷告：** 包衡指出，有些后来的犹太传统说，天使在天上的颂赞会暂停，好让神听见他百姓在地上的祷告。紧接着的8:3-4正是众圣徒的祈祷升到神面前，这种理解和上下文很相合。
- **和末了的审判相连：** 毕尔把这寂静和最后的审判连在一起，也引用犹太著作《以斯拉四书》7.30说，世界在末了要回到起初的寂静。
这几种理解并不互相排斥。二刻就是半个小时，是一段短短的时间，不是要我们推算的数字。

**2. 七号和七印的关系。** 七印、七号、七碗是先后发生还是重复叙述，第6章已经把几种读法摆出来了。这里只补充两点和七号直接有关的。第一，第七印揭开以后，除了寂静，并没有自己的灾难，接着就是七位天使领受七枝号（8:1-2）。对这一点，两派的解释不同：主张先后发生的学者（例如约翰·华尔伏）说，七号就是第七印的内容；主张重复叙述的学者（例如毕尔）则认为，寂静本身就是第一组「七」的终局，七号再从头讲起。第二，两种读法都看到灾难越来越重：印的灾难临到「四分之一」（6:8），号的灾难临到「三分之一」，碗的灾难却没有限度。

**3. 「站在神面前的七位天使」。** 「我看见那站在神面前的七位天使，有七枝号赐给他们。」（8:2）「站在神面前」的意思是随时侍立、听候差遣。天使加百列对撒迦利亚说：「我是站在神面前的加百列」（路1:19）。号是「赐给他们」的：吹号的权柄不是天使自己的，是从神来的。

**4. 号在旧约中的意思。** 读者听到号声，会想起旧约中好几个场景：
- **耶利哥：** 七个祭司拿着七个羊角，第七日绕城七次，「祭司也吹角」，城墙就塌陷了（书6:4、20）。神的百姓进入应许之地，第一座城不是靠兵器攻下的，而是在号声中倒塌的。
- **打仗和纪念：** 以色列人打仗时「要用号吹出大声，便在耶和华－你们的神面前得蒙纪念」（民10:9）。
- **耶和华的日子：** 「你们要在锡安吹角……因为耶和华的日子将到」（珥2:1）；那日是「吹角呐喊的日子」（番1:16）。
- **守望的人：** 守望的人看见刀剑临到，就吹角警戒众民；「他若受警戒，便是救了自己的性命」（结33:3-5）。
所以号声是审判的宣告，在旧约中也常常是警告。释经者对七号的目的看法不同：许多人（例如包衡、格兰特·奥斯本）认为，前六枝号的灾难有限度，是为了叫人在结局以前听见、回转；毕尔则强调，正像埃及的十灾使法老越发刚硬，这些审判主要是显明人心的刚硬，也使这刚硬成为定局。第9章末了「仍旧不悔改」（9:20-21）这句话，两种读法都要认真对待。

> 要点：号声响起以前，天上先安静下来。神的审判不仓促，也不任意，乃是在宝座前预备好的，并且带着警告临到世界。

## 深读 8:3-6 众圣徒的祈祷

**1. 金香炉和金坛。** 「另有一位天使，拿着金香炉来，站在祭坛旁边。有许多香赐给他，要和众圣徒的祈祷一同献在宝座前的金坛上。」（8:3）这里的背景是会幕和圣殿的香坛。神吩咐摩西「用皂荚木做一座烧香的坛」，亚伦每天早晚要在坛上烧香，「作为世世代代常烧的香」（出30:1、7-8）。这座坛摆在幔子前，最靠近施恩座。约翰看见的是天上的殿，地上的会幕只是它的影像（来8:5）。许多释经者（例如毕尔）认为，6:9殉道者灵魂所在的「祭坛」，就是这里的金坛。

**2. 这位天使是谁？** 有些较早的释经者（例如马太·亨利）认为，这位拿着香炉的「另有一位天使」就是基督，我们的大祭司。现代多数释经者（例如毕尔、格兰特·奥斯本）认为，他是一位天使：启示录从来没有明明地称基督为天使，基督在这卷书中是坐在宝座中间的羔羊（5:6；7:17），这位天使只是在坛前供职。而且，香是「赐给他」的；使祷告蒙悦纳的，不是天使自己的功劳。新约清楚地说，「在神和人中间，只有一位中保，乃是降世为人的基督耶稣」（提前2:5）。这一节经文没有教导我们向天使祷告，也没有说天使替我们代求；它让我们看见，地上圣徒的祷告在天上有一个位置。

**3. 「众圣徒的祈祷」。** 「那香的烟和众圣徒的祈祷从天使的手中一同升到神面前。」（8:4）
- **祷告和香：** 诗人说：「愿我的祷告如香陈列在你面前！」（诗141:2）施洗约翰的父亲撒迦利亚在圣殿烧香的时候，「众百姓在外面祷告」（路1:10）；殿里的香和殿外百姓的祷告，是在同一个时辰一同献上的。
- **「许多香」：** 香是从神那一边加上去的。圣徒的祷告本身也许软弱、零碎，可是它不是单独升上去的。
- **「众」圣徒：** 不是少数会祷告的人，而是所有的圣徒。第5章长老手中的金炉「盛满了香」（5:8）；殉道者在坛下呼求「要等到几时呢」（6:10）。现在，这些多年积存的祷告一同升到神面前。

**4. 坛上的火倒在地上。** 「天使拿着香炉，盛满了坛上的火，倒在地上；随有雷轰、大声、闪电、地震。」（8:5）
- **以西结的异象：** 主吩咐那穿细麻衣的人，从基路伯中间「将火炭取满两手，撒在城上」（结10:2）。火从神的面前出来，成了审判。
- **祷告和审判：** 同一个香炉，先把祷告献上去，又把火倒下来。号声随即响起（8:6）。许多释经者认为，这就是神对圣徒祷告的回答：他们求神伸冤，求神的国降临（太6:10），神就起来审判世界。到了第六号，那吩咐释放四个使者的声音，正是「从神面前金坛的四角出来」的（9:13）。圣徒这样求，不是自己报复，而是把伸冤交给神（罗12:19）。
- **越来越大的雷声：** 包衡指出，「闪电、声音、雷轰」在启示录中出现了四次（4:5；8:5；11:19；16:18），每一次都比前一次多出一些：这里加上地震，第七号又加上大雹，第七碗的地震更是空前的大，又有「大雹子从天落在人身上」（16:21）。每一组「七」都在宝座前的雷声中结束。

> 要点：圣徒的祷告不会落空。它们和天上的香一同升到神面前，神也藉着这些祷告，成就他在历史中的作为。

## 深读 8:7-12 前四枝号：三分之一

**1. 出埃及的回声。** 前四枝号击打的是地、海、江河和天体，几乎每一样都叫人想起出埃及时的十灾：
- **第一号，雹子与火（8:7）：** 第七灾时，「耶和华就打雷下雹，有火闪到地上」，「雹与火搀杂，甚是厉害」（出9:23-24）。
- **第二号，海变成血（8:8-9）：** 第一灾时，亚伦「击打河里的水，河里的水都变作血了。河里的鱼死了」（出7:20-21）。
- **第三号，水变苦（8:10-11）：** 这大星「名叫『茵陈』」，就是苦艾。神曾说：「我必将茵陈给这百姓吃，又将苦胆水给他们喝。」（耶9:15）出埃及以后，以色列人在玛拉遇见苦水，神却使苦水变甜（出15:23-25）；这里却是甜水变苦。
- **第四号，黑暗（8:12）：** 第九灾时，「埃及遍地就乌黑了三天」（出10:22）。
火烧的大山也叫人想起神对巴比伦说的「使你成为烧毁的山」（耶51:25）。神从前怎样审判压迫他百姓的埃及，又领他的百姓出来，他也要照样审判这个世界，并领他的百姓经历一次新的出埃及。

**2. 为什么是三分之一？** 「三分之一」在这几节的原文中出现了十二次。它表示审判是真实的，却还有限度。印的灾难临到四分之一（6:8），号的灾难临到三分之一，到了七碗，就不再有限度了。神在审判中仍然有所保留；许多释经者认为，这是要给人回转的余地。第9章末了却说，人仍旧不悔改（9:20-21）。

**3. 四种读法怎样看前四枝号？**
- **未来派：** 约翰·华尔伏等人认为，这些是将来大灾难中真实发生在自然界的灾害，按字面应验，只是约翰用他那个时代的语言来描写。
- **历史派：** 许多较早的历史派释经者（例如十九世纪的艾略特和阿尔伯特·巴恩斯）把前四枝号读作五世纪蛮族入侵西罗马帝国：哥特人、汪达尔人从海上进攻、匈奴王阿提拉，最后西罗马帝国灭亡，好像帝国的「日头」黑暗了。
- **过去派：** 肯尼斯·詹特里等人把这些灾和主后66-70年犹太战争中加利利、犹太地遭受的毁坏连在一起。
- **理想派：** 威廉·亨德里克森和毕尔等人认为，前四枝号描写从基督升天到他再来之间，神一再藉着自然界的灾难（饥荒、水灾、瘟疫）警告世人：受造之物不是人可以随意利用的，它们都听从创造主的命令。
本研读和导读一样，采取综合的读法。一世纪的读者在自己的世界里，已经认得出这类有限度的审判，例如饥荒、地震、战争。历世历代的教会也一再看见这样的事。这些灾难又指向基督再来以前那真实、更完全的结局。

**4. 不要拿新闻来对号入座。** 1986年切尔诺贝利核电站出事以后，有人说切尔诺贝利在乌克兰语中就是「茵陈」，所以第三号已经应验了。其实那个词是一种蒿草的名字，和茵陈同属一类植物，却不是同一种；而且这样的说法很容易把读经变成猜谜。约翰要我们看见的，不是某一则新闻，而是神对拜偶像的世界的审判，以及世界因此变苦的光景。

> 要点：前四枝号用出埃及的灾难告诉我们：神是受造之物的主，他审判压迫他百姓的世界；可是在最后的审判以前，他的审判仍有限度，人还有回转的时间。

## 深读 8:13 三声「祸哉」

**1. 飞在空中的鹰。** 「我又看见一个鹰飞在空中，并听见它大声说：『三位天使要吹那其余的号。你们住在地上的民，祸哉！祸哉！祸哉！』」（8:13）英文钦定本这里译作天使，是根据较晚的抄本；较早的抄本多作鹰。鹰在空中最高的地方飞，人人都能看见、听见。

**2. 「住在地上的民」。** 在启示录中，这个说法常常不是单指住在哪里，而是指那些把这世界当作唯一家乡、与神为敌的人（6:10；13:8）。神的百姓也住在地上，可是他们额上受了神的印（7:3），他们的名字记在羔羊的生命册上（13:8）。第五号的蝗虫只伤害「额上没有神印记的人」（9:4）。

**3. 三样灾祸。** 鹰的三声「祸哉」连着后面三枝号：第五号是第一样灾祸（9:12），第六号是第二样（11:14）；第三样灾祸「快到了」（11:14），多数释经者认为就是第七号和它所带出的审判（参12:12）。前四枝号击打的主要是受造之物，后三枝号直接临到人身上。灾难越来越重，可是警告的声音也越来越大。

**4. 「祸哉」也是哀叹。** 主耶稣曾一连对文士和法利赛人说「有祸了」，最后却说：「我多次愿意聚集你的儿女，好像母鸡把小鸡聚集在翅膀底下，只是你们不愿意。」（太23:37）鹰的三声「祸哉」是严厉的，但是在第七号吹响以前，它仍然是一个警告，警告就表示还有时间。

> 要点：神在最重的审判以前大声警告。听见「祸哉」的人，还来得及回转。

## 默想

**默想：** 「另有一位天使，拿着金香炉来，站在祭坛旁边。有许多香赐给他，要和众圣徒的祈祷一同献在宝座前的金坛上。那香的烟和众圣徒的祈祷从天使的手中一同升到神面前。」（8:3-4）每周三晚上，教会的祷告会上总有几位弟兄姊妹祷告得很流利，一开口就是一长段，用词也很属灵。坐在角落里的李阿姨从来没有出声祷告过。她小学没有读完，在工厂干了一辈子，一想到要在大家面前开口，心就怦怦跳。轮到她的时候，她总是低着头，等别人接下去。她心里祷告的，都是些小事：儿子的工作，老母亲的膝盖，楼下那个刚死了丈夫的邻居。她常常想：神大概更愿意听那些会说话的人祷告；我这几句，算不了什么。

启示录第8章让她看见另一幅画面。天上千千万万的声音停了下来，「天上寂静约有二刻」（8:1）；在这寂静中，升到神面前的，是「众圣徒的祈祷」（8:3）。不是少数会祷告的人的祷告，而是所有圣徒的祷告，包括那些在角落里、只在心里说出来的话。撒迦利亚在殿里烧香的时候，「众百姓在外面祷告」（路1:10），他们的名字没有记下来，可是他们的祷告和香是在同一个时辰献上的。接下来，天使用同一个香炉盛满坛上的火，倒在地上，号声就响起来了（8:5-6）。神在历史中的作为，和这些祷告连在一起。李阿姨在心里为楼下那位邻居说的几句话，不是可有可无的。神愿意让「愿你的国降临」的祷告，也从她那个角落升上去。

那些祷告能升到神面前，也不是因为说得好。经上说「有许多香赐给他」（8:3）：香是从神那一边加上去的。地上的香坛，每年一次要用赎罪祭牲的血「在坛的角上行赎罪之礼」（出30:10）；连烧香的坛也要用血洁净，祷告才能蒙悦纳。我们的祷告也是这样。经文没有说这香是什么，可是整本新约告诉我们，使祷告蒙悦纳的是基督：他「为我们舍了自己，当作馨香的供物和祭物，献与神」（弗5:2）。所以我们「藉着耶稣基督奉献神所悦纳的灵祭」（彼前2:5）。那位弟兄流利的祷告和李阿姨结结巴巴的一句「主啊，求你看顾我妈的腿」，都是靠着同一位基督升到神面前的；没有哪一句是靠自己的口才。

也许有人已经很久不祷告了，觉得祷告没有用；也许有人心里瞧不起那些长长的祷告，觉得都是在表演；也有人祷告得很流利，却暗暗看不起不会开口的人。不祷告的灰心，看不起别人的冷眼，暗暗自以为会祷告的骄傲，都可以原样带到主面前。他的血不但洁净了坛，也洁净了献祷告的人（参来9:23-24）。李阿姨下一次也许仍然不出声，也许会开口说一句；无论怎样，她的祷告都不会落在地上。在宝座前的金坛上，众圣徒的祈祷正和香一同上升。那香不是我们带去的，是神赐下的；那馨香的祭，是为我们舍了自己的基督。李阿姨那句结结巴巴的「主啊，求你看顾我妈的腿」，也在这烟里，和众圣徒的祈祷一同升到神面前。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 7 was an interlude: the servants of God were sealed on their foreheads, and a great multitude from every nation stood before the throne and before the Lamb. Now the Lamb opens the last seal. Readers expect the end to arrive at once, but instead heaven suddenly falls silent. Chapter 8 falls into four parts:
- **8:1, the seventh seal and the silence:** "When the Lamb opened the seventh seal, there was silence in heaven for about half an hour."
- **8:2–6, seven trumpets and the golden censer:** seven angels are given seven trumpets; another angel offers the prayers of all the saints with incense on the golden altar, and then throws fire from the altar on the earth.
- **8:7–12, the first four trumpets:** hail and fire, a burning mountain, a great star called Wormwood and darkness in the sun, moon and stars each strike a third of creation.
- **8:13, the eagle's warning:** an eagle flies overhead announcing three more woes.

The most striking thing in this chapter is that before the trumpets sound, there is first silence and prayer. The seven trumpets are already in the angels' hands, but they do not sound until the prayers of the saints have risen before God. God's judgments in history are bound up with the prayers of his people.

## Going Deeper: 8:1–2 Silence in Heaven for About Half an Hour

**1. What does the silence mean?** In chapters 4–5 heaven was full of sound: the living creatures never ceased to say "Holy" day and night (4:8), and myriads of angels sang with a loud voice (5:11–12). Now heaven is completely still. Interpreters have three main readings:
- **Awed silence before judgment:** the prophets say again and again, "Be silent before the Lord GOD! For the day of the LORD is near" (Zeph 1:7); "Let all the earth keep silence before him" (Hab 2:20); "Be silent, all flesh, before the LORD, for he has roused himself from his holy dwelling" (Zech 2:13). Many interpreters (e.g. Grant Osborne) take heaven's silence as the breathless moment before God rises to judge.
- **Silence so that the saints' prayers are heard:** Bauckham points out that some later Jewish traditions say the angels' praise in heaven pauses so that God may hear the prayers of his people on earth. Since 8:3–4 goes straight on to the prayers of all the saints rising before God, this reading fits the context well.
- **Tied to the last judgment:** Beale ties the silence to the last judgment, citing among other texts *4 Ezra* 7.30, where the world returns to its primeval silence.
These readings need not exclude one another. Half an hour is a short time, not a number for calculation.

**2. The trumpets and the seals.** Whether the seals, trumpets and bowls follow one another or retell the same period is set out in chapter 6 (see "How do the seals relate to the trumpets and bowls that follow?"). Two points belong here. First, when the seventh seal is opened, nothing follows but silence, and then the seven angels are given seven trumpets (8:1–2). The two readings take this differently: those who read the series in sequence (e.g. John Walvoord) say the seventh seal contains the trumpets, while those who read them as recapitulation (e.g. Beale) take the silence itself as the close of the first series, with the trumpets beginning the story again. Second, both readings see the judgments growing heavier: the seals strike "a fourth" (6:8), the trumpets "a third," and the bowls have no limit.

**3. "The seven angels who stand before God."** "Then I saw the seven angels who stand before God, and seven trumpets were given to them" (8:2). To "stand before God" is to wait in attendance, ready to be sent. The angel Gabriel said to Zechariah, "I am Gabriel, who stands in the presence of God" (Luke 1:19). The trumpets "were given to them": the authority to sound them is not the angels' own but comes from God.

**4. What trumpets mean in the Old Testament.** The sound of a trumpet would call several Old Testament scenes to mind:
- **Jericho:** seven priests bore seven trumpets of rams' horns, marched around the city seven times on the seventh day, "and the priests shall blow the trumpets," and the wall fell down flat (Josh 6:4, 20). The first city God's people took in the promised land fell not to weapons but at the sound of trumpets.
- **War and remembrance:** when Israel went to war they were to "sound an alarm with the trumpets, that you may be remembered before the LORD your God" (Num 10:9).
- **The day of the LORD:** "Blow a trumpet in Zion... for the day of the LORD is coming" (Joel 2:1); that day is "a day of trumpet blast and battle cry" (Zeph 1:16).
- **The watchman:** when the watchman sees the sword coming, he blows the trumpet and warns the people; "if he had taken warning, he would have saved his life" (Ezek 33:3–5).
So the trumpet announces judgment, and in the Old Testament it is often a warning too. Interpreters differ on the trumpets' purpose: many (e.g. Bauckham, Grant Osborne) take the limited plagues of the first six trumpets as meant to make people hear and turn before the end; Beale stresses that, as the plagues of Egypt hardened Pharaoh, these judgments chiefly expose and confirm hardened hearts. Both readings have to reckon with the closing words of chapter 9, "they did not repent" (9:20–21).

> The point: before the trumpets sound, heaven falls silent. God's judgment is neither hasty nor arbitrary; it is prepared before the throne, and it comes to the world with a warning.

## Going Deeper: 8:3–6 The Prayers of All the Saints

**1. The golden censer and the golden altar.** "And another angel came and stood at the altar with a golden censer, and he was given much incense to offer with the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar before the throne" (8:3). The background is the altar of incense in the tabernacle and temple. God told Moses, "You shall make an altar on which to burn incense," and Aaron was to burn incense on it morning and evening, "a regular incense offering before the LORD throughout your generations" (Exod 30:1, 7–8). This altar stood before the veil, nearest to the mercy seat. What John sees is the temple in heaven, of which the earthly tabernacle was "a copy and shadow" (Heb 8:5). Many interpreters (e.g. Beale) take the "altar" under which the martyrs' souls were seen (6:9) to be this same golden altar.

**2. Who is this angel?** Some older interpreters (e.g. Matthew Henry) took "another angel" with the censer to be Christ, our high priest. Most modern interpreters (e.g. Beale, Grant Osborne) take him to be an angel: Revelation never explicitly calls Christ an angel; in this book Christ is the Lamb in the midst of the throne (5:6; 7:17); and this angel only serves at the altar. Notice that the incense "was given" to him; what makes the prayers acceptable is not the angel's own merit. The New Testament says plainly that "there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim 2:5). This passage does not teach us to pray to angels or say that angels intercede for us; it shows us that the prayers of the saints on earth have a place in heaven.

**3. "The prayers of all the saints."** "And the smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, rose before God from the hand of the angel" (8:4).
- **Prayer and incense:** the psalmist says, "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you" (Ps 141:2). When Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, burned incense in the temple, "the whole multitude of the people were praying outside at the hour of incense" (Luke 1:10); the incense inside and the prayers of the people outside went up at the same hour.
- **"Much incense":** the incense is added from God's side. The saints' prayers may be weak and broken, but they do not rise alone.
- **"All" the saints:** not a few who pray well, but all the saints. In chapter 5 the elders held "golden bowls full of incense" (5:8); the martyrs under the altar cried, "How long?" (6:10). Now these prayers, stored up for years, rise together before God.

**4. Fire from the altar thrown on the earth.** "Then the angel took the censer and filled it with fire from the altar and threw it on the earth, and there were peals of thunder, rumblings, flashes of lightning, and an earthquake" (8:5).
- **Ezekiel's vision:** the Lord told the man clothed in linen to "fill your hands with burning coals from between the cherubim, and scatter them over the city" (Ezek 10:2). Fire comes out from God's presence as judgment.
- **Prayer and judgment:** the same censer that carried the prayers up now pours fire down, and the trumpets begin at once (8:6). Many interpreters take this as God's answer to the saints' prayers: they asked him to vindicate them and for his kingdom to come (Matt 6:10), and God rises to judge the world. At the sixth trumpet, the voice that orders the four angels released comes "from the four horns of the golden altar before God" (9:13). This is not revenge but leaving vengeance to God (Rom 12:19).
- **Growing thunder:** Bauckham points out that "lightning, rumblings and thunder" appear four times in Revelation (4:5; 8:5; 11:19; 16:18), each time with something added: here an earthquake, at the seventh trumpet heavy hail as well, and at the seventh bowl an earthquake greater than any before, with "great hailstones" (16:21). Each series of seven ends in the thunder before the throne.

> The point: the prayers of the saints are not lost. They rise before God with the incense of heaven, and through them God carries out his work in history.

## Going Deeper: 8:7–12 The First Four Trumpets: A Third

**1. Echoes of the exodus.** The first four trumpets strike the earth, the sea, the rivers and the heavenly lights, and nearly every one recalls the plagues of Egypt:
- **First trumpet, hail and fire (8:7):** in the seventh plague, "the LORD sent thunder and hail, and fire ran down to the earth"; "there was hail and fire flashing continually in the midst of the hail" (Exod 9:23–24).
- **Second trumpet, the sea becomes blood (8:8–9):** in the first plague, Aaron "struck the water in the Nile, and all the water in the Nile turned into blood. And the fish in the Nile died" (Exod 7:20–21).
- **Third trumpet, bitter waters (8:10–11):** "The name of the star is Wormwood," a bitter plant. God had said, "I will feed this people with bitter food, and give them poisonous water to drink" (Jer 9:15; the Hebrew for "bitter food" is *la'anah*, "wormwood"). After the exodus Israel found bitter water at Marah, and God made it sweet (Exod 15:23–25); here sweet water turns bitter.
- **Fourth trumpet, darkness (8:12):** in the ninth plague, "there was pitch darkness in all the land of Egypt three days" (Exod 10:22).
The burning mountain also recalls God's word to Babylon, "make you a burnt mountain" (Jer 51:25). As God once judged Egypt, the oppressor of his people, and brought his people out, so he will judge this world and lead his people out in a new exodus.

**2. Why "a third"?** In the Greek, "a third" appears twelve times in these verses. It means the judgment is real but still limited. The seals strike a fourth (6:8), the trumpets a third, and the bowls have no limit at all. In judgment God still holds back; many take this as leaving room to turn. Yet at the end of chapter 9 we read that people still did not repent (9:20–21).

**3. How do the four schools read the first four trumpets?**
- **Futurist:** John Walvoord and others take these as real natural disasters in a future great tribulation, fulfilled literally, though described in the language of John's day.
- **Historicist:** many older historicist interpreters (e.g. E. B. Elliott and Albert Barnes in the nineteenth century) read the first four trumpets as the barbarian invasions of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century: the Goths, the Vandals attacking by sea, Attila the Hun, and finally the fall of the Western Empire, as if the empire's "sun" were darkened.
- **Preterist:** Kenneth Gentry and others connect these plagues with the devastation of Galilee and Judea in the Jewish War of AD 66–70.
- **Idealist:** William Hendriksen, Beale and others take the first four trumpets as describing how, between Christ's ascension and his return, God again and again warns the world through disasters in nature (famine, flood, plague): creation is not ours to use as we please; it obeys its Creator's command.
As in the introduction, this study reads eclectically (see chapter 0): the first readers would already have recognized such limited judgments in their own world (famine, earthquake, war); the church has seen them again and again through the ages; and they point ahead to a real and fuller end before Christ returns.

**4. Beware of matching headlines.** After the Chernobyl nuclear disaster in 1986, some said that Chernobyl means "wormwood" in Ukrainian, so the third trumpet had been fulfilled. In fact the word names a kind of mugwort, a plant of the same family as wormwood but not the same plant; and such claims easily turn Bible reading into a guessing game. What John wants us to see is not a particular news story but God's judgment on an idolatrous world, and the bitterness that comes on the world because of it.

> The point: the first four trumpets use the plagues of the exodus to tell us that God is Lord of creation and judges the world that oppresses his people; yet before the final judgment his judgments are still limited, and there is still time to turn.

## Going Deeper: 8:13 Three Woes

**1. The eagle in midheaven.** "Then I looked, and I heard an eagle crying with a loud voice as it flew directly overhead, 'Woe, woe, woe to those who dwell on the earth, at the blasts of the other trumpets that the three angels are about to blow!'" (8:13). The King James Version reads "angel" here, following later manuscripts; most of the earlier manuscripts read "eagle." The eagle flies at the highest point of the sky, where everyone can see and hear it.

**2. "Those who dwell on the earth."** In Revelation this phrase usually means not simply where people live but those who treat this world as their only home and set themselves against God (6:10; 13:8). God's people also live on the earth, but they are sealed on their foreheads (7:3), and their names are written in the Lamb's book of life (13:8). The locusts of the fifth trumpet harm "only those people who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads" (9:4).

**3. Three woes.** The three woes go with the last three trumpets: the fifth trumpet is the first woe (9:12), the sixth the second (11:14); the third "is soon to come" (11:14), which most take to be the seventh trumpet and the judgments it unfolds (cf. 12:12). The first four trumpets strike mainly creation; the last three fall directly on people. The judgments grow heavier, but the voice of warning grows louder too.

**4. "Woe" is also a lament.** The Lord Jesus pronounced woe after woe on the scribes and Pharisees, and then said, "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you would not!" (Matt 23:37). The eagle's three woes are severe; yet until the seventh trumpet sounds they are still a warning, and a warning means there is still time.

> The point: before his heaviest judgments God gives a loud warning. Those who hear the "woe" can still turn.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "And another angel came and stood at the altar with a golden censer, and he was given much incense to offer with the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar before the throne, and the smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, rose before God from the hand of the angel" (8:3–4). At the church's Wednesday evening prayer meeting there are always a few brothers and sisters who pray fluently: once they begin, it is a long stretch of prayer in very spiritual language. Auntie Li, sitting in the corner, has never prayed aloud. She did not finish primary school and worked in a factory all her life; the thought of opening her mouth in front of everyone makes her heart pound. When her turn comes, she keeps her head down and waits for someone else to go on. What she prays in her heart are small things: her son's job, her old mother's knees, the neighbor downstairs whose husband has just died. She often thinks: God would rather hear people who know how to talk; these few words of mine don't count for much.

Revelation 8 shows her another picture. The myriad voices of heaven stop: "there was silence in heaven for about half an hour" (8:1); and in that silence what rises before God is "the prayers of all the saints" (8:3). Not the prayers of a few who pray well, but of all the saints, including words spoken only in the heart, in the corner. When Zechariah burned incense in the temple, "the whole multitude of the people were praying outside" (Luke 1:10); their names were not recorded, yet their prayers went up at the same hour as the incense. Then the angel fills the same censer with fire from the altar and throws it on the earth, and the trumpets begin (8:5–6). God's work in history is bound up with these prayers. The few words Auntie Li says in her heart for the widow downstairs are not an extra. God is pleased to let the prayer "your kingdom come" rise from her corner too.

Nor do those prayers rise before God because they are well put. "He was given much incense" (8:3): the incense is added from God's side. Once a year Aaron had to make atonement on the horns of the earthly incense altar "with the blood of the sin offering of atonement" (Exod 30:10); even the altar of incense needed blood to be cleansed before prayer could be accepted. So it is with our prayers. The text does not say what the incense is, but the whole New Testament tells us that what makes them acceptable is Christ, who "gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2). So we "offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (1 Pet 2:5). A brother's fluent prayer and Auntie Li's halting "Lord, please look after my mother's legs" both rise before God by the same Christ; neither rises by its own eloquence.

Perhaps someone has not prayed for a long time and thinks prayer is useless; perhaps someone looks down on long prayers as a performance; perhaps someone prays fluently and quietly looks down on those who cannot speak up. The discouragement that stopped praying, the contempt that sneers at others' prayers, the quiet pride of the one who prays well: all of it can be brought to the Lord just as it is. His blood cleansed not only the altar but those who bring their prayers (cf. Heb 9:23–24). Next time Auntie Li may still stay silent, or she may say just one sentence; either way, her prayer will not fall to the ground. On the golden altar before the throne, the prayers of all the saints are rising with the incense. We did not bring that incense; God gave it. The fragrant offering is Christ, who gave himself up for us. Auntie Li's halting "Lord, please look after my mother's legs" is in that smoke too, rising before God with the prayers of all the saints.
