## 本章脉络

第15章预备了七碗：七位天使掌管「末了的七灾」，「因为神的大怒在这七灾中发尽了」（15:1）。第16章把七碗一个接一个倒出来，中间几乎没有停顿。全章可以分成五段：
- **16:1-4，头三碗：** 有兽印记的人身上生疮，海和江河都变成了血。
- **16:5-7，「义哉！诚哉！」：** 掌管众水的天使和祭坛一同称颂神的判断是公义的。
- **16:8-11，第四碗和第五碗：** 日头烤人，兽的国黑暗了；人亵渎神，却不悔改。
- **16:12-16，第六碗：** 幼发拉底河干了，污秽的灵把众王招聚到哈米吉多顿；中间插进主自己的话：「看哪，我来像贼一样。」
- **16:17-21，第七碗：** 宝座上的大声音说：「成了！」大地震，巴比伦要喝神烈怒的杯。

这一章很沉重，几乎每一碗都叫人想起神从前降在埃及的灾。可是全章的中心不是灾难，而是天上的称颂：「主神－全能者啊，你的判断义哉！诚哉！」（16:7）

## 深读 16:1-4 盛神大怒的七碗

**1. 命令从殿中出来。** 「我听见有大声音从殿中出来，向那七位天使说：『你们去，把盛神大怒的七碗倒在地上。』」（16:1）这殿刚刚「因神的荣耀和能力」充满了烟，「没有人能以进殿」（15:8）。命令是从神圣洁的同在中发出来的，不是从一时的冲动发出来的。

**2. 祷告的碗和忿怒的碗。** 这些碗是「金碗」（15:7）。在第5章，长老们拿着「盛满了香的金炉；这香就是众圣徒的祈祷」（5:8）。和合本译作金炉和金碗，原文却是同一个词（*phialē*）。许多释经者（如毕尔）认为这不是巧合：祭坛底下的灵魂呼求「要等到几时呢」（6:10）；圣徒的祈祷和香一同升到神面前，接着坛上的火就倒在地上（8:3-5）。神的审判，有一部分正是他对受苦百姓祷告的回答。

**3. 新的出埃及。** 疮（出9:10）、水变血（出7:20）、黑暗（出10:22）、青蛙（出8:6）、冰雹（出9:24），七碗处处叫人想起埃及的灾。在埃及，神分别了「以色列人所住的歌珊地」（出8:22）；在这里，疮只生在「那些有兽印记、拜兽像的人身上」（启16:2），而神的仆人额上早已受了印（启7:3）。神正在施行一次更大的出埃及，所以玻璃海上的得胜者唱的是「摩西的歌和羔羊的歌」（启15:3）。

**4. 号和碗。** 七碗和七号的次序很相近：地、海、江河、日头、黑暗、幼发拉底河，然后是末了；不同的是，号只伤害「三分之一」（8:7-12），碗却是全面的。七碗是重述同一段时期，还是先后发生，第6章和第15章已经讨论过，这里不再重复。无论采用哪一种读法，这都是「末了的」七灾（15:1）：神的忿怒是有尽头的。

> 要点：七碗从圣洁的殿中发出，和盛满圣徒祈祷的金炉是同一种碗。神的审判不是任性的发怒，而是他对百姓呼求的回答。

## 深读 16:5-7 「你这样判断是公义的」

**1. 「昔在、今在的圣者」。** 「我听见掌管众水的天使说：昔在、今在的圣者啊，你这样判断是公义的」（16:5）。正是掌管众水的那一位，看见江河变成了血，却称颂神公义。在1:4、1:8和4:8，神被称为「昔在、今在、以后永在」的；在11:17和这里却只说「昔在、今在」。许多释经者认为，这是因为在异象中，神已经来施行审判、「执掌大权作王了」（11:17）。

**2. 「这是他们所该受的」。** 「他们曾流圣徒与先知的血，现在你给他们血喝；这是他们所该受的。」（16:6）惩罚和罪行相称：「你怎样行，他也必照样向你行」（俄1:15）。「该受的」原文（*axios*）和羔羊「配得」（5:9、12）是同一个词。逼迫圣徒的人得着他们的行为所当得的；圣徒能穿白衣与主同行，他们的「配得过」（3:4）却是从那「曾被杀」的羔羊来的（5:9；7:14）。

**3. 祭坛说：「是的」。** 「我又听见祭坛中有声音说：是的，主神－全能者啊，你的判断义哉！诚哉！」（16:7）第五印揭开时，祭坛底下被杀之人的灵魂曾经呼求：「圣洁真实的主啊，你不审判住在地上的人，给我们伸流血的冤，要等到几时呢？」（6:9-10）现在，同一座祭坛回答说：「是的。」这和摩西的歌一样：「你的道途义哉！诚哉！」（15:3；参19:2）

**4. 我们为什么难以接受神的忿怒？** 许多现代读者读到这里会不安。经文至少让我们看见几件事：
- **神的忿怒不像人的怒气：** 人发怒常是失控、偏心、过分的；神的判断却是「义哉！诚哉！」，不多也不少。「神的忿怒从天上显明在一切不虔不义的人身上」（罗1:18），这是他的圣洁对罪恶坚定的反对。
- **从不审判的神，并不是更有爱：** 一位看见虐待孩童、屠杀无辜却从不发怒的神，不是慈爱，而是冷漠。神要「败坏那些败坏世界之人」（11:18）；他的忿怒保护他所造、所爱的。
- **相信神审判，叫人放下报复：** 克罗地亚神学家米罗斯拉夫·沃尔夫经历过前南斯拉夫的战争。他在《排斥与接纳》（1996年）中大意是说：正因为相信神最终要公义地审判，受害的人才能放下手中的刀；以为神从不审判的想法，往往是在安稳舒适的地方才容易产生的。启示录里的圣徒从来没有拿起刀；他们祷告、作见证，把伸冤交给神（13:10；罗12:19）。这并不是说受害的人要沉默：向警察和法院举报罪行是对的（罗13:4）。
- **审判以前，福音仍在传：** 就在七碗以前，天使还在传「永远的福音」，呼召人「应当敬畏神，将荣耀归给他」（14:6-7）。

> 要点：受苦的圣徒并不为神的忿怒难为情，他们说：「是的。」神审判罪恶，是他公义和慈爱的一部分，不是与它们相反。

## 深读 16:8-11 「并不悔改」

**1. 同一个日头。** 「第四位天使把碗倒在日头上，叫日头能用火烤人。」（16:8）这和神对他百姓的应许正好相反：「日头和炎热也必不伤害他们」（7:16）。

**2. 兽的座位黑暗了。** 第五碗「倒在兽的座位上，兽的国就黑暗了」（16:10），像埃及「遍地就乌黑了三天」（出10:22）。兽的座位是龙给它的（启13:2）。它曾叫全地希奇跟从（13:3），现在连它的国也陷在黑暗里。兽的权柄是借来的，也是有限的。

**3. 苦难本身不会叫人悔改。** 人「亵渎那有权掌管这些灾的神之名，并不悔改将荣耀归给神」（16:9）；又「亵渎天上的神，并不悔改所行的」（16:11）。他们承认神有权柄，却不肯低头，拒绝的正是永远福音的呼召（14:7）。在11:13，经历地震的人「其余的都恐惧，归荣耀给天上的神」；这里的人却像法老：「法老见灾祸松缓，就硬着心」（出8:15；参启9:20-21）。苦难可以叫人回头，也可以叫人更刚硬。悔改是神的恩赐：「或者神给他们悔改的心」（提后2:25）。不过经文说他们「不悔改」，也表示悔改原是他们当做的；主论耶洗别也说：「我曾给她悔改的机会」（启2:21）。

> 要点：同样的痛苦，有人归荣耀给神，有人却亵渎他。悔改不是苦难逼出来的，而是神的恩典赐下的。

## 深读 16:12-16 哈米吉多顿

**1. 幼发拉底河干了。** 第六碗倒在幼发拉底河上，「河水就干了，要给那从日出之地所来的众王预备道路」（16:12）。这条河是古代巴比伦的屏障；古代的历史家（如希罗多德）记载，波斯王塞鲁士曾把河水引开，从河床攻进巴比伦城（参赛44:27-28；耶51:36）。从前神使红海和约旦河干了，叫他的百姓过去（出14:21；书3:17）；现在神使河水干了，却是叫敌对他的众王聚集，走向他们自己的审判。

**2. 「从日出之地所来的众王」。** 释经者有几种看法：
- **一世纪的背景：** 罗马人惧怕河东的帕提亚人，当时也流传着尼禄要带着东方的军队回来的传说（参第17章）。
- **将来从东方来的军队：** 一些未来派这样看（例如华尔伏）。1970年代美国一本畅销的预言书（哈尔·林赛著）把他们和9:16「二万万」的马军连在一起，说就是中国的军队；这种说法也在华人教会中流传过。
- **普天下的众王：** 毕尔等人指出，经文接着说这些灵出去「到普天下众王那里」（16:14），所以「东方」很可能代表全世界一同敌挡神的权势。
无论哪一种看法，重点都不是哪一国的军队，而是神使他们聚集，为要审判他们。

**3. 像青蛙的污秽之灵。** 三个污秽的灵「从龙口、兽口并假先知的口中出来」（16:13）。许多人把龙、兽和假先知称为邪恶的三一，是对父、子、圣灵的模仿。假先知就是第13章那「说话好像龙」、行奇事迷惑人的兽（13:11-14；19:20）。灵从「口」中出来，表示这是骗人的言语。在埃及，行法术的也能「照样而行，叫青蛙上了埃及地」（出8:7）；仇敌能模仿神迹，却不能救人。

**4. 「看哪，我来像贼一样」。** 正当众王聚集，主自己的声音插进来：「看哪，我来像贼一样。那警醒、看守衣服、免得赤身而行、叫人见他羞耻的有福了！」（16:15）这是全书七次「有福」中的第三次。主曾这样警告撒狄（3:3；参太24:43）；他也劝老底嘉「买白衣穿上，叫你赤身的羞耻不露出来」（启3:18）。这白衣是用羔羊的血洗白的（7:14）。在描写末日大战的中间，主给教会的吩咐不是推算战争的时间和地点，而是警醒，穿好他所赐的衣服。

**5. 哈米吉多顿是什么？** 「那三个鬼魔便叫众王聚集在一处，希伯来话叫作哈米吉多顿。」（16:16）许多学者认为，这个名字的意思是米吉多的山。米吉多在耶斯列平原边上，是旧约多次争战的地方（士5:19；王下23:29；亚12:11）。主要有两种看法：
- **将来在米吉多一带真实的战争：** 许多时代论者（例如华尔伏）认为，主再来以前，列国的军队真要聚集在以色列北部的这个平原。
- **最后一次敌挡神的总冲突：** 毕尔、包衡等人指出，米吉多是一座土丘，附近并没有一座叫米吉多的山，所以这名字可能是象征性的，借旧约著名的战场代表世上君王最后一次聚集攻击神和他的百姓。
不少学者注意到，「聚集……争战」在16:14、19:19和20:8都出现，可能是从不同角度描写同一场最后的争战。经文在这里根本没有描写战争的过程；到了19:20-21和20:9，仇敌一下子就被擒拿、被烧灭了。所以读到中东的新闻，我们不必把它当作末日的倒计时（参看导读论定日期的提醒）。

> 要点：仇敌能用言语和奇事招聚全世界的君王，却改变不了结局。主的吩咐不是叫我们推算哈米吉多顿，而是警醒，穿着他所赐的白衣。

## 深读 16:17-21 「成了！」

**1. 宝座上的声音。** 「第七位天使把碗倒在空中，就有大声音从殿中的宝座上出来，说：『成了！』」（16:17）这个动词（*gegonen*）是完成式，表示事情已经完成。新天新地降临时，坐宝座的也说：「都成了！」（21:6）主耶稣在十字架上说的「成了」（约19:30）原文是另一个词，不过许多读者看见这两句话在神学上的关连：神对罪恶的审判要在末日完成；而在十字架上，羔羊已经为信他的人担当了这审判。

**2. 空前的大地震。** 「又有闪电、声音、雷轰、大地震，自从地上有人以来，没有这样大、这样厉害的地震。」（16:18）包衡指出，这些从宝座发出的记号在4:5、8:5、11:19和这里一次比一次加多，像神在西乃山降临时一样（出19:16）：印、号、碗都走向同一个高潮，就是圣洁的神亲自来审判。

**3. 神想起巴比伦大城来。** 「那大城裂为三段，列国的城也都倒塌了；神也想起巴比伦大城来，要把那盛自己烈怒的酒杯递给他。」（16:19）多数释经者认为「那大城」就是巴比伦；也有人（例如一些过去派）认为指耶路撒冷（参11:8）。这杯在14:10已经提过，也是先知的画面：「耶和华手里有杯……地上的恶人必都喝这酒的渣滓」（诗75:8；参耶25:15）。「神想起」，表示他没有忘记流在那城里的血（启18:5、24）。第17-18章要详细描写这城的倾倒。

**4. 万物在审判者面前退去。** 「各海岛都逃避了，众山也不见了。」（16:20）这预告了白色大宝座前「天地都逃避」的景象（20:11）。每个约重一他连得（大约几十公斤）的大雹从天落下，可是全章最后一句仍是：「人就亵渎神」（16:21）。

> 要点：七碗以神的一句「成了」结束。审判世界的神，也是在十字架上为信他的人担当了审判的神。

## 默想

**默想：** 「我又听见祭坛中有声音说：是的，主神－全能者啊，你的判断义哉！诚哉！」（16:7）小雅是大三的学生，团契正一章一章地读启示录。轮到第16章那个星期，她在宿舍里预备查经，室友凑过来念了几行「给他们血喝……这是他们所该受的」，笑着说她的神像复仇电影里的人。小雅也跟着笑，说这是古老的写法，现在不太这样理解，然后关掉了手机上的圣经。她老家教会的牧师讲道二十年，几乎从来不讲审判，怕把年轻人和慕道友吓跑。说实话，我们很多人都和他们一样：读到神的忿怒，有一点难为情，好像要替神道歉。

可是在这一章里，称颂神「义哉！诚哉！」的，不是坐在安稳地方的旁观者，而是祭坛，就是那些「为神的道、并为作见证被杀之人」所在的地方（6:9）。亲眼看见弟兄姊妹的血被流的人，并不为神的审判难为情；他们一直在问的，是神要等到几时。一位看见孩子被虐待、无辜的人被屠杀却从不发怒的神，并不是更有爱的神。神的忿怒，是他的爱对一切毁坏他所造之物的拒绝。

不过如果我诚实，我的不安还有更深的原因：那些碗是「盛满」的（15:7），而我并不只站在受害者那一边。我也曾在难处中埋怨神，而不是将荣耀归给他（16:9）。神的审判叫我难为情，是因为它也照出了我自己。那「盛自己烈怒的酒杯」（16:19），我也配喝。

就在这里，福音进来了。在客西马尼园，主耶稣俯伏祷告：「阿爸！父啊！在你凡事都能；求你将这杯撤去。然而，不要从我的意思，只要从你的意思。」（可14:36）几个小时以后，彼得拔刀，耶稣却对他说：「收刀入鞘吧，我父所给我的那杯，我岂可不喝呢？」（约18:11）他所惧怕的，不只是鞭打和钉子，更是先知们所说神忿怒的杯。他喝了，一直喝到底。以赛亚早就听见神应许：「看哪，我已将那使人东倒西歪的杯，就是我忿怒的爵，从你手中接过来；你必不致再喝。」（赛51:22）「现在我们既靠着他的血称义，就更要藉着他免去神的忿怒。」（罗5:9）

这对那天关上手机、陪着室友笑的小雅是好消息；对那位多年把审判轻轻带过的牧师，也是好消息。主没有因为我们曾为他的话难为情，就把我们留在碗下；他喝的那杯，也担当了我们的怯懦和沉默。牧师不必从此改用恐惧来传道，他可以像祭坛那样讲神的判断，也像客西马尼那样讲神的儿子。小雅也可以再打开第16章，告诉室友：神向罪恶发怒，我为此感谢他；那碗本来也该倒在我身上，可是有一位替我喝了那杯。

七碗倒出来的时候，在基督里的人不在碗下。他们站在玻璃海上唱：「万世之王啊，你的道途义哉！诚哉！」（15:3）他们能毫无惧怕地唱，只因为那位羔羊在客西马尼说了「我岂可不喝呢」，然后真的为他们把那杯喝干了。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 15 prepared the seven bowls: seven angels with "seven plagues, which are the last, for with them the wrath of God is finished" (15:1). Chapter 16 pours them out one after another, with hardly a pause. The chapter falls into five parts:
- **16:1–4, the first three bowls:** sores break out on those who bear the mark of the beast, and the sea and the rivers become blood.
- **16:5–7, "Just are you":** the angel in charge of the waters and the altar together praise God's judgments as just.
- **16:8–11, the fourth and fifth bowls:** the sun scorches people, and the beast's kingdom is plunged into darkness; people curse God but do not repent.
- **16:12–16, the sixth bowl:** the Euphrates dries up, and unclean spirits gather the kings to Armageddon; in the middle comes the Lord's own word: "Behold, I am coming like a thief!"
- **16:17–21, the seventh bowl:** a loud voice from the throne says, "It is done!" A great earthquake follows, and Babylon is made to drain the cup of God's fury.

The chapter is heavy, and almost every bowl recalls the plagues God once sent on Egypt. Yet its center is not the disasters but the praise of heaven: "Yes, Lord God the Almighty, true and just are your judgments!" (16:7)

## Going Deeper: 16:1–4 The Seven Bowls of the Wrath of God

**1. The command comes from the temple.** "Then I heard a loud voice from the temple telling the seven angels, 'Go and pour out on the earth the seven bowls of the wrath of God'" (16:1). This temple has just been "filled with smoke from the glory of God and from his power," so that "no one could enter" (15:8). The command comes from God's holy presence, not from a passing impulse.

**2. Bowls of prayer and bowls of wrath.** These are "golden bowls" (15:7). In chapter 5 the elders held "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (5:8). The Chinese Union Version uses two different words, but the Greek word is the same (*phialē*). Many interpreters (such as Beale) think this is no accident: the souls under the altar cry, "How long?" (6:10); the prayers of the saints rise with incense before God, and then fire from the altar is thrown on the earth (8:3–5). God's judgment is, in part, his answer to the prayers of his suffering people.

**3. A new exodus.** Sores (Exod 9:10), water turned to blood (Exod 7:20), darkness (Exod 10:22), frogs (Exod 8:6), hail (Exod 9:24): the bowls recall the plagues of Egypt at every turn. In Egypt God set apart "the land of Goshen, where my people dwell" (Exod 8:22). Here the sores fall only "upon the people who bore the mark of the beast and worshiped its image" (Rev 16:2), while God's servants have already been sealed on their foreheads (Rev 7:3). God is carrying out a greater exodus, and so the conquerors by the sea of glass sing "the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb" (Rev 15:3).

**4. Trumpets and bowls.** The bowls follow almost the same order as the trumpets: earth, sea, rivers, sun, darkness, the Euphrates, and then the end; the difference is that the trumpets strike "a third" (8:7–12), while the bowls are total. Whether the bowls retell the same period or follow the trumpets in sequence is discussed in chapters 6 and 15, and is not repeated here. On either reading these are the "last" plagues (15:1): God's wrath has an end.

> The point: The bowls come out of the holy temple, and they are the same kind of bowl that held the prayers of the saints. God's judgment is not a fit of temper but his answer to his people's cry.

## Going Deeper: 16:5–7 "Just Are You"

**1. "Who is and who was."** "And I heard the angel in charge of the waters say, 'Just are you, O Holy One, who is and who was, for you brought these judgments'" (16:5). It is the very angel over the waters who sees the rivers turned to blood and praises God as just. In 1:4, 1:8 and 4:8 God is the one "who was and is and is to come"; here, as in 11:17, he is only the one "who is and who was." Many interpreters think the third phrase drops out because in the vision God has now come in judgment and "begun to reign" (11:17).

**2. "It is what they deserve."** "For they have shed the blood of saints and prophets, and you have given them blood to drink. It is what they deserve!" (16:6) The punishment fits the crime: "As you have done, it shall be done to you" (Obad 15). The Greek for "deserve" (*axios*) is the same word as the Lamb's "worthy" (5:9, 12). Those who persecute the saints receive what their deeds deserve; the saints walk in white, but their being "worthy" (3:4) comes from the Lamb who "was slain" (5:9; 7:14).

**3. The altar says, "Yes."** "And I heard the altar saying, 'Yes, Lord God the Almighty, true and just are your judgments!'" (16:7) At the fifth seal the souls of the slain under the altar cried, "O Sovereign Lord, holy and true, how long before you will judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" (6:9–10) Now the same altar answers, "Yes." It echoes the song of Moses: "Just and true are your ways" (15:3; see 19:2).

**4. Why do we find God's wrath hard to accept?** Many modern readers are uneasy here. The text shows us at least these things:
- **God's wrath is not like human anger:** human anger is often uncontrolled, partial and excessive, but God's judgments are "true and just," neither more nor less. "The wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men" (Rom 1:18); it is his holiness standing firmly against evil.
- **A God who never judged would not be more loving:** a God who saw children abused and the innocent slaughtered and was never angry would not be kind but indifferent. God will destroy "the destroyers of the earth" (11:18); his wrath protects what he has made and loves.
- **Believing in God's judgment frees us from revenge:** the Croatian theologian Miroslav Volf lived through the wars in the former Yugoslavia. In *Exclusion and Embrace* (1996) he argues, in effect, that belief in God's final, just judgment is what lets victims lay down the sword, and that the idea of a God who never judges tends to grow in quiet and comfortable places. The saints in Revelation never take up the sword; they pray, bear witness and leave vengeance to God (13:10; Rom 12:19). This does not mean victims must stay silent: reporting crime to the police and the courts is right (Rom 13:4).
- **Before judgment, the gospel is still proclaimed:** just before the bowls, an angel proclaims "an eternal gospel," calling people to "fear God and give him glory" (14:6–7).

> The point: The suffering saints are not embarrassed by God's wrath; they say, "Yes." God's judgment of evil is part of his justice and his love, not their opposite.

## Going Deeper: 16:8–11 "They Did Not Repent"

**1. The same sun.** "The fourth angel poured out his bowl on the sun, and it was allowed to scorch people with fire" (16:8). This is the reverse of God's promise to his people: "the sun shall not strike them, nor any scorching heat" (7:16).

**2. Darkness on the beast's throne.** The fifth bowl is poured "on the throne of the beast, and its kingdom was plunged into darkness" (16:10), as when "there was pitch darkness in all the land of Egypt three days" (Exod 10:22). The dragon gave the beast its throne (Rev 13:2). Once the whole earth marveled and followed it (13:3); now its own kingdom lies in darkness. The beast's power is borrowed, and limited.

**3. Suffering alone does not bring repentance.** People "cursed the name of God who had power over these plagues. They did not repent and give him glory" (16:9); they "cursed the God of heaven for their pain and sores. They did not repent of their deeds" (16:11). They admit God's power but will not bow, refusing the very call of the eternal gospel (14:7). After the earthquake of 11:13, "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven"; these people are like Pharaoh instead: "when Pharaoh saw that there was a respite, he hardened his heart" (Exod 8:15; see Rev 9:20–21). Suffering can turn people back, and it can make them harder. Repentance is God's gift: "God may perhaps grant them repentance" (2 Tim 2:25). Yet the words "they did not repent" also imply that repentance was what they ought to do; of Jezebel the Lord says, "I gave her time to repent" (Rev 2:21).

> The point: Under the same pain, some give glory to God and others curse him. Repentance is not squeezed out by suffering; it is given by God's grace.

## Going Deeper: 16:12–16 Armageddon

**1. The Euphrates dries up.** The sixth bowl is poured on the Euphrates, "and its water was dried up, to prepare the way for the kings from the east" (16:12). The river was the defense of ancient Babylon; ancient historians (such as Herodotus) record that Cyrus of Persia diverted it and entered the city along its bed (see Isa 44:27–28; Jer 51:36). Once God dried up the Red Sea and the Jordan so that his people could pass over (Exod 14:21; Josh 3:17). Now he dries up the river so that the kings who oppose him can gather, and march toward their own judgment.

**2. "The kings from the east."** Interpreters hold several views:
- **The first-century background:** Rome feared the Parthians beyond the river, and a legend was current that Nero would return leading armies from the east (see chapter 17).
- **Armies from the east still to come:** some futurists take it this way (for example Walvoord). A best-selling American prophecy book of 1970 (by Hal Lindsey) linked these kings with the army of "twice ten thousand times ten thousand" in 9:16 and identified it as China's; the idea has also circulated in Chinese churches.
- **The kings of the whole world:** Beale and others point out that the text goes on to say these spirits go out "to the kings of the whole world" (16:14), so "the east" probably stands for all the world's powers rising together against God.
On any view, the point is not which nation's army it is, but that God lets them gather in order to judge them.

**3. Unclean spirits like frogs.** Three unclean spirits come "out of the mouth of the dragon and out of the mouth of the beast and out of the mouth of the false prophet" (16:13). Many call the dragon, the beast and the false prophet an "unholy trinity," a parody of the Father, the Son and the Spirit. The false prophet is the beast of chapter 13 that "spoke like a dragon" and deceived people by signs (13:11–14; 19:20). The spirits come out of mouths: these are deceiving words. In Egypt the magicians "did the same by their secret arts and made frogs come up on the land of Egypt" (Exod 8:7); the enemy can imitate signs, but he cannot save.

**4. "Behold, I am coming like a thief!"** Just as the kings gather, the Lord's own voice breaks in: "Behold, I am coming like a thief! Blessed is the one who stays awake, keeping his garments on, that he may not go about naked and be seen exposed!" (16:15) This is the third of the book's seven beatitudes. The Lord warned Sardis in these words (3:3; see Matt 24:43), and he counseled Laodicea to buy "white garments so that you may clothe yourself and the shame of your nakedness may not be seen" (Rev 3:18). These robes are washed white in the blood of the Lamb (7:14). In the middle of a picture of the last great battle, the Lord's instruction to the church is not to calculate its time and place, but to stay awake and keep on the garments he has given.

**5. What is Armageddon?** "And they assembled them at the place that in Hebrew is called Armageddon." (16:16) Many scholars take the name to mean "mountain of Megiddo." Megiddo stood at the edge of the Jezreel plain, the site of many Old Testament battles (Judg 5:19; 2 Kgs 23:29; Zech 12:11). There are two main views:
- **A real future battle near Megiddo:** many dispensationalists (for example Walvoord) believe the armies of the nations will literally gather in this plain in northern Israel before the Lord returns.
- **The final conflict against God:** Beale, Bauckham and others note that Megiddo is a mound, with no "mountain of Megiddo" nearby, so the name may be symbolic, using a famous Old Testament battlefield to stand for the last gathering of the world's rulers against God and his people.
Many scholars notice that "gather... for battle" appears in 16:14, 19:19 and 20:8, and think these may describe the same final battle from different angles. The text describes no fighting at all here; in 19:20–21 and 20:9 the enemies are simply captured and consumed. So when we read news from the Middle East, we need not treat it as a countdown to the end (see the introduction's warning about setting dates).

> The point: The enemy can use words and signs to gather all the kings of the world, but he cannot change the outcome. The Lord's instruction is not to work out where Armageddon is, but to stay awake, clothed in the white garments he gives.

## Going Deeper: 16:17–21 "It Is Done!"

**1. The voice from the throne.** "The seventh angel poured out his bowl into the air, and a loud voice came out of the temple, from the throne, saying, 'It is done!'" (16:17) The verb (*gegonen*) is a perfect: the thing has been accomplished. When the new heaven and new earth come, the One on the throne will say again, "It is done!" (21:6) Jesus' "It is finished" on the cross (John 19:30) uses a different Greek word, but many readers see a theological link: God's judgment of evil will be completed at the end, and on the cross the Lamb has already borne that judgment for all who trust in him.

**2. An earthquake without equal.** "And there were flashes of lightning, rumblings, peals of thunder, and a great earthquake such as there had never been since man was on the earth, so great was that earthquake." (16:18) Bauckham points out that these signs from the throne grow at each step, in 4:5, 8:5, 11:19 and here, like the signs when God came down on Sinai (Exod 19:16): the seals, trumpets and bowls all move toward one climax, the holy God himself coming to judge.

**3. God remembered Babylon the great.** "The great city was split into three parts, and the cities of the nations fell, and God remembered Babylon the great, to make her drain the cup of the wine of the fury of his wrath." (16:19) Most interpreters take "the great city" to be Babylon; some (for example some preterists) take it to be Jerusalem (see 11:8). This cup was mentioned in 14:10, and it is the prophets' picture: "in the hand of the LORD there is a cup... all the wicked of the earth shall drain it down to the dregs" (Ps 75:8; see Jer 25:15). "God remembered" means he has not forgotten the blood shed in that city (Rev 18:5, 24). Chapters 17–18 describe this city's fall at length.

**4. All things flee before the Judge.** "And every island fled away, and no mountains were to be found." (16:20) This anticipates the great white throne, from whose presence "earth and sky fled away" (20:11). Hailstones of "about one hundred pounds each" fall from heaven, and yet the chapter's last words are: "they cursed God" (16:21).

> The point: The bowls end with God's word, "It is done!" The God who judges the world is the God who, on the cross, bore judgment for all who trust in him.

## Meditation

**Meditation:** "And I heard the altar saying, 'Yes, Lord God the Almighty, true and just are your judgments!'" (16:7) Xiaoya is a third-year university student, and her fellowship is reading through Revelation chapter by chapter. In the week of chapter 16 she is preparing in her dorm room when her roommate leans over, reads aloud a few lines, "you have given them blood to drink. It is what they deserve!", and laughs that her God sounds like someone out of a revenge movie. Xiaoya laughs along, says it is an old way of writing that no one really takes like that now, and closes the Bible app on her phone. The pastor of her home church has preached for twenty years and almost never on judgment, afraid of driving away young people and seekers. Honestly, many of us are like them: when we read of God's wrath we feel a little embarrassed, as though we should apologize for him.

But in this chapter the ones who praise God as "true and just" are not onlookers in safe places. It is the altar, where lay "those who had been slain for the word of God and for the witness they had borne" (6:9). Those who have watched the blood of their brothers and sisters being shed are not embarrassed by God's judgment; the question they kept asking was how long he would wait. A God who saw children abused and the innocent slaughtered and was never angry would not be a more loving God. God's wrath is his love's refusal of everything that destroys what he has made.

And yet, if I am honest, my unease has a deeper root. Those bowls are "full" (15:7), and I do not stand only on the side of the victims. I too have complained against God in hard times instead of giving him glory (16:9). God's judgment embarrasses me because it shows me myself. The "cup of the wine of the fury of his wrath" (16:19) is one I deserve to drink.

Right here the gospel comes in. In Gethsemane the Lord Jesus fell to the ground and prayed: "Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will." (Mark 14:36) A few hours later, when Peter drew his sword, Jesus said to him, "Put your sword into its sheath; shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?" (John 18:11) What he dreaded was not only the whip and the nails, but the cup of God's wrath that the prophets spoke of. He drank it, all the way down. Long before, Isaiah heard God promise: "Behold, I have taken from your hand the cup of staggering; the bowl of my wrath you shall drink no more" (Isa 51:22). "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God." (Rom 5:9)

That is good news for Xiaoya, who closed her phone and laughed along. It is good news for the pastor who for years passed lightly over the words of judgment. The Lord has not left us under the bowl because we were once embarrassed by his word; the cup he drank bore our cowardice and our silence too. The pastor need not now switch to preaching by fear; he can speak of God's judgments as the altar does, and of God's Son as Gethsemane does. And Xiaoya can open chapter 16 again and tell her roommate: God is angry at evil, and I thank him for it; that bowl should have been poured on me too, but someone drank the cup for me.

When the seven bowls are poured out, those who are in Christ are not under them. They stand beside the sea of glass and sing, "Just and true are your ways, O King of the nations!" (15:3) They can sing it without fear for one reason only: in Gethsemane the Lamb said, "Shall I not drink the cup?" and then drank it dry for them.
