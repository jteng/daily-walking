## 本章脉络

第17章让约翰看见骑在兽上的大淫妇，就是「管辖地上众王的大城」（17:18）；第18章接着描写这座城的倾倒。全章几乎都是诗，像一首哀歌，又像先知宣告审判的信息。约翰用的话，大多来自旧约先知对巴比伦（赛13章；21章；47章；耶50-51章）和推罗（结26-28章）的预言。全章可以分成五段：
- **18:1-3，倾倒的宣告：** 一位有大权柄的天使从天降下，大声喊着说：「巴比伦大城倾倒了！倾倒了！」
- **18:4-8，「你们要从那城出来」：** 从天上有声音呼召神的百姓离开她的罪，并说明她受审判的原因。
- **18:9-19，三首哀歌：** 地上的君王、客商、船主和水手都「远远地站着」，为她哭泣哀号。
- **18:20，天上的欢喜：** 众圣徒、众使徒、众先知要因神为他们伸冤而欢喜。
- **18:21-24，大磨石：** 一位大力的天使把一块大磨石扔在海里，宣告这城要永远沉寂。

巴比伦指的是什么，第17章已经列出五种主要的看法：
- **一世纪的罗马：** 多数释经者的看法。
- **背道的耶路撒冷：** 一些过去派的看法。
- **教皇的罗马：** 许多宗教改革时期历史派的看法。
- **将来重建的巴比伦城：** 一些时代论者认为古巴比伦会在末日重建，也有时代论者认为这是末日的世界体系。
- **一切敌挡神的世界体系：** 理想派（例如威廉·亨德里克森）认为巴比伦代表每个时代用享乐、财富和权势引诱人离开神的世界。

这里只补充一点：有些时代论者（例如约翰·华尔伏）把两章分开，第17章读作宗教的巴比伦，第18章读作政治和经济的巴比伦。

本研读和多数释经者一样，认为第17、18章说的是同一座城，因为两章都称她为「大城」（17:18；18:10），她的打扮一样（17:4；18:16），审判也都在「一时之间」（17:12；18:10）。她是约翰当年眼前的罗马，也是每一个时代自高自大、靠财富和权势引诱列国离开神的「大城」。

## 深读 18:1-3 倾倒了！倾倒了！

**1. 「地就因他的荣耀发光」。** 「此后，我看见另有一位有大权柄的天使从天降下，地就因他的荣耀发光。」（18:1）以西结看见神的荣耀回到圣殿的时候，也说「地就因他的荣耀发光」（结43:2）。巴比伦用金子、宝石和珍珠妆饰自己（17:4），天使一来，她的光彩就被比下去了。

**2. 已经倾倒了。** 「他大声喊着说：巴比伦大城倾倒了！倾倒了！」（18:2）这句话第14章已经说过一次（14:8），出自以赛亚书：「巴比伦倾倒了！倾倒了！他一切雕刻的神像都打碎于地。」（赛21:9）「倾倒了」在原文是不定过去时，许多释经者认为这是先知的笔法：事情还在将来，却说得像已经发生一样，因为神既然定了，就必定成就。在亚细亚那些小教会眼中，罗马好像永远不会倒；天使却说，在神眼中，她已经倒了。

**3. 三种罪。** 「因为列国都被她邪淫大怒的酒倾倒了。地上的君王与她行淫；地上的客商因她奢华太过就发了财。」（18:3）
- **列国喝了她的酒，君王与她行淫：** 「邪淫」在先知书中常指拜偶像和对神不忠（何2:2-5）。罗马把皇帝崇拜、各城的守护神和她的繁荣连在一起，列国和君王为了分享她的权势和富足，就在她的偶像面前低头。
- **客商因她发了财：** 这一章特别注意经济。包衡有一篇专门讨论第18章的文章，收在他的《预言的高峰》（*The Climax of Prophecy*）一书中。他指出约翰对罗马的批评，很大一部分是针对她的经济：整个帝国的财富都流向这座城，供她一个城享受。
巴比伦的罪，不只是道德上的放纵，更是一整套的生活方式：把财富、安全感和身份都建立在神以外的东西上。

> 要点：在人看来最坚固、最繁华的城，在神面前已经倾倒了。神的百姓不必被她的光彩迷住，也不必被她的权势吓倒。

## 深读 18:4-8 我的民哪，你们要从那城出来

**1. 「我的民哪」。** 「我又听见从天上有声音说：我的民哪，你们要从那城出来，免得与她一同有罪，受她所受的灾殃」（18:4）。这是旧约先知对被掳的以色列人一再说的话：「我的民哪，你们要从其中出去！各人拯救自己，躲避耶和华的烈怒。」（耶51:45；参赛48:20；52:11）保罗也引用过同样的话：「你们务要从他们中间出来，与他们分别」（林后6:17）。请留意：神还没有说「出来」，就先说「我的民」。他们还住在巴比伦，神已经认他们是自己的人。

**2. 「出来」是什么意思？** 释经者有几种看法：
- **在生活上与她的罪分开：** 许多释经者（例如包衡、毕尔）认为，这不是叫信徒搬出罗马城，而是叫他们不再参与她拜偶像、贪婪和欺压的事，「免得与她一同有罪」。
- **真实地逃离一座城：** 有些时代论者认为巴比伦是将来一座真实的城，就把这句话读作末世的信徒要真正离开那城。有些过去派把巴比伦读作耶路撒冷，就常联想到优西比乌《教会史》所记，耶路撒冷的信徒在城被围以前逃到了比拉（第三卷第五章第三节）。
这两种读法都承认：神的百姓不可和巴比伦同流合污。不过，「出来」并不等于离开社会、躲进山里。同一位神也曾吩咐被掳的人：「我所使你们被掳到的那城，你们要为那城求平安，为那城祷告耶和华」（耶29:7）。信徒住在城里，为城祷告，在城里工作；只是不再分享她的罪，也不再把心交给她。

**3. 「她的不义，神已经想起来了」。** 「因她的罪恶滔天；她的不义，神已经想起来了。」（18:5）「滔天」是说她的罪一直连到天上，这句话来自耶利米书论巴比伦的话：「她受的审判通于上天，达到穹苍」（耶51:9）；也有人联想到巴别塔「塔顶通天」（创11:4）。神「想起」，不是说他从前忘了，而是说到了他施行审判的时候（16:19）。那些在帝国里默默受苦、没有人理会的人，神都记得。

**4. 「加倍地报应她」。** 「她怎样待人，也要怎样待她，按她所行的加倍地报应她；用她调酒的杯加倍地调给她喝。」（18:6）
- **对谁说的？** 这里不是吩咐圣徒去报仇。有人认为是对执行审判的天使说的，有人认为是对兽和十角说的，因为它们后来要「用火将她烧尽」（17:16）。无论哪一种，伸冤的是神（19:2；罗12:19）。
- **「加倍」：** 许多人认为这是旧约的说法，表示完全、足够的报应（耶16:18；参赛40:2）；也有学者（例如毕尔，跟随梅雷迪思·克莱恩）倾向认为，这个词在这里指相等、相称的报应，正如上半句所说「她怎样待人，也要怎样待她」。
无论哪一种，神的审判都不是任意的，而是按着她「所行的」。

**5. 「我坐了皇后的位」。** 「她怎样荣耀自己，怎样奢华，也当叫她照样痛苦悲哀，因她心里说：我坐了皇后的位，并不是寡妇，决不至于悲哀。」（18:7）这句话几乎是从以赛亚书搬过来的：古时的巴比伦说「我必永为主母」，又说「我必不致寡居，也不遭丧子之事」（赛47:7-8）。她的根本罪是骄傲：以为自己永远安全，不需要神。所以「在一天之内，她的灾殃要一齐来到」，「因为审判她的主神大有能力」（18:8）。

> 要点：神还没有说「出来」，就先说「我的民」。他呼召属他的人，在巴比伦中间生活，却不分享她的罪。

## 深读 18:9-19 远远地站着的哀哭

**1. 三群哀哭的人。** 先是地上的君王（18:9-10），然后是地上的客商（18:11-17上），最后是船主、水手和一切靠海为业的（18:17下-19）。这三首哀歌的样式，来自以西结书为推罗所作的哀歌：推罗的客商、水手和掌舵的也为她哭号，把尘土撒在头上（结27:27-36）。三首哀歌都说他们「因怕她的痛苦」（18:10、15）「远远地站着」，都喊「哀哉！哀哉！」，又都说审判在「一时之间」就来到了（18:10、17、19）。他们和她一同奢华，却不愿和她一同受苦；几百年积起来的富厚，一个时辰就没有了。

**2. 他们哭的是什么？** 客商哭，是「因为没有人再买他们的货物了」（18:11）。船主和水手哭，是因为「凡有船在海中的，都因她的珍宝成了富足」（18:19），如今再也赚不到了。他们没有一个为这城里的人哭，也没有一个为她的罪哭。他们哭的是自己的生意。水手喊着说：「有何城能比这大城呢？」（18:18）这句话叫人想起世人怎样拜兽：「谁能比这兽，谁能与它交战呢？」（13:4）

**3. 一张货单。** 约翰把客商的货物一样一样列出来（18:12-13），共有二十几种：从金银珠宝、丝绸香料，到牛羊车马，最后是「奴仆、人口」。包衡指出，这张单子上的许多货物，正是当时从帝国各地、甚至从遥远的东方运进罗马城的奢侈品。一座城的奢华，要用整个世界来供养。

**4. 「奴仆、人口」。** 这张单子最后的两项最叫人心惊。「奴仆」这个词，原文的意思是身体（*sōmata*），当时的买卖文书常用它指奴隶；「人口」原文是人的魂（*psychas anthrōpōn*），正是七十士译本以西结书27:13的说法。这两个词可以有两种读法：
- **同一样东西的两种说法：** 奴仆，也就是人的魂。英文标准版就这样译，意思是：这些被当作身体买卖的，其实是有灵魂的人。
- **两类人：** 「奴仆」是已经为奴的人，「人口」可能指被掳掠、被贩卖的其他人。
无论哪一种读法，约翰都故意把人列在牛、羊、车、马之后，好像他们只是另一种货物。以西结书也说，推罗用「人口和铜器」交换货物（结27:13）。罗马帝国的经济大大倚靠奴隶劳动；这座城的繁华，离不开人口买卖。巴比伦最深的罪，就在这里：她把人的魂当作货物。

**5. 一切都失去了。** 「哪，你所贪爱的果子离开了你；你一切的珍馐美味和华美的物件也从你中间毁灭，决不能再见了。」（18:14）这城穿着细麻、紫色、朱红色的衣服（18:16），和第17章大淫妇的打扮一样（17:4）。到第19章，我们会看见另一个女子的衣服：羔羊的新妇所穿的，是「光明洁白的细麻衣」（19:8）。

> 要点：巴比伦的客商为失去的货物哭，却不为被当作货物的人哭。一个把人的魂列在货单末尾的文明，无论多么繁华，在神面前都已经被放在天平里称过，显出亏欠了（但5:27）。

## 深读 18:20-24 大磨石扔在海里

**1. 「你们都要因她欢喜」。** 「天哪，众圣徒、众使徒、众先知啊，你们都要因她欢喜，因为神已经在她身上伸了你们的冤。」（18:20）地上的人远远地站着哭，天上却欢喜。这不是幸灾乐祸。祭坛底下的灵魂曾经呼求：「圣洁真实的主啊，你不审判住在地上的人，给我们伸流血的冤，要等到几时呢？」（6:10）现在神回答了。圣徒欢喜，是因为神的公义显明了，受冤屈的人得了伸冤，而不是因为他们自己报了仇。

**2. 磨石。** 「有一位大力的天使举起一块石头，好像大磨石，扔在海里，说：巴比伦大城也必这样猛力地被扔下去，决不能再见了。」（18:21）这个动作来自耶利米书：耶利米吩咐人把一块石头拴在写着巴比伦灾祸的书上，扔在幼发拉底河中，说「巴比伦……必如此沉下去，不再兴起」（耶51:63-64）。大磨石沉进海里，就再也浮不上来了。

**3. 城里的声音都止息了。** 天使一连说了六次「决不能再」（18:21-23）：琴声、笛声、号声不再听见，手艺人不再遇见，推磨的声音不再听见，灯光不再照耀，「新郎和新妇的声音，在你中间决不能再听见」（18:23）。这几乎是照着耶利米书说的：神要使「新郎和新妇的声音，推磨的声音和灯的亮光」从他们中间止息（耶25:10）。音乐、工作、做饭、灯光、婚礼，这些本来都是神所赐的好东西，巴比伦却拿来成全自己的骄傲，最后都失去了。可是新郎和新妇的声音并没有从世界上消失：下一章，天上就宣告「羔羊婚娶的时候到了」（19:7）。

**4. 两个原因。** 天使最后说明这城受审判的原因（18:23-24）：
- **「你的客商原来是地上的尊贵人；万国也被你的邪术迷惑了」：** 「邪术」（*pharmakeia*）原指用药物和法术迷惑人。她的繁华本身就像迷药。
- **「先知和圣徒，并地上一切被杀之人的血，都在这城里看见了」：** 不只是圣徒的血，还有「地上一切被杀之人的血」。巴比伦要为她历世历代所代表的一切暴力和欺压交账。

> 要点：神必为一切被欺压、被杀害的人伸冤。巴比伦的声音要止息，羔羊婚筵的声音却要响起来。

## 默想

**默想：** 「我又听见从天上有声音说：我的民哪，你们要从那城出来，免得与她一同有罪」（18:4）。一位弟兄在一家公司做部门主管。公司的口号是「拼搏」，晚上十点下班算早的，周末来公司也不算加班。上面给他的指标每季都往上加，他就把压力一层层压到下面。他手下有个刚毕业的年轻人，连续几个月每天只睡五个钟头，有一天在工位上晕倒，被送进医院。那天晚上开会，他想的却是：这个项目的进度要找谁来补。他在报表上写的是人力成本和人效，在他的电脑里，每一个同事都只是一行数字。他自己也一样。老板对他说过：「你不干，有的是人干。」他心里最怕的，就是有一天自己也成了那个随时可以被换掉的人。他越怕被当作成本，就越把别人当作成本。主日他坐在礼拜堂里，唱着爱主的诗歌，周一早上又回到那座城里去。

读到第18章那张货单，他停在最后一项：「奴仆、人口」。巴比伦把人列在牛、羊、车、马后面，客商为货物哭，却没有人为那些人哭。他忽然看见，他所在的地方也有这样一张单子，而他自己既在单子上，也在写这张单子。

可是在说「出来」以前，神先叫了一声：「我的民哪」。神在呼召以前，先认了他们。在巴比伦的账本上，那个晕倒的年轻人是一项成本，这位主管也是一项成本；神却不是这样看人。神从前在埃及就是这样说的：「我的百姓在埃及所受的困苦，我实在看见了；他们因受督工的辖制所发的哀声，我也听见了。」（出3:7）他看见那些被当作货物的人，他记得巴比伦的一切不义（18:5）。

如果我们像这位主管一样，曾经把人当作数字，甚至在别人倒下的时候只想到进度，这是真实的罪，不是一句「行业就是这样」可以带过的。可是神没有说，这样的人永远不配被称为「我的民」。凡悔改、信靠基督的，罪都得赦免，就属于他。他呼召我们出来，是要我们离开她的罪，不是要我们先把自己洗干净，才配作他的民。这位弟兄的「出来」，不一定是第二天就辞职，虽然有时候确实需要离开。可能是到医院去看那个年轻人，向他道歉；也可能是把他的活儿减下来，下一次上面再压指标的时候，不再原样压给手下的人，而是在会上说一句：「这个数字，我们做不到。」如果你自己就是被压榨的人，「出来」也可以包括寻求劳动法的保护，或者为了健康和家庭离开一个吞噬人的地方；这不是不忠心，也不是不能吃苦。主耶稣问过：「人就是赚得全世界，赔上自己的生命，有什么益处呢？」（可8:36）巴比伦给我们全世界的货物，却要我们的魂。

主耶稣自己也曾被人估价。犹大问祭司长：「我把他交给你们，你们愿意给我多少钱？」他们就给了他三十块钱（太26:15）。按照摩西的律法，这正是一个奴仆的价钱（出21:32）。那位本有神形象的，「取了奴仆的形象」（腓2:7），被人用一个奴仆的价钱卖了。他被钉在十字架上，「义的代替不义的」（彼前3:18），担当了我们把人当货物的罪。因此，被当作货物的人，和把人当作货物的人，都可以因信成为神的百姓。这不是说那个年轻人受的苦可以一笔勾销：神记得（18:5），十字架也正是神对这种罪的审判，并没有轻轻放过。巴比伦要倾倒，她的账本要被烧尽，她城里的琴声和婚礼的声音都要止息；羔羊的婚筵却要开席，神的这一句话也不会改变。今天他仍然在那座城里呼唤，不叫他们人口，也不叫他们成本，而是叫：「我的民哪」。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 17 showed John the great prostitute riding the beast, "the great city that has dominion over the kings of the earth" (17:18); chapter 18 describes that city's fall. Almost the whole chapter is poetry, part funeral lament and part prophetic oracle of judgment. Most of John's language comes from the Old Testament prophets' oracles against Babylon (Isa 13; 21; 47; Jer 50–51) and against Tyre (Ezek 26–28). The chapter falls into five parts:
- **18:1–3, the announcement:** an angel with great authority comes down from heaven and cries with a mighty voice, "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!"
- **18:4–8, "Come out of her":** a voice from heaven calls God's people out of her sins and gives the reasons for her judgment.
- **18:9–19, three laments:** the kings of the earth, the merchants, and the shipmasters and sailors all "stand far off" and weep over her.
- **18:20, joy in heaven:** saints, apostles and prophets are to rejoice, because God has given judgment for them.
- **18:21–24, the millstone:** a mighty angel throws a great millstone into the sea and announces that the city will fall silent forever.

Chapter 17 has already set out five main views of what Babylon is:
- **First-century Rome:** the view of most interpreters.
- **Apostate Jerusalem:** the view of some preterists.
- **Papal Rome:** the view of many Reformation-era historicists.
- **A rebuilt city of Babylon in the future:** some dispensationalists expect ancient Babylon to be rebuilt in the last days; others take it as an end-time world system.
- **Every world system opposed to God:** idealists (e.g. William Hendriksen) see Babylon as the world in every age that lures people from God by pleasure, wealth and power.

One addition here: some dispensationalists (e.g. John Walvoord) separate the two chapters, reading chapter 17 as religious Babylon and chapter 18 as political and economic Babylon.

With most interpreters, this study takes chapters 17 and 18 as the same city, since both call her "the great city" (17:18; 18:10), both describe the same dress (17:4; 18:16), and both speak of "one hour" (17:12; 18:10). She is the Rome John could see, and also the "great city" of every age that exalts itself and uses wealth and power to draw the nations away from God.

## Going Deeper: 18:1–3 Fallen, Fallen

**1. "The earth was made bright with his glory."** "After this I saw another angel coming down from heaven, having great authority, and the earth was made bright with his glory" (18:1). When Ezekiel saw the glory of God return to the temple, he said, "the earth shone with his glory" (Ezek 43:2). Babylon adorned herself with gold and jewels and pearls (17:4); when the angel comes, her splendor is outshone.

**2. Already fallen.** "And he called out with a mighty voice, 'Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!'" (18:2). The cry has already been heard once (14:8), and it comes from Isaiah: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon; and all the carved images of her gods he has shattered to the ground" (Isa 21:9). "Fallen" is an aorist, and many interpreters take it as prophetic idiom: something still future is spoken of as already done, because what God has decreed will surely happen. To the small churches of Asia, Rome looked as if it could never fall. The angel says that in God's sight it has already fallen.

**3. Three sins.** "For all nations have drunk the wine of the passion of her sexual immorality, and the kings of the earth have committed immorality with her, and the merchants of the earth have grown rich from the power of her luxurious living" (18:3).
- **The nations drank her wine, and the kings committed immorality with her:** in the prophets "immorality" often means idolatry and unfaithfulness to God (Hos 2:2–5). Rome bound the imperial cult and the patron gods of its cities to its prosperity, and nations and kings bowed before her idols to share her power and plenty.
- **The merchants grew rich:** this chapter pays close attention to economics. In an essay on chapter 18 (in *The Climax of Prophecy*), Bauckham shows how much of John's critique of Rome is economic: the wealth of the whole empire flowed into one city for that city's enjoyment.
Babylon's sin is not only moral excess but a whole way of life that builds wealth, security and identity on something other than God.

> The point: the city that looks strongest and richest to us has already fallen in God's sight. God's people need not be dazzled by her splendor or cowed by her power.

## Going Deeper: 18:4–8 "Come Out of Her, My People"

**1. "My people."** "Then I heard another voice from heaven saying, 'Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins, lest you share in her plagues'" (18:4). This is what the prophets said again and again to Israel in exile: "Go out of the midst of her, my people! Let every one save his life from the fierce anger of the LORD!" (Jer 51:45; see Isa 48:20; 52:11). Paul uses the same words: "go out from their midst, and be separate from them" (2 Cor 6:17). Notice that before God says "come out," he says "my people." They are still living in Babylon, and God already owns them as his.

**2. What does "come out" mean?** Interpreters hold several views:
- **Separate from her sins in the way you live:** many (e.g. Bauckham, Beale) take it not as a call to leave the city of Rome but to stop sharing in her idolatry, greed and oppression, "lest you take part in her sins."
- **Literal flight from a city:** dispensationalists who take Babylon as a future, literal city read it as believers at the end physically leaving. Preterists who read Babylon as Jerusalem often recall Eusebius' account that the Jerusalem church fled to Pella before the siege (*Church History* 3.5.3).
Both readings agree that God's people must not be complicit with Babylon. But "come out" does not mean withdrawing from society into the hills. The same God told the exiles, "seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf" (Jer 29:7). Believers live in the city, pray for it and work in it; they simply stop sharing her sins and stop giving her their hearts.

**3. "God has remembered her iniquities."** "For her sins are heaped high as heaven, and God has remembered her iniquities" (18:5). Her sins have "clung together" or piled up as high as heaven. The words come from Jeremiah's oracle against Babylon, "her judgment has reached up to heaven and has been lifted up even to the skies" (Jer 51:9); some also hear an echo of Babel's "tower with its top in the heavens" (Gen 11:4). God "remembering" does not mean he had forgotten; it means the time has come for him to act in judgment (16:19). God remembers the people who suffered unnoticed in that empire.

**4. "Repay her double."** "Pay her back as she herself has paid back others, and repay her double for her deeds; mix a double portion for her in the cup she mixed" (18:6).
- **Who is addressed?** The saints are not being told to take revenge. Some think the command is to the angels who carry out judgment; others to the beast and the ten horns, who will "burn her up with fire" (17:16). On either view, vengeance belongs to God (19:2; Rom 12:19).
- **"Double":** many take it as an Old Testament idiom for full, sufficient recompense (Jer 16:18; see Isa 40:2). Some scholars (e.g. Beale, following Meredith Kline) lean toward reading the word here as an "equivalent" or "matching" payment, as the first line says: "as she herself has paid back others."
On either reading, God's judgment is not arbitrary; it is "for her deeds."

**5. "I sit as a queen."** "As she glorified herself and lived in luxury, so give her a like measure of torment and mourning, since in her heart she says, 'I sit as a queen, I am no widow, and mourning I shall never see'" (18:7). These words come almost directly from Isaiah, where ancient Babylon says, "I shall be mistress forever," and "I shall not sit as a widow or know the loss of children" (Isa 47:7–8). Her root sin is pride: she believes she is secure forever and has no need of God. So "her plagues will come in a single day," "for mighty is the Lord God who has judged her" (18:8).

> The point: before God says "come out," he says "my people." He calls those who belong to him to live in the middle of Babylon without sharing her sins.

## Going Deeper: 18:9–19 Weeping from Far Off

**1. Three groups of mourners.** First the kings of the earth (18:9–10), then the merchants (18:11–17a), and last the shipmasters, sailors and all whose trade is on the sea (18:17b–19). The pattern of the three laments comes from Ezekiel's lament over Tyre, where Tyre's merchants, mariners and pilots wail over her and throw dust on their heads (Ezek 27:27–36). In each they "stand far off, in fear of her torment" (18:10, 15), cry "Alas, alas," and say that judgment came "in a single hour" (18:10, 17, 19). They shared her luxury but will not share her suffering; wealth gathered over centuries is gone in an hour.

**2. What are they weeping for?** The merchants weep "since no one buys their cargo anymore" (18:11). The shipmasters and sailors weep because "all who had ships at sea grew rich by her wealth" (18:19), and the money has stopped. Not one of them weeps for the people of the city or for her sins. They weep for their business. The sailors cry, "What city was like the great city?" (18:18), which echoes the world's worship of the beast: "Who is like the beast, and who can fight against it?" (13:4).

**3. A cargo list.** John lists the merchants' cargo item by item (18:12–13), more than twenty items in all: from gold, jewels, silk and spices to cattle, horses and chariots, and last of all "slaves, that is, human souls." Bauckham shows that many items on this list were luxury imports brought into Rome from every part of the empire and as far as the East. The luxury of one city was fed by the whole world.

**4. "Slaves, that is, human souls."** The last two words on the list are the most chilling. "Slaves" is literally "bodies" (*sōmata*), a word the trade documents of the day often used for slaves; "human souls" is literally "souls of men" (*psychas anthrōpōn*), the very phrase of the Greek Old Testament at Ezek 27:13. The phrase can be read two ways:
- **One thing named twice:** "slaves, that is, human souls," as the ESV translates. The point is that those bought and sold as "bodies" are in fact living human souls.
- **Two groups:** "slaves" are those already enslaved; "human souls" may mean others captured and trafficked.
On either reading, John deliberately places people after cattle, sheep, horses and chariots, as though they were one more kind of cargo. Ezekiel too says that Tyre traded "human beings and vessels of bronze" (Ezek 27:13). Rome's economy depended heavily on slave labor; the human trade was an indispensable part of the city's wealth. Here is Babylon's deepest sin: she treats human souls as merchandise.

**5. Everything lost.** "The fruit for which your soul longed has gone from you, and all your delicacies and your splendors are lost to you, never to be found again!" (18:14). The city was clothed in fine linen, purple and scarlet (18:16), dressed like the prostitute of chapter 17 (17:4). In chapter 19 we will see another woman's clothing: the Bride of the Lamb is given "fine linen, bright and pure" (19:8).

> The point: Babylon's merchants weep for lost cargo but not for the people who were treated as cargo. A civilization that lists human souls at the end of its cargo manifest, however splendid, has already been weighed by God and found wanting (Dan 5:27).

## Going Deeper: 18:20–24 A Millstone Thrown into the Sea

**1. "Rejoice over her."** "Rejoice over her, O heaven, and you saints and apostles and prophets, for God has given judgment for you against her!" (18:20). On earth they stand far off and weep; in heaven there is joy. This is not gloating. The souls under the altar cried, "O Sovereign Lord, holy and true, how long before you will judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" (6:10). Now God has answered. The saints rejoice because God's justice has been shown and the wronged have been vindicated, not because they have taken revenge themselves.

**2. The millstone.** "Then a mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone and threw it into the sea, saying, 'So will Babylon the great city be thrown down with violence, and will be found no more'" (18:21). The action comes from Jeremiah, who told a messenger to tie a stone to the scroll of Babylon's doom, throw it into the Euphrates and say, "Thus shall Babylon sink, to rise no more" (Jer 51:63–64). A great millstone sunk in the sea never comes up again.

**3. The sounds of the city fall silent.** Six times the angel says "no more" (18:21–23): no more harp, flute or trumpet, no more craftsmen, no more sound of the mill, no more lamplight, and "the voice of bridegroom and bride will be heard in you no more" (18:23). This follows Jeremiah closely: God will banish "the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride, the grinding of the millstones and the light of the lamp" (Jer 25:10). Music, work, cooking, light and weddings are all good gifts of God; Babylon made them serve her pride, and in the end lost them all. Yet the voice of bridegroom and bride does not vanish from the world: in the very next chapter heaven announces, "the marriage of the Lamb has come" (19:7).

**4. Two reasons.** The angel ends by giving the reasons for the city's judgment (18:23–24):
- **"Your merchants were the great ones of the earth, and all nations were deceived by your sorcery":** "sorcery" (*pharmakeia*) first meant bewitching people with drugs and spells. Her splendor itself works like a drug.
- **"In her was found the blood of prophets and of saints, and of all who have been slain on earth":** not only the blood of the saints but of "all who have been slain on earth." Babylon must answer for all the violence and oppression she has stood for in every age.

> The point: God will vindicate everyone who has been oppressed and killed. Babylon's sounds will fall silent, but the sound of the Lamb's marriage feast is about to begin.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "Then I heard another voice from heaven saying, 'Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins'" (18:4). A brother manages a department in a company. The company's slogan is about "fighting hard"; leaving at ten at night counts as early, and coming in at weekends does not count as overtime. Every quarter the targets passed down to him rise, and he presses the pressure down, layer by layer, onto those below him. One of his team, a young man just out of university, has slept five hours a night for months; one day he collapses at his desk and is taken to hospital. In that evening's meeting, what the manager thinks about is who can cover the project's schedule. In his reports he writes "labor costs" and "output per head"; on his computer, every colleague is a line of figures. He is one too: his own boss once told him, "If you won't do it, plenty of others will." What he fears most is becoming the one who can be replaced at any moment. The more he fears being treated as a cost, the more he treats others as costs. On Sunday he sits in church and sings "Lord, I love you"; on Monday morning he goes back into that city.

Reading the cargo list of chapter 18, he stops at the last item: "slaves, that is, human souls." Babylon lists people after cattle, sheep, horses and chariots; the merchants weep for their cargo, and no one weeps for the people. He suddenly sees that his workplace has a list like this too, and that he is both on it and writing it.

But before God says "come out," he first calls to them: "my people." Before God calls, he claims them. In Babylon's ledger, the young man who collapsed is a cost, and so is the manager; but God does not see people that way. This is how God spoke in Egypt: "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters" (Exod 3:7). He sees those treated as cargo; he remembers all Babylon's iniquities (18:5).

If, like this manager, we have treated people as figures, and even thought only of schedules when someone fell, that is real sin, not something to wave away with "that's just the industry." But God has not said that such a person can never be called "my people." Everyone who repents and trusts in Christ is forgiven and belongs to him. He calls us out of her sins; he does not require us to wash ourselves first before we may be his people. For this brother, "coming out" may not mean resigning the next day, though sometimes leaving is what is needed. It may mean going to the hospital to visit the young man and apologizing to him, or it may mean lightening his load, and the next time targets come down from above, not pressing them straight onto his team but saying in the meeting, "We cannot do this number." If you are yourself the one being squeezed, "coming out" may also mean seeking the protection of labor law, or leaving a place that devours people for the sake of health and family; that is not unfaithfulness or a failure to endure hardship. Jesus asked, "What does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his life?" (Mark 8:36). Babylon offers us the whole world's cargo and asks for our souls.

Jesus himself was once given a price. Judas asked the chief priests, "What will you give me if I deliver him over to you?" and they paid him thirty pieces of silver (Matt 26:15). Under the law of Moses, that was the price of a slave (Exod 21:32). The one who was in the form of God took "the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7), was sold for the price of a slave. He was nailed to a cross, "the righteous for the unrighteous" (1 Pet 3:18), bearing the sins of people like us who treat others as cargo. So both those treated as cargo and those who treated them so can become, through faith, God's own people. This does not mean the young man's suffering is simply written off: God remembers (18:5), and the cross is God's judgment on this very sin, not a way of letting it pass. Babylon will fall and her ledgers will burn; her harps and wedding songs will fall silent, while the Lamb's wedding feast begins; and this word of God will not change. Today he is still calling in that city, not "human souls," not "costs," but "my people."
