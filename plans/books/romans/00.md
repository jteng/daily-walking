## 这卷书在讲什么

罗马书是保罗写给罗马教会的一封信，也是新约中对福音最完整、最有系统的阐述。马丁·路德说它是「新约中最主要的部分，是最纯正的福音」；奥古斯丁、路德、卫斯理的悔改归主，都和这卷书有关。可是罗马书不是一本抽象的神学教科书，而是一封写给真实教会的信：一群犹太和外邦信徒，在帝国的首都，学习在同一位主里合而为一。

全书的主题，保罗在开头就说明了：「我不以福音为耻；这福音本是神的大能，要救一切相信的，先是犹太人，后是希腊人。因为神的义正在这福音上显明出来；这义是本于信，以至于信。」（1:16-17）整封信，就是在展开这两节经文。

## 作者与写作背景

**1. 作者。** 保罗在信的开头自称「耶稣基督的仆人保罗，奉召为使徒，特派传神的福音」（1:1）。全书由一位名叫德提的人代笔（16:22）。

**2. 时间和地点。** 保罗写这封信时，正要把马其顿和亚该亚教会的捐项送到耶路撒冷（15:25-26），时间大约是主后57年，在他第三次宣教旅程的末了，很可能是在哥林多（参徒20:2-3）。信中提到的「坚革哩教会」（16:1）是哥林多的港口，接待保罗的该犹（16:23）也很可能是哥林多人（林前1:14）。

**3. 罗马的教会。** 保罗写信时还没有去过罗马。这个教会不是使徒建立的，可能是由五旬节在耶路撒冷信主、回到罗马的犹太人和进教的人开始的（徒2:10）。主后49年左右，皇帝克劳第把犹太人赶出罗马（徒18:2），几年后他们才陆续回来。许多学者认为，这期间教会大概由外邦信徒带领；等犹太信徒回来，两群人之间难免产生摩擦。第十四至十五章关于「软弱的」和「刚强的」的劝勉，很可能与这个处境有关。

**4. 写信的目的。** 保罗至少有三个目的：一，预备访问罗马，并请他们支持他往西班牙去宣教（15:23-24）；二，把他所传的福音完整地讲明，因为他常被误解和毁谤（3:8）；三，帮助犹太和外邦信徒在福音里彼此接纳（15:7）。

## 钥匙经文：1:16-17

**1. 「我不以福音为耻」。** 在罗马这个以权力和荣耀为傲的城市，传讲一个被钉十字架的犹太人，简直是笑话。保罗却说，这福音「本是神的大能」。

**2. 「要救一切相信的」。** 救恩是给「一切相信的」，不分犹太人和外邦人。这是全书一再出现的「一切」和「都」：众人都犯了罪（3:23），信的人都可以称义（3:22）。

**3. 「神的义」。** 这是全书的关键词。它既指神自己公义的属性，也指神白白赐给信的人的义：信的人因此在神面前被算为义（3:21-26）。路德说，他明白这节经文的那一刻，「我觉得自己完全重生了，好像穿过敞开的门，进了乐园」。

**4. 「义人必因信得生」。** 保罗引用哈巴谷书2:4。人在神面前得以称义，不是靠行律法，而是本于信，以至于信。

## 全书结构

罗马书的论证，大致可以分成四个部分：
- **神的义与人的罪**（1–4章）：全人类，无论外邦人或犹太人，都在罪中，都需要神的义；这义藉着信耶稣基督白白赐下，正如亚伯拉罕因信称义。
- **新生命与盼望**（5–8章）：因信称义的人，与神相和，脱离罪和律法的辖制，靠圣灵而活，并且任何受造之物「都不能叫我们与神的爱隔绝」（8:39）。
- **神与以色列**（9–11章）：如果神的应许是给以色列的，为什么许多以色列人不信？神的话落空了吗？保罗讲论神的拣选、以色列的责任，以及神对以色列最终的怜悯。
- **福音的生活**（12–16章）：「所以，弟兄们，我以神的慈悲劝你们，将身体献上」（12:1）。在教会、社会和彼此接纳中活出福音。

## 怎样读这卷书

**1. 跟着论证读。** 罗马书不像福音书或使徒行传那样讲故事，而是一步一步地论证。读的时候要留意连接词：「因为」「所以」「这样」「但如今」「既是这样」。保罗常常先提出一个问题（「这样，我们可说什么呢？」），然后回答。

**2. 留意旧约。** 罗马书引用旧约的次数，比保罗其他书信都多。保罗要证明，他所传的福音不是新发明，而是「神从前藉众先知在圣经上所应许的」（1:2）。

**3. 不要只停在教义上。** 第一至十一章讲神为我们做了什么，第十二至十六章讲我们因此怎样活。教义带出敬拜（11:33-36），敬拜带出生活（12:1-2）。读罗马书，最终是要叫我们赞美神。

## 默想

**默想：** 保罗说：「我不以福音为耻。」在一个看重成就和面子的环境里，有没有哪一部分的福音是我觉得难以启齿的：在不信主的父母面前说，他们也是需要救恩的罪人，或者人得救全靠恩典，不靠自己的好行为？这卷书要一章一章地告诉我，这福音为什么不是羞耻，而是「神的大能」。在开始读罗马书以前，我能不能先求神，让我不只是明白一套道理，而是重新被那位为我死、为我复活的主所吸引？保罗讲完前十一章的大论证，不是下一个冷冰冰的结论，而是发出敬拜：「深哉，神丰富的智慧和知识！」（11:33）愿这也是我读完这卷书时的回应。

## What This Book Is About

Romans is Paul's letter to the church in Rome, and the New Testament's fullest and most systematic exposition of the gospel. Martin Luther called it "the chief part of the New Testament and the very purest gospel"; the conversions of Augustine, Luther and John Wesley are all bound up with it. Yet Romans is not an abstract theology textbook but a letter to a real church: Jewish and Gentile believers in the empire's capital, learning to be one in the same Lord.

Paul states the theme at the start: "For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith" (1:16–17). The whole letter unfolds these two verses.

## Author and Setting

**1. Author.** Paul introduces himself as "a servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle, set apart for the gospel of God" (1:1). The letter was written down by a man named Tertius (16:22).

**2. Date and place.** When Paul wrote, he was about to take the gift of the churches of Macedonia and Achaia to Jerusalem (15:25–26), around AD 57, near the end of his third missionary journey, very likely from Corinth (cf. Acts 20:2–3). He mentions "the church at Cenchreae" (16:1), Corinth's port, and his host Gaius (16:23) was probably a Corinthian (1 Cor 1:14).

**3. The church in Rome.** Paul had not yet been to Rome. The church there wasn't founded by an apostle; it may have begun with Jews and proselytes who came to faith in Jerusalem at Pentecost and returned home (Acts 2:10). Around AD 49 the emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2), and they returned only some years later. Many scholars think Gentile believers may have led the church in the meantime, and that tension followed when Jewish believers returned. The counsel about "the weak" and "the strong" in chapters 14–15 may well reflect that situation.

**4. Purpose.** Paul had at least three aims: to prepare for his visit to Rome and ask the church's support for his mission to Spain (15:23–24); to set out the gospel he preached in full, since he was often misunderstood and slandered (3:8); and to help Jewish and Gentile believers welcome one another in the gospel (15:7).

## Key Verses: 1:16–17

**1. "I am not ashamed of the gospel."** In Rome, a city proud of power and glory, a crucified Jew was a joke. Yet Paul says this gospel is "the power of God."

**2. "To everyone who believes."** Salvation is for "everyone who believes," Jew and Gentile alike. "All" and "everyone" run through the whole letter: all have sinned (3:23), and all who believe may be justified (3:22).

**3. "The righteousness of God."** This is the letter's key phrase. It is both God's own righteous character and the standing God freely gives to those who believe, by which they are counted righteous before him (3:21–26). Luther said that when he understood this verse, "I felt that I was altogether born again and had entered paradise itself through open gates."

**4. "The righteous shall live by faith."** Paul quotes Habakkuk 2:4. A person is put right with God not by works of the law, but by faith from first to last.

## Structure

Paul's argument falls broadly into four parts:
- **God's righteousness and human sin** (chs. 1–4): all humanity, Gentile and Jew, is under sin and needs God's righteousness, which is freely given through faith in Jesus Christ, just as Abraham was justified by faith.
- **New life and hope** (chs. 5–8): those justified by faith have peace with God, are freed from the reign of sin and law, live by the Spirit, and nothing "will be able to separate us from the love of God" (8:39).
- **God and Israel** (chs. 9–11): if God's promises were given to Israel, why do so many Israelites not believe? Has God's word failed? Paul speaks of God's election, Israel's responsibility and God's final mercy to Israel.
- **Living the gospel** (chs. 12–16): "I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies" (12:1), living out the gospel in the church, in society and in welcoming one another.

## How to Read This Book

**1. Follow the argument.** Romans doesn't tell a story like the Gospels or Acts; it argues step by step. Watch the connecting words: "for," "therefore," "so," "but now," "then." Paul often raises a question ("What then shall we say?") and answers it.

**2. Watch the Old Testament.** Romans quotes the Old Testament more than any other letter of Paul's. Paul wants to show that his gospel is no new invention but what God "promised beforehand through his prophets in the holy Scriptures" (1:2).

**3. Don't stop at doctrine.** Chapters 1–11 tell what God has done for us; chapters 12–16 tell how we therefore live. Doctrine leads to worship (11:33–36), and worship leads to life (12:1–2). In the end, reading Romans is meant to make us praise God.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Paul said, "I am not ashamed of the gospel." In a world that prizes achievement and face, is there some part of the gospel I find hard to say aloud: telling unbelieving parents that they too are sinners who need saving, or that salvation is all of grace and not of our good works? Chapter by chapter, this book will show me why the gospel is not something to be ashamed of but "the power of God." Before I begin reading Romans, can I ask God to keep me from merely mastering a set of ideas, and to draw me afresh to the Lord who died and rose for me? At the end of his great argument Paul doesn't draw a conclusion; he worships: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!" (11:33). May that be my response when I finish this book too.
