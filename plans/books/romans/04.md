## 本章脉络

3:28说：「人称义是因着信，不在乎遵行律法。」犹太读者一定会问：那么亚伯拉罕呢？犹太人的祖宗，岂不是因为顺服神才蒙悦纳的吗？第四章就用亚伯拉罕作例证，证明因信称义不是保罗的新发明，而是律法书本身的见证（3:21，31）。

本章分四段。**不是因行为**（1-8节）：「亚伯拉罕信神，这就算为他的义」（4:3，引创15:6）；大卫也称「主不算为有罪的」人是有福的。**不是因割礼**（9-12节）：亚伯拉罕称义在受割礼以前，所以他是一切信之人的父，不论受割礼与否。**不是因律法**（13-17节）：应许是「因信而得的义」，所以「就属乎恩」。**信心的样式**（17-25节）：亚伯拉罕所信的，是「那叫死人复活、使无变为有的神」；我们所信的，是使耶稣从死里复活的神。

## 深读 4:1-8 算为义

**1. 亚伯拉罕有可夸的吗？** 「倘若亚伯拉罕是因行为称义，就有可夸的；只是在神面前并无可夸。」（4:2）当时一些犹太教导，强调亚伯拉罕的顺服，特别是献以撒（创22章），认为他因此蒙神悦纳。保罗却回到更早的一节经文。（雅各书2:21-23也引用了这些经文，雅各讲的是用行为证明信心是真的，而不是称义的根据。）

**2. 「算为他的义」。** 「经上说什么呢？说：『亚伯拉罕信神，这就算为他的义。』」（4:3；引创15:6）「算」（原文 *logizomai*）可以用在记账上（如4:4），意思是记在某人的账上；七十士译本在创15:6就用这个字。这个字在第四章出现十一次。亚伯拉罕的义，不是他自己挣来的，而是神「算」给他的。要留意：信心本身不是一种代替行为的功劳；保罗说神是「在行为以外」把「义」算给人（4:6，11）。信心只是空手，领受神所算给的义。

**3. 工价与恩典。** 「做工的得工价，不算恩典，乃是该得的；惟有不做工的，只信称罪人为义的神，他的信就算为义。」（4:4-5）这是本章最令人惊讶的一句话。「称罪人为义」，原文是「称不敬虔的人为义」。旧约要求审判官不可「定恶人为义」（箴17:15），神自己也说：「我必不以恶人为义」（出23:7）。那么，神怎能「称不敬虔的人为义」，而不违背自己的公义呢？答案在3:25-26：神设立耶稣作挽回祭，基督在十字架上担当了他们罪的刑罚，所以神称信的人为义，「使人知道他自己为义」。神不是对罪视而不见，而是在基督身上审判了罪。神所称为义的，不是已经变好的人，而是「不敬虔的人」。这就是福音的好消息：我们不需要先把自己变好，才能来到神面前。

**4. 大卫的见证。** 「得赦免其过、遮盖其罪的，这人是有福的。主不算为有罪的，这人是有福的。」（4:7-8；引诗32:1-2）大卫犯了奸淫和谋杀，正是一个「不敬虔的人」，却经历了神的赦免。这里有一个美丽的对照：神把义「算」给信的人，却不把罪「算」在他们身上。

> 要点：神所称为义的，是「不敬虔的人」。称义不是工价，而是恩典：我们不必先变好才能来到神面前，而是空着手来，领受神白白算给我们的义。

## 深读 4:9-12 割礼以前

**1. 时间的先后。** 「是怎么算的呢？是在他受割礼的时候呢？是在他未受割礼的时候呢？不是在受割礼的时候，乃是在未受割礼的时候。」（4:10）创世记十五章亚伯拉罕因信称义，到十七章他才受割礼，中间相隔至少十三年（参创16:16；17:24）。

**2. 记号与印证。** 「并且他受了割礼的记号，作他未受割礼的时候因信称义的印证。」（4:11）割礼不是称义的原因，而是称义的「印证」，像盖在文件上的印章，证明里面所写的是真实的。许多信徒（特别是改革宗传统）从这里看到圣礼的意义（参西2:11-12）：洗礼和圣餐是主所命定的恩典的记号和印证，用来坚固我们的信心；它们是必须遵守的，却不是我们称义的根据。

**3. 一切信之人的父。** 这样，亚伯拉罕就「作一切未受割礼而信之人的父」，也「作受割礼之人的父」，就是那些「按我们的祖宗亚伯拉罕未受割礼而信之踪迹去行的人」（4:11-12）。在神的家里，血统不能叫人成为亚伯拉罕的儿女，只有信才能。

> 要点：亚伯拉罕在受割礼以前就已经称义，所以宗教的仪式不能叫人称义；它只是印证神已经藉着信所赐下的义。

## 深读 4:13-17 本乎信，属乎恩

**1. 承受世界。** 「因为神应许亚伯拉罕和他后裔，必得承受世界，不是因律法，乃是因信而得的义。」（4:13）神原来应许亚伯拉罕的是迦南地（创12:7；15:18），也应许地上的万族要因他得福（创12:3）。这应许从一开始就是普世的：亚伯拉罕要「作多国的父」（创17:4-6），他的后裔要得着仇敌的城门（创22:17-18），诗篇也盼望那位王「执掌权柄，从这海直到那海」（诗72:8）。这应许在基督，就是那「一个子孙」，以及一切属基督的人身上得着应验（加3:16，29）；神的应许最终是要更新整个受造界（参8:19-21）。

**2. 律法惹动忿怒。** 「若是属乎律法的人才得为后嗣，信就归于虚空，应许也就废弃了。因为律法是惹动忿怒的。」（4:14-15）如果应许要靠遵守律法才能得着，没有人能得着，因为人人都违背了律法。

**3. 「本乎信，因此就属乎恩」。** 「所以人得为后嗣是本乎信，因此就属乎恩，叫应许定然归给一切后裔。」（4:16）信和恩典是连在一起的：正因为是靠信，所以是恩典；正因为是恩典，应许才「定然」，也就是确定、稳固。如果应许要靠我们，它就永远不会稳固；正因为它靠的是神，所以它是确定的。

> 要点：应许若靠我们的表现，就永远不会稳固；正因为它本乎信、属乎恩，靠的是神的信实，所以它是确定的。

## 深读 4:17-25 叫死人复活的神

**1. 亚伯拉罕所信的神。** 「亚伯拉罕所信的，是那叫死人复活、使无变为有的神。」（4:17）信心的力量，不在于信心本身，而在于所信的对象。亚伯拉罕的信心之所以伟大，是因为他所信的神，能从无中创造，能从死里带出生命。

**2. 无可指望中的指望。** 「他在无可指望的时候，因信仍有指望。」（4:18）这句话原文很有力：「在指望以外，仍在指望中相信。」按人的眼光，一切都已经没有指望；可是亚伯拉罕把眼光放在神的应许上。

**3. 正视事实的信心。** 「他将近百岁的时候，虽然想到自己的身体如同已死，撒拉的生育已经断绝，他的信心还是不软弱。」（4:19）信心不是闭上眼睛不看现实。亚伯拉罕很清楚地「想到」自己的身体如同已死，他没有假装事情不是这样；他只是相信，神比这个事实更大。

**4. 将荣耀归给神。** 「并且仰望神的应许，总没有因不信心里起疑惑，反倒因信心里得坚固，将荣耀归给神，且满心相信神所应许的必能做成。」（4:20-21）这不是说亚伯拉罕从来没有软弱：他曾两次说妻子是他的妹子（创12章；20章），也曾想靠夏甲生子（创16章），甚至在神应许时「就俯伏在地喜笑」（创17:17）。保罗描写的不是一份毫无瑕疵的记录，而是他信心的总方向：从整体来看，他的信心是一直望着神的。信心本身就是「将荣耀归给神」：它承认神是信实的，神是有能力的。这和1:21正好相反：在那里，人「不当作神荣耀他」；在这里，信心把荣耀归给神。

**5. 为我们写的。** 「『算为他义』的这句话不是单为他写的，也是为我们将来得算为义之人写的，就是我们这信神使我们的主耶稣从死里复活的人。」（4:23-24）亚伯拉罕信神能从「如同已死」的身体中带出生命；我们信神已经使耶稣从死里复活。我们的信心，和亚伯拉罕的信心，是同一种信心，所信的是同一位神。

**6. 被交与复活。** 「耶稣被交给人，是为我们的过犯；复活，是为叫我们称义。」（4:25）这句话很可能是初期教会的一句信仰宣告，回应以赛亚书53章的受苦仆人。「被交给人」是神亲自把他交出来（参8:32）。耶稣的死，是替我们担当了罪的刑罚；耶稣的复活，证明神已经接纳了他的牺牲，也宣告了我们的称义。

> 要点：信心的力量不在信心本身，而在所信的神：他叫死人复活，使无变为有。信心正视现实，却相信神比现实更大，这样的信心就是把荣耀归给神。

## 默想

**默想：** 「惟有不做工的，只信称罪人为义的神。」我是否仍然觉得，必须先把自己的生命整理好一点，神才会接纳我？保罗说，神所称为义的，正是「不敬虔的人」。这个好消息，我是只在信主的那一天需要，还是今天、在我又一次失败以后，也同样需要？再想想亚伯拉罕：他没有假装自己的身体不是「如同已死」，却仍然相信神。在我的生命中，有没有一件事，在人看来已经「无可指望」：一段破裂的关系、一个久治不愈的病、一个还没有信主的家人？我能不能像亚伯拉罕一样，诚实地面对现实，却仍把眼光放在那位「叫死人复活、使无变为有的神」身上？我的信心可能很软弱，可是我所信的神并不软弱。

## The Shape of the Chapter

Romans 3:28 says, "one is justified by faith apart from works of the law." A Jewish reader would immediately ask: what about Abraham? Wasn't the forefather of Israel accepted by God because he obeyed? Chapter 4 uses Abraham to prove that justification by faith is not Paul's invention but the testimony of the Law itself (3:21, 31).

The chapter has four parts. **Not by works** (vv. 1–8): "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness" (4:3, quoting Gen 15:6); David too calls blessed the one "against whom the Lord will not count his sin." **Not by circumcision** (vv. 9–12): Abraham was justified before he was circumcised, so he is the father of all who believe, circumcised or not. **Not by the law** (vv. 13–17): the promise came "through the righteousness of faith," so it "rests on grace." **The shape of faith** (vv. 17–25): Abraham believed the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist"; we believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead.

## Going Deeper: 4:1–8 Counted as Righteousness

**1. Did Abraham have something to boast of?** "For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God" (4:2). Some Jewish teaching of the time stressed Abraham's obedience, especially the offering of Isaac (Gen 22), as the reason God accepted him. Paul goes back to an earlier verse. (James 2:21–23 cites these texts too; James speaks of works showing that faith is genuine, not of the ground of justification.)

**2. "Counted to him as righteousness."** "For what does the Scripture say? 'Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness'" (4:3, quoting Gen 15:6). "Counted" (*logizomai*) is a word that can be used of keeping accounts (as in 4:4): to credit something to someone's account; it is the Septuagint's word in Gen 15:6. It appears eleven times in chapter 4. Abraham's righteousness was not something he earned but something God "counted" to him. Note that faith is not a substitute merit that God accepts in place of works; Paul says God counts righteousness "apart from works" (4:6, 11). Faith is the empty hand that receives it.

**3. Wages and grace.** "Now to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due. And to the one who does not work but trusts him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness" (4:4–5). This is the most astonishing sentence in the chapter. The Old Testament forbade judges to justify the wicked (Prov 17:15), and God said of himself, "I will not acquit the wicked" (Exod 23:7). How then can God "justify the ungodly" without betraying his own justice? The answer is 3:25–26: God put Christ forward as a propitiation, and Christ bore the penalty of their sin, so that God is "just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus." God does not overlook sin; he has judged it in Christ. The people God justifies are not those who have already improved, but "the ungodly." This is the good news of the gospel: we don't have to make ourselves better before we can come to God.

**4. David's testimony.** "Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, and whose sins are covered; blessed is the man against whom the Lord will not count his sin" (4:7–8, quoting Ps 32:1–2). David, an adulterer and murderer, was just such an "ungodly" man, and he knew God's forgiveness. There is a beautiful contrast: God "counts" righteousness to those who believe, and does not "count" their sin against them.

> The point: The people God justifies are "the ungodly." Justification is not a wage but a gift: we don't first become good and then come to God; we come with empty hands and receive the righteousness God freely credits to us.

## Going Deeper: 4:9–12 Before Circumcision

**1. Which came first.** "How then was it counted to him? Was it before or after he had been circumcised? It was not after, but before he was circumcised" (4:10). Abraham was justified by faith in Genesis 15 and circumcised only in Genesis 17, at least thirteen years later (cf. Gen 16:16; 17:24).

**2. Sign and seal.** "He received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised" (4:11). Circumcision was not the cause of his justification but its "seal," like the stamp on a document that certifies what is written in it. Many Christians, especially in the Reformed tradition, see here a pattern for the sacraments (cf. Col 2:11–12): baptism and the Lord's Supper are signs and seals of grace, commanded by Christ to strengthen faith. They are not optional, but they are not the ground of our justification.

**3. Father of all who believe.** So Abraham became "the father of all who believe without being circumcised," and "the father of the circumcised who are not merely circumcised but who also walk in the footsteps of the faith that our father Abraham had before he was circumcised" (4:11–12). In God's family, bloodline doesn't make anyone a child of Abraham; only faith does.

> The point: Abraham was justified before he was circumcised, so religious ritual cannot justify anyone; it only seals the righteousness God has already given through faith.

## Going Deeper: 4:13–17 By Faith, Resting on Grace

**1. Heir of the world.** "For the promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir of the world did not come through the law but through the righteousness of faith" (4:13). God first promised Abraham the land of Canaan (Gen 12:7; 15:18), and that all the families of the earth would be blessed in him (Gen 12:3). The promise had a worldwide horizon from the start: Abraham would be "the father of a multitude of nations" (Gen 17:4–6), his offspring would possess the gate of his enemies (Gen 22:17–18), and the Psalms look for a king who rules "from sea to sea" (Ps 72:8). It is fulfilled in Christ, the offspring, and in all who belong to him (Gal 3:16, 29), and finally in a renewed creation (cf. 8:19–21).

**2. The law brings wrath.** "For if it is the adherents of the law who are to be the heirs, faith is null and the promise is void. For the law brings wrath" (4:14–15). If the promise depended on keeping the law, no one would receive it, since everyone has broken the law.

**3. "It depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace."** "That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring" (4:16). Faith and grace belong together: because it is by faith, it is by grace; because it is by grace, the promise is "guaranteed," that is, sure and firm. If the promise depended on us, it would never be secure; because it depends on God, it is certain.

> The point: A promise resting on our performance would never be secure; because it depends on faith and rests on grace, on God's faithfulness, it is certain.

## Going Deeper: 4:17–25 The God Who Gives Life to the Dead

**1. The God Abraham believed.** Abraham believed the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17). The power of faith lies not in faith itself but in its object. Abraham's faith was great because the God he trusted can create out of nothing and bring life out of death.

**2. Hope against hope.** "In hope he believed against hope" (4:18); literally, "against hope, in hope he believed." By human reckoning there was nothing left to hope for; but Abraham fixed his eyes on God's promise.

**3. Faith that faces the facts.** "He did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, which was as good as dead (since he was about a hundred years old), or when he considered the barrenness of Sarah's womb" (4:19). Faith does not close its eyes to reality. Abraham clearly "considered" that his body was as good as dead; he did not pretend otherwise. He simply believed that God was bigger than the facts.

**4. Giving glory to God.** "No distrust made him waver concerning the promise of God, but he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God, fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised" (4:20–21). This doesn't mean Abraham was never weak: twice he passed off his wife as his sister (Gen 12; 20), he tried to gain a son through Hagar (Gen 16), and he even laughed at God's promise (Gen 17:17). Paul describes not a flawless record but the settled direction of his trust: taken as a whole, his faith kept looking to God. Faith itself "gives glory to God": it confesses that God is faithful and able. It is the exact reverse of 1:21: there people "did not honor him as God"; here faith gives him glory.

**5. Written for us.** "But the words 'it was counted to him' were not written for his sake alone, but for ours also. It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord" (4:23–24). Abraham believed God could bring life out of a body "as good as dead"; we believe God has raised Jesus from the dead. Our faith and Abraham's are the same kind of faith in the same God.

**6. Delivered up and raised.** "Who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (4:25). This may well be an early Christian confession echoing the suffering servant of Isaiah 53. "Delivered up" means that God himself handed him over (cf. 8:32). Jesus' death bore the penalty of our sins; his resurrection shows that God accepted his sacrifice and declares our justification.

> The point: The power of faith lies not in faith itself but in the God it trusts, who gives life to the dead and calls into being what does not exist. Faith faces reality yet trusts that God is greater, and such faith gives glory to God.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "To the one who does not work but trusts him who justifies the ungodly." Do I still feel I need to get my life in better order before God will accept me? Paul says the very people God justifies are "the ungodly." Is this good news something I needed only on the day I first believed, or do I need it just as much today, after yet another failure? Think too of Abraham: he did not pretend his body was not "as good as dead," yet he still believed God. Is there something in my life that looks "beyond hope": a broken relationship, a long illness, a family member who has not yet believed? Can I, like Abraham, face the facts honestly and still fix my eyes on the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist"? My faith may be weak, but the God I trust is not.
