## 本章脉络

第五章是罗马书的第二个大段落（5–8章）的开始。前四章讲人怎样称义；从这里开始，保罗讲称义的人得着了什么，以及他们的盼望为什么是稳固的。

本章分两部分。**称义的果效**（1-11节）：「我们既因信称义，就藉着我们的主耶稣基督得与神相和」；我们站在恩典中，在患难中也欢喜，因为神的爱已经浇灌在我们心里，而这爱的明证，是「基督在我们还作罪人的时候为我们死」。**亚当与基督**（12-21节）：罪和死藉着一个人进入世界，临到众人；义和生命也藉着一个人，耶稣基督，更加丰盛地临到一切属他的人。「罪在哪里显多，恩典就更显多了。」

第五至八章有一个首尾呼应：本章以「神的爱」开始（5:5，8），第八章以「神的爱」结束（8:39）。中间的一切，都被神的爱包围着。

## 深读 5:1-5 称义的人得着什么

**1. 与神相和。** 「我们既因信称义，就藉着我们的主耶稣基督得与神相和。」（5:1）「相和」不只是心里感到平安，而是我们和神之间的关系改变了：从前我们是「仇敌」（5:10），在神的忿怒之下（1:18）；如今战争已经结束。这和平是一个事实，不是一种感觉；即使我们感觉不到，它也仍然是真的。（不少重要的古抄本作「让我们与神相和吧」，原文两个字只差一个字母，当时读音几乎相同；可是多数学者和译本根据上下文，认为保罗是在陈述一个已经成就的事实。）

**2. 站在恩典中。** 「我们又藉着他，因信得进入现在所站的这恩典中。」（5:2）「进入」这个词，有被引见到君王面前的意思。我们不是偶尔进入恩典，又偶尔被赶出来；我们「站」在恩典中，这是我们的位置。

**3. 盼望神的荣耀。** 「并且欢欢喜喜盼望神的荣耀。」（5:2）人「亏缺了神的荣耀」（3:23）；可是称义的人，盼望有一天要分享神的荣耀（8:18，30）。

**4. 在患难中也欢喜。** 「不但如此，就是在患难中也是欢欢喜喜的；因为知道患难生忍耐，忍耐生老练，老练生盼望。」（5:3-4）保罗不是说患难本身是快乐的，而是说我们「知道」神在患难中做什么。「老练」原文是经过试验而证明是真的，像金子经过火炼。患难不会叫信徒的盼望消失，反倒叫它更坚固。

**5. 盼望不至于羞耻。** 「盼望不至于羞耻，因为所赐给我们的圣灵将神的爱浇灌在我们心里。」（5:5）「不至于羞耻」，就是不会落空，不会叫我们失望。为什么？因为我们的盼望不是建立在我们的感觉或环境上，而是建立在神的爱上；而圣灵把这爱「浇灌」在我们心里，这正是约珥书所应许、五旬节所应验的「浇灌」（珥2:28；徒2:17，33），像大雨倾盆，不是一点一滴。

> 要点：称义的人与神相和，站在恩典中，盼望神的荣耀。患难不能夺去这盼望，反而使它更坚固，因为它建立在神已经浇灌在我们心里的爱上。

## 深读 5:6-11 神的爱就在此显明

**1. 在我们还软弱的时候。** 「因我们还软弱的时候，基督就按所定的日期为罪人死。」（5:6）保罗用了几个词来描写我们：「软弱」（5:6）、「罪人」（原文是「不敬虔的人」，5:6；参4:5）、「罪人」（5:8）、「仇敌」（5:10）。基督不是为好人死的，而是为这样的人死的。

**2. 人间最高的爱。** 「为义人死，是少有的；为仁人死，或者有敢做的。」（5:7）人间最高的爱，是愿意为一个好人舍命，而这样的人也是「少有的」。

**3. 神的爱。** 「惟有基督在我们还作罪人的时候为我们死，神的爱就在此向我们显明了。」（5:8）神的爱和人的爱不同：人的爱，是因为对方可爱；神的爱，是在我们一点也不可爱的时候临到我们。「显明」是现在时态：十字架不只是过去的一件事，它今天仍然在向我们证明神的爱。还要留意，保罗说是「神的爱」在基督的死上显明：十字架不是儿子说服了一位不情愿的父，而是父亲自差遣儿子（8:3，32），显明他自己的爱：神的爱预备了他的公义所要求的挽回祭（3:25）。

**4. 「就更要」。** 「现在我们既靠着他的血称义，就更要藉着他免去神的忿怒。因为我们作仇敌的时候，且藉着神儿子的死，得与神和好；既已和好，就更要因他的生得救了。」（5:9-10）这是从难到易的推论：神在我们作仇敌的时候，尚且为我们付出了最大的代价；如今我们已经成了他的朋友，他岂不更要保守我们到底吗？「因他的生得救」，是指复活的基督长远活着，为我们代求（8:34）。我们将来的得救，不是建立在我们的表现上，而是建立在神过去已经为我们所做的事上。

**5. 以神为乐。** 「不但如此，我们既藉着我主耶稣基督得与神和好，也就藉着他以神为乐。」（5:11）救恩的终极目的，不只是免去忿怒，也不只是得着福气，而是得着神自己。和好的最高峰，是「以神为乐」。

> 要点：神的爱不是因为我们可爱，而是在我们还作罪人、作仇敌的时候就临到我们。神既在那时为我们付出了儿子的生命，如今就更要保守我们到底。救恩最大的福气，是得着神自己。

## 深读 5:12-21 亚当与基督

**1. 一人入了世界。** 「这就如罪是从一人入了世界，死又是从罪来的，于是死就临到众人，因为众人都犯了罪。」（5:12）保罗回到创世记第三章。罪不是人类的本来面目，而是一个「入侵者」；死也是这样，它不是自然的，而是罪的结果。

**2. 「因为众人都犯了罪」。** 「因为」（原文 *eph' hō*）多数学者理解为「因为」，而不是奥古斯丁所用拉丁译本的「在他里面」。这句话怎样理解，历来有不同的看法：

- 有人认为，意思是每个人都亲自犯了罪；可是他们犯罪，是因为从亚当承受了败坏的本性（参5:19「成为罪人」），所以亚当的罪仍是根源。
- 也有许多解经家（包括许多改革宗传统的解经家）认为，意思是众人在亚当里犯了罪：亚当作为人类的代表，他的罪算在所有人身上。他们的理由是：5:13-14说，从亚当到摩西，死也临到那些「不与亚当犯一样罪过的」人；5:18-19更明说「因一次的过犯，众人都被定罪」、「因一人的悖逆，众人成为罪人」。

两种看法都同意，每个人都是罪人，都需要救恩；而保罗的重点，是要拿亚当来和基督对比。

**3. 亚当是预像。** 「亚当乃是那以后要来之人的预像。」（5:14）亚当和基督都是「代表」：亚当所做的，影响了所有在他里面的人；基督所做的，也影响了所有在他里面的人。这样的代表观念，对现代人很陌生，我们习惯以为每个人只为自己负责。可是保罗的论证正是建立在这个代表的原则上：我们若觉得「在亚当里被定罪」不公平，就要记得，我们得救的盼望也正是「在基督里被称义」，靠的是另一个人的顺从，而不是我们自己（5:18-19）。

**4. 「何况……更加」。** 保罗不只说亚当和基督相似，更说基督远远超过亚当。「只是过犯不如恩赐……何况神的恩典，与那因耶稣基督一人恩典中的赏赐，岂不更加倍地临到众人吗？」（5:15）亚当的一次过犯，带来了定罪；可是基督的恩赐，是「由许多过犯而称义」（5:16）。恩典不只是把我们带回亚当犯罪以前的光景，而是带我们去到一个更高的地方：「在生命中作王」（5:17）。

**5. 一次的义行，一人的顺从。** 「因一次的过犯，众人都被定罪；照样，因一次的义行，众人也就被称义得生命了。因一人的悖逆，众人成为罪人；照样，因一人的顺从，众人也成为义了。」（5:18-19）「一次的义行」，可能指基督在十字架上的死，也可能指他一生的顺服，在十字架上达到顶峰（参腓2:8）。「成为义」的「成为」，原文的意思是「被立为」「被算为」，是地位的改变：正如因亚当的悖逆，众人被定为罪人；照样，因基督的顺从，信的人被定为义人。这里的「众人」，从上下文看，是指一切属基督的人，就是那些「受洪恩又蒙所赐之义的」（5:17），不是说每一个人都必然得救。

**6. 恩典更显多。** 「律法本是外添的，叫过犯显多；只是罪在哪里显多，恩典就更显多了。」（5:20）律法使罪成为明知故犯的过犯，叫过犯显多（4:15；5:13；7:7-13）。可是无论罪有多深，恩典总是比罪更深。最后两个「作王」对比：「就如罪作王叫人死；照样，恩典也藉着义作王，叫人因我们的主耶稣基督得永生。」（5:21）

> 要点：亚当和基督都是人类的代表。在亚当里，罪和死临到众人；在基督里，恩典和生命更加丰盛地临到一切属他的人。罪无论多深，恩典总是更深。

## 默想

**默想：** 保罗说，「神的爱就在此向我们显明了」：不是在我变好的时候，而是在我「还作罪人」、甚至「作仇敌」的时候。当我怀疑神是否爱我时，我常常看的是什么：我最近的表现、我的感觉、我的环境？保罗要我回头看十字架，因为神的爱是在那里「显明」的，而那里的证据今天仍然有效。如果神在我作他仇敌的时候都愿意为我舍了他的儿子，那么在我今天的软弱、失败、患难中，他岂会撇下我？再想想第一节：「得与神相和」。我是否把这和平当作一种时有时无的感觉？其实它是一个已经成就的事实。最后，保罗说，救恩的最高峰是「以神为乐」。我是只想从神那里得着平安、福气和盼望，还是渴慕得着神自己？

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 5 opens the letter's second major section (chs. 5–8). The first four chapters explained how people are justified; from here Paul explains what justified people receive, and why their hope is secure.

The chapter has two parts. **The fruits of justification** (vv. 1–11): "since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ"; we stand in grace, rejoice even in suffering, because God's love has been poured into our hearts, and the proof of that love is that "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." **Adam and Christ** (vv. 12–21): sin and death entered the world through one man and spread to all; righteousness and life came through one man, Jesus Christ, abounding far more to all who belong to him. "Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more."

Chapters 5–8 are framed by God's love: this chapter begins with "God's love" (5:5, 8), and chapter 8 ends with "the love of God" (8:39). Everything in between is enclosed by God's love.

## Going Deeper: 5:1–5 What the Justified Receive

**1. Peace with God.** "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (5:1). "Peace" is not only a feeling of calm but a changed relationship: once we were "enemies" (5:10), under God's wrath (1:18); now the war is over. This peace is a fact, not a feeling; even when we don't feel it, it remains true. (Many important early manuscripts read "let us have peace with God"; the two Greek words differ by one letter and sounded alike. But most scholars and translations judge from the context that Paul is stating an accomplished fact.)

**2. Standing in grace.** "Through him we have also obtained access by faith into this grace in which we stand" (5:2). "Access" carries the sense of being introduced into a king's presence. We don't drift in and out of grace; we "stand" in it. It is where we are.

**3. Hope of the glory of God.** "And we rejoice in hope of the glory of God" (5:2). Humanity "falls short of the glory of God" (3:23); but the justified hope one day to share in God's glory (8:18, 30).

**4. Rejoicing even in suffering.** "More than that, we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope" (5:3–4). Paul isn't saying suffering is pleasant in itself, but that we "know" what God is doing through it. "Character" means proven genuineness, like gold tested in fire. Suffering doesn't extinguish a believer's hope; it makes it firmer.

**5. Hope that does not put us to shame.** "And hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us" (5:5). "Not put to shame" means it won't disappoint us or come to nothing. Why? Because our hope rests not on our feelings or circumstances but on God's love, which the Spirit has "poured" into our hearts, the very word for the outpouring promised in Joel and fulfilled at Pentecost (Joel 2:28; Acts 2:17, 33), like a downpour, not a trickle.

> The point: The justified have peace with God, stand in grace and hope for God's glory. Suffering cannot take that hope away; it makes it firmer, because it rests on the love God has already poured into our hearts.

## Going Deeper: 5:6–11 God Shows His Love

**1. While we were still weak.** "For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly" (5:6). Paul uses several words for us: "weak" (5:6), "ungodly" (5:6; cf. 4:5), "sinners" (5:8), "enemies" (5:10). Christ didn't die for good people but for people like this.

**2. The highest human love.** "For one will scarcely die for a righteous person, though perhaps for a good person one would dare even to die" (5:7). The highest human love is willingness to lay down one's life for a good person, and even that is "scarce."

**3. God's love.** "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (5:8). God's love differs from human love: we love because the other is lovable; God's love came to us when we were not lovable at all. "Shows" is present tense: the cross is not only a past event; it still proves God's love to us today. Notice too that Paul says it is "God's love" shown in Christ's death: the cross is not the Son persuading a reluctant Father, but the Father himself sending the Son (8:3, 32) and showing his own love: God's love provides the propitiation his justice requires (3:25).

**4. "Much more."** "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God. For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life" (5:9–10). This is an argument from the harder to the easier: if God paid the highest price for us while we were his enemies, won't he much more keep us to the end now that we are his friends? "Saved by his life" refers to the risen Christ, who lives forever and intercedes for us (8:34). Our future salvation rests not on our performance but on what God has already done for us.

**5. Rejoicing in God.** "More than that, we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation" (5:11). The final goal of salvation is not only escape from wrath, nor only receiving blessings, but receiving God himself. The summit of reconciliation is "rejoicing in God."

> The point: God's love did not come because we were lovable; it came to us while we were still sinners and enemies. If God gave his Son's life for us then, he will much more keep us to the end now. The greatest blessing of salvation is God himself.

## Going Deeper: 5:12–21 Adam and Christ

**1. Through one man.** "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (5:12). Paul returns to Genesis 3. Sin is not humanity's original nature but an "invader"; so too is death, which is not natural but the result of sin.

**2. "Because all sinned."** Most scholars take *eph' hō* as "because," not "in whom" as in the Latin version Augustine used. The phrase has been understood in different ways:

- Some take it to mean that each person has personally sinned; but they sin because they have inherited a corrupt nature from Adam (cf. 5:19, "made sinners"), so Adam's sin remains the root.
- Many interpreters (including many in the Reformed tradition) take it to mean that all sinned in Adam: Adam acted as humanity's representative, and his sin is counted to all. They point out that 5:13–14 says death reigned from Adam to Moses even over those "whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam," and that 5:18–19 states plainly that "one trespass led to condemnation for all men" and "by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners."

Both views agree that everyone is a sinner who needs salvation; and Paul's main purpose is to set Adam alongside Christ.

**3. Adam as a type.** "Adam, who was a type of the one who was to come" (5:14). Adam and Christ are both "representatives": what Adam did affected all who are in him; what Christ did affects all who are in him. Such representation feels foreign to modern people, used to thinking that each of us answers only for ourselves. Yet Paul's argument rests on this very principle of representation: if being condemned "in Adam" seems unfair, remember that our only hope is being justified "in Christ," by another's obedience rather than our own (5:18–19).

**4. "Much more."** Paul doesn't only say that Adam and Christ are alike; he says Christ far surpasses Adam. "But the free gift is not like the trespass. For if many died through one man's trespass, much more have the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded for many" (5:15). Adam's one trespass brought condemnation; but Christ's gift "following many trespasses brought justification" (5:16). Grace doesn't simply return us to where Adam was before he fell; it takes us higher: to "reign in life" (5:17).

**5. One act of righteousness, one man's obedience.** "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men. For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (5:18–19). "One act of righteousness" may refer to Christ's death on the cross, or to his whole life of obedience reaching its climax at the cross (cf. Phil 2:8). "Made" (*kathistēmi*) means "appointed" or "constituted," a change of status: as Adam's disobedience constituted the many sinners, so Christ's obedience constitutes believers righteous. In context, "all" and "the many" here refer to all who belong to Christ, "those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness" (5:17); Paul is not saying that every person will inevitably be saved.

**6. Grace abounding.** "Now the law came in to increase the trespass, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more" (5:20). The law turned sin into conscious transgression and made it abound (4:15; 5:13; 7:7–13). But however deep sin goes, grace goes deeper still. The chapter ends with two reigns: "so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (5:21).

> The point: Adam and Christ are both representatives of humanity. In Adam, sin and death came to all; in Christ, grace and life abound far more to all who belong to him. However deep sin goes, grace goes deeper.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** Paul says, "God shows his love for us": not when I've improved, but while I was "still a sinner," even an "enemy." When I doubt that God loves me, what do I usually look at: my recent performance, my feelings, my circumstances? Paul turns me back to the cross, because that is where God's love is "shown," and that evidence still stands today. If God gave his Son for me while I was his enemy, will he abandon me now, in today's weakness, failure or suffering? Think too of verse 1: "we have peace with God." Do I treat this peace as a feeling that comes and goes? In fact it is an accomplished fact. Finally, Paul says the summit of salvation is to "rejoice in God." Do I want only peace, blessing and hope from God, or do I long for God himself?
