## 本章脉络

第八章以凯歌结束：没有什么能叫我们与神的爱隔绝。可是一个问题随即出现：神曾经把应许赐给以色列，如今大多数以色列人却拒绝了基督。如果神对以色列的应许都可能落空，我们又凭什么相信8:28-39的应许呢？第九至十一章就是回答这个问题。

第九章分四段。**保罗的伤痛**（1-5节）：他为骨肉之亲「大有忧愁，心里时常伤痛」，甚至愿意自己被咒诅。**神的话没有落空**（6-13节）：「从以色列生的不都是以色列人」；神的应许从来是照着他的拣选，从以撒和雅各就是这样。**神不公平吗？**（14-29节）：「断乎没有！」「我要怜悯谁就怜悯谁」；窑匠有权柄处置泥土；神所召的人「不但是从犹太人中，也是从外邦人中」。**绊脚石**（30-33节）：外邦人因信得了义，以色列人却想靠行为，就跌在那块石头上。

这是罗马书中最难的一章，历代教会对它有许多讨论。读的时候要记得两件事：第一，保罗写这一章，不是带着冷冰冰的逻辑，而是带着眼泪（9:2）；第二，这整段论证（九至十一章）的结尾，不是哲学结论，而是敬拜（11:33-36）。华人教会在这一章上也有不同立场；但大家都同意：得救全在乎神的怜悯，人仍要为不信负责。读的时候要谦卑，不要拿它来给弟兄姊妹贴标签。

## 深读 9:1-5 保罗的眼泪

**1. 大有忧愁。** 「我在基督里说真话，并不谎言，有我良心被圣灵感动，给我作见证：我是大有忧愁，心里时常伤痛。」（9:1-2）刚刚说完「得胜有余」（8:37），保罗立刻流下眼泪。确信神的爱，并不会叫人对别人的灭亡麻木，反而叫人更为他们伤痛。

**2. 愿意被咒诅。** 「为我弟兄，我骨肉之亲，就是自己被咒诅，与基督分离，我也愿意。」（9:3）这让人想起摩西：以色列人造了金牛犊以后，摩西求神说：「倘或你肯赦免他们的罪……不然，求你从你所写的册上涂抹我的名。」（出32:32）保罗知道这是不可能的（8:38-39），可是他的心愿意这样。保罗愿意却不能做的，基督真的做了：他「为我们受了咒诅」（加3:13），在十字架上被离弃，好叫我们永远不与神的爱隔绝。保罗的心，是基督之心的回声。

**3. 以色列的特权。** 「他们是以色列人；那儿子的名分、荣耀、诸约、律法、礼仪、应许都是他们的。列祖就是他们的祖宗；按肉体说，基督也是从他们出来的。」（9:4-5）以色列的特权是真实的，而最大的特权，就是基督按肉体是从他们出来的。

**4. 「永远可称颂的神」。** 「他是在万有之上，永远可称颂的神。阿们！」（9:5）最早的抄本没有标点，所以这句话怎样断句，是编者和译者的判断：有人把后半句读作一句独立的颂赞（「愿那在万有之上的神永远受称颂」）。可是多数解经家都认为，这里是直接称基督为「在万有之上……的神」。这是新约中明确宣告基督神性的经文之一。

> 要点：保罗确信神的爱，所以为不信的骨肉之亲伤痛；他的心，是那位为我们受咒诅的基督之心的回声。我们认识恩典越深，就越不能对别人的灭亡无动于衷。

## 深读 9:6-13 不在乎行为，乃在乎召人的主

**1. 神的话没有落空。** 「这不是说神的话落了空。因为从以色列生的不都是以色列人。」（9:6）这是第九至十一章的主题句。神的应许从来不是给每一个按血统作以色列人的，而是给「应许的儿女」（9:8）。

**2. 以撒与以实玛利。** 「惟独『从以撒生的才要称为你的后裔。』」（9:7；引创21:12）亚伯拉罕有两个儿子，可是应许是藉着以撒延续的。以撒是一个「应许的儿子」：他的出生本身就是一个神迹（4:19）。

**3. 雅各与以扫。** 有人可能会说：以实玛利的母亲是使女，所以不同。保罗就举出一个更清楚的例子：雅各和以扫是双胞胎，同一个父亲、同一个母亲，「双子还没有生下来，善恶还没有做出来，只因要显明神拣选人的旨意，不在乎人的行为，乃在乎召人的主」（9:11）。拣选的根据，不在于人做了什么，甚至不在于人将来会做什么，而是在于神自己。

**4. 「雅各是我所爱的；以扫是我所恶的」。**（「恶」读wù，憎恶） （9:13；引玛1:2-3）这句话常令读者不安。要注意几点：

- 在希伯来的用语中，「爱」与「恶」的对比，常常表示拣选与不拣选，或「爱这个胜过爱那个」（参创29:30-31，「失宠」原文就是「被恨」；路14:26与太10:37对照）。但玛拉基书1:3-4也显明，「恶」包括神对以东的审判；它的意思是不拣选、弃绝，而不是情绪上的怨恨。
- 玛拉基书讲的是两个民族（以色列和以东）的历史。有解经家因此认为，罗马书第九章讲的是神拣选一个民族，在救恩历史中担当使命，而不是个人的永远得救。
- 可是也有许多解经家（特别是改革宗传统）指出，保罗在这里的问题正是个人的得救：为什么有些以色列人得救，有些没有（9:1-3，27；10:1）？而且「不在乎人的行为」这句话，是关乎得救的原则。

无论哪一种理解，保罗的重点都很清楚：神的拣选是出于他的恩典，不在乎人的行为。

> 要点：神的应许从来不是靠血统或行为，而是靠神自己的拣选。这样，神的话就不会落空，因为它从来不依靠人。

## 深读 9:14-29 窑匠与泥土

**1. 神不公平吗？** 「这样，我们可说什么呢？难道神有什么不公平吗？断乎没有！」（9:14）许多（特别是改革宗的）解经家指出，如果拣选是根据人的行为或预见的信心，这个反问就很难出现了。正因为拣选完全在乎神，人才会问：这公平吗？

**2. 怜悯的主权。** 「因他对摩西说：我要怜悯谁就怜悯谁，要恩待谁就恩待谁。据此看来，这不在乎那定意的，也不在乎那奔跑的，只在乎发怜悯的神。」（9:15-16；引出33:19）要留意神的这句话是在什么时候说的：以色列人刚刚造了金牛犊，全民族都配受审判。在那个时候，神宣告自己的名，宣告的正是他的怜悯。保罗讲的拣选，也不是华人所说的「命」或「宿命」：宿命是没有位格、冷漠的定数；圣经的拣选，是一位有位格、满有怜悯的神在基督里施恩，同时要人负责回应（9:32）。怜悯，本来就不是人配得的；若是应得的，就不叫怜悯了。

**3. 法老。** 「因为经上有话向法老说：『我将你兴起来，特要在你身上彰显我的权能，并要使我的名传遍天下。』如此看来，神要怜悯谁就怜悯谁，要叫谁刚硬就叫谁刚硬。」（9:17-18；引出9:16）出埃及记在第一场灾难之前，就记载神说「我要使他的心刚硬」（出4:21；7:3）；接着说法老的心刚硬、法老自己硬着心（出7:13；8:15，32），然后说「耶和华使法老的心刚硬」（出9:12）。神的主权与人的责任，在圣经中是并存的。神叫人刚硬，从来不是把恶放进一颗无辜的心，而是在一个已经悖逆的罪人身上施行审判，任凭他在自己所选的路上越走越刚硬（参1:24-28）；然而保罗在9:18把刚硬和怜悯一样，都归在神的主权之下。

**4. 「你是谁？」** 「这样，你必对我说：『他为什么还指责人呢？有谁抗拒他的旨意呢？』你这个人哪，你是谁，竟敢向神强嘴呢？」（9:19-20）保罗没有给一个哲学上的答案，而是提醒我们自己的位置：我们是受造之物，他是创造主。这不是叫人不要思想，而是叫人不要坐在审判官的位置上审判神。

**5. 窑匠与器皿。** 「窑匠难道没有权柄从一团泥里拿一块做成贵重的器皿，又拿一块做成卑贱的器皿吗？」（9:21；参赛29:16；耶18:1-6）自奥古斯丁以来，许多解经家认为，「一团泥」是在亚当里已经堕落的全人类（5:12）；也有人认为是指以色列（参耶18章）。无论如何，保罗的重点是窑匠的权柄。神没有冤枉任何人；他向一些人施怜悯，是恩典；他让一些人承受他们自己选择的后果，是公义。要留意9:22-23的一个差别：「蒙怜悯」的器皿是神「早预备得荣耀的」，而「可怒」的器皿只说是「预备遭毁灭的」，没有说是谁预备的；而且神对他们也是「多多忍耐宽容」。许多解经家看重这个差别：神是救恩的主动者，而灭亡是罪人应得的。

**6. 不是我子民的。** 「这器皿就是我们被神所召的，不但是从犹太人中，也是从外邦人中。」（9:24）保罗引用何西阿书：「那本来不是我子民的，我要称为『我的子民』。」（9:25-26；引何2:23；1:10）。何西阿原是论到被弃又蒙恢复的北国以色列；保罗看见同样的怜悯原则，如今临到外邦人。神的主权不是叫人绝望的教义：正因为得救在乎神，不在乎人，连那些「本来不是蒙爱的」外邦人，也可以被称为「永生神的儿子」（9:26）。

> 要点：怜悯本来就不是人配得的。神没有亏欠任何人；他向不配的人施怜悯，是何等的恩典。正因为得救只在乎发怜悯的神，连最远的人也有指望。

## 深读 9:30-33 绊脚石

**1. 一个令人惊讶的结果。** 「那本来不追求义的外邦人反得了义，就是因信而得的义。但以色列人追求律法的义，反得不着律法的义。」（9:30-31）

**2. 原因。** 「这是什么缘故呢？是因为他们不凭着信心求，只凭着行为求；他们正跌在那绊脚石上。」（9:32）这里要留意：保罗在讲完神的主权之后，立刻讲人的责任。以色列人没有得着义，是因为他们自己「不凭着信心求」。神的拣选和人的责任，保罗都同时宣告，并不用一边去否定另一边。

**3. 那块石头。** 「就如经上所记：我在锡安放一块绊脚的石头，跌人的磐石；信靠他的人必不至于羞愧。」（9:33；引赛28:16；8:14）这块石头就是基督。想靠自己的人，在他身上绊跌；信靠他的人，却以他为稳固的根基。这块石头今天仍摆在每一个人面前。

> 要点：神的主权和人的责任，保罗同时宣告。以色列人跌倒，是因为他们想靠行为；同一块石头，对信靠他的人却是磐石。

## 默想

**默想：** 「这不在乎那定意的，也不在乎那奔跑的，只在乎发怜悯的神。」我是否在心里暗暗觉得，我能信主，多少是因为我比别人更有悟性、更愿意寻求、更有属灵的胃口？保罗说，我今天能站在恩典中，唯一的原因是神向我发了怜悯；而怜悯，本来就是我不配得的。这样的认识，会叫我在不信的人面前更谦卑，而不是更骄傲。再看保罗：他越确信神的主权，就越为他的骨肉之亲流泪。神的主权在我身上，是否也带出同样的结果：不是冷漠地说「反正神都定好了」，而是更迫切地为我的同胞、我的家乡、我从小一起长大的那群人祷告，也为保罗的骨肉之亲，就是今天的犹太人祷告，因为我知道，连像我这样「本来不是蒙爱的」人，神都能称为「蒙爱的」？如果我读这一章时心里害怕：「万一我不是蒙拣选的呢？」保罗并没有叫我去窥探神隐秘的旨意，而是指给我看那块石头：「信靠他的人必不至于羞愧。」（9:33）法老从来没有为自己的刚硬担忧；一颗害怕离神太远、渴望来到基督面前的心，正是神的怜悯在作工的样子。主耶稣说：「到我这里来的，我总不丢弃他。」（约6:37）今天我不必先解开拣选的奥秘，就可以直接投靠他。当我读到这一章难以明白的地方，我能不能先像保罗在11:33那样俯伏下来：「深哉，神丰富的智慧和知识！」

## The Shape of the Chapter

Chapter 8 ended in triumph: nothing can separate us from the love of God. But a question follows at once: God once gave his promises to Israel, yet now most Israelites have rejected Christ. If God's promises to Israel could fail, what grounds do we have to trust the promises of 8:28–39? Chapters 9–11 answer this question.

Chapter 9 has four parts. **Paul's anguish** (vv. 1–5): he has "great sorrow and unceasing anguish" for his own people, even wishing himself accursed. **God's word has not failed** (vv. 6–13): "not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel"; God's promise has always followed his election, from Isaac and Jacob on. **Is God unjust?** (vv. 14–29): "By no means!" "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy"; the potter has rights over the clay; God's mercy reaches those called "not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles." **The stumbling stone** (vv. 30–33): Gentiles attained righteousness by faith, while Israel, pursuing it by works, stumbled over the stone.

This is the hardest chapter in Romans, and the church has debated it through the centuries. Keep two things in mind as you read. First, Paul writes it not with cold logic but with tears (9:2). Second, this whole argument (chs. 9–11) ends not in a philosophical conclusion but in the worship of 11:33–36. Chinese churches hold different positions on this chapter, but all agree that salvation rests wholly on God's mercy and that people remain responsible for unbelief. Read it humbly, and don't use it to label fellow believers.

## Going Deeper: 9:1–5 Paul's Tears

**1. Great sorrow.** "I am speaking the truth in Christ (I am not lying; my conscience bears me witness in the Holy Spirit) that I have great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" (9:1–2). Having just said "more than conquerors" (8:37), Paul is at once in tears. Assurance of God's love does not make a person numb to the lostness of others; quite the opposite.

**2. Willing to be accursed.** "For I could wish that I myself were accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of my brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh" (9:3). This recalls Moses: after the golden calf, Moses prayed, "But now, if you will forgive their sin, but if not, please blot me out of your book that you have written" (Exod 32:32). Paul knows this is impossible (8:38–39), yet his heart would be willing. What Paul wished but could not do, Christ actually did: he became "a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), forsaken on the cross so that we would never be separated from God's love. Paul's heart is an echo of Christ's.

**3. Israel's privileges.** "They are Israelites, and to them belong the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises. To them belong the patriarchs, and from their race, according to the flesh, is the Christ" (9:4–5). Israel's privileges were real, and the greatest of them was that Christ, according to the flesh, came from them.

**4. "God over all, blessed forever."** "...the Christ, who is God over all, blessed forever. Amen" (9:5). The earliest manuscripts had no punctuation, so how to divide this sentence is an editorial judgment: some take the last clause as a separate doxology ("God who is over all be blessed forever"). But most interpreters (including the translators of the CUV and ESV) take it as directly calling Christ "God over all." It is one of the New Testament's clear affirmations of Christ's deity.

> The point: Because Paul was sure of God's love, he grieved for his unbelieving kinsmen; his heart echoes that of Christ, who became a curse for us. The more deeply we know grace, the less we can be indifferent to the lostness of others.

## Going Deeper: 9:6–13 Not Because of Works but Because of His Call

**1. God's word has not failed.** "But it is not as though the word of God has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel" (9:6). This is the thesis of chapters 9–11. God's promise was never given to every physical Israelite, but to "the children of the promise" (9:8).

**2. Isaac and Ishmael.** "But 'Through Isaac shall your offspring be named'" (9:7, quoting Gen 21:12). Abraham had two sons, but the promise continued through Isaac. Isaac was a "son of promise": his very birth was God's miracle (4:19).

**3. Jacob and Esau.** Someone might say that Ishmael's mother was a servant, so the case is different. So Paul gives a clearer example: Jacob and Esau were twins, with the same father and mother, and "though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad, in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of his call" (9:11). The ground of election is not what people have done, nor even what they will do, but God himself.

**4. "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated."** (9:13, quoting Mal 1:2–3). This sentence troubles many readers. Note several things:

- In Hebrew idiom, the contrast of "love" and "hate" often expresses choosing and not choosing, or "loving one more than the other" (cf. Gen 29:30–31; compare Luke 14:26 with Matt 10:37). But Mal 1:3–4 shows that "hated" includes God's judgment on Edom; it means not chosen, rejected, not emotional spite.
- Malachi speaks of the history of two nations (Israel and Edom). Some interpreters therefore think Romans 9 concerns God's choosing of groups for roles in salvation history rather than the eternal salvation of individuals.
- Many others (especially in the Reformed tradition) point out that Paul's question here is precisely individual salvation: why some Israelites are saved and others are not (9:1–3, 27; 10:1). And "not because of works" is a principle of salvation.

On any reading, Paul's point is clear: God's election flows from his grace, not from human works.

> The point: God's promise has never rested on bloodline or works, but on God's own election. So God's word cannot fail, because it never depended on us.

## Going Deeper: 9:14–29 The Potter and the Clay

**1. Is God unjust?** "What shall we say then? Is there injustice on God's part? By no means!" (9:14). Many (especially Reformed) interpreters note that if election rested on works or foreseen faith, this objection would hardly arise. It is precisely because election rests wholly on God that people ask: is this fair?

**2. Sovereign mercy.** "For he says to Moses, 'I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.' So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy" (9:15–16, quoting Exod 33:19). Notice when God spoke these words: Israel had just made the golden calf, and the whole nation deserved judgment. At that moment God proclaimed his name, and what he proclaimed was his mercy. Nor is the election Paul speaks of the "fate" or "destiny" of Chinese tradition: fate is impersonal and indifferent; biblical election is a personal, merciful God showing grace in Christ while calling people to respond (9:32). Mercy means what is not deserved. If it were deserved, it would not be mercy.

**3. Pharaoh.** "For the Scripture says to Pharaoh, 'For this very purpose I have raised you up, that I might show my power in you, and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth.' So then he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills" (9:17–18, quoting Exod 9:16). Before the first plague God announces, "I will harden his heart" (Exod 4:21; 7:3); then Pharaoh's heart is hardened and Pharaoh hardens his own heart (Exod 7:13; 8:15, 32); then "the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh" (Exod 9:12). In Scripture, God's sovereignty and human responsibility stand side by side. God's hardening never puts evil into an innocent heart; it is his judicial act on a sinner already in rebellion, giving him over to and confirming him in his chosen way (cf. 1:24–28). Yet in 9:18 Paul places hardening, like mercy, under God's sovereign will.

**4. "Who are you?"** "You will say to me then, 'Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?' But who are you, O man, to answer back to God?" (9:19–20). Paul gives no philosophical answer; he reminds us of our place: we are creatures, and he is the Creator. This is not a ban on thinking but a ban on sitting as judge over God.

**5. The potter and the vessels.** "Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honored use and another for dishonorable use?" (9:21; cf. Isa 29:16; Jer 18:1–6). Many interpreters since Augustine take "the same lump" as the human race already fallen in Adam (5:12); others take it as Israel (cf. Jer 18). Either way, Paul's point is the potter's right. God wrongs no one; to show mercy to some is grace; to let others bear the consequences of their own choices is justice. Note a difference in 9:22–23: the "vessels of mercy" are ones God "has prepared beforehand for glory," while the "vessels of wrath" are simply "prepared for destruction," without saying who prepared them; and God "has endured with much patience vessels of wrath." Many see significance in this difference: God is the active author of salvation, while destruction is what sinners deserve.

**6. "Not my people."** "Even us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles" (9:24). Paul quotes Hosea: "Those who were not my people I will call 'my people'" (9:25–26, quoting Hos 2:23; 1:10). Hosea spoke of the northern kingdom of Israel, rejected and then restored; Paul sees the same pattern of mercy now reaching the Gentiles. God's sovereignty is not a doctrine of despair: precisely because salvation depends on God and not on us, even Gentiles who were "not beloved" can be called "sons of the living God" (9:26).

> The point: Mercy means what is not deserved. God owes no one anything; how great a grace it is that he shows mercy to the undeserving. Precisely because salvation depends only on God who has mercy, even those farthest away have hope.

## Going Deeper: 9:30–33 The Stumbling Stone

**1. A surprising result.** "That Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have attained it, that is, a righteousness that is by faith; but that Israel who pursued a law that would lead to righteousness did not succeed in reaching that law" (9:30–31).

**2. The reason.** "Why? Because they did not pursue it by faith, but as if it were based on works. They have stumbled over the stumbling stone" (9:32). Notice: immediately after speaking of God's sovereignty, Paul speaks of human responsibility. Israel failed to attain righteousness because they themselves "did not pursue it by faith." Paul declares both God's election and human responsibility, without letting either cancel the other.

**3. The stone.** "As it is written, 'Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense; and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame'" (9:33, quoting Isa 28:16; 8:14). The stone is Christ. To those who rely on themselves he is a stumbling stone; to those who trust him he is a firm foundation.

> The point: Paul declares God's sovereignty and human responsibility together. Israel stumbled because they relied on works; yet the same stone is a sure rock for all who trust him.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "It depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy." Do I secretly think that I came to faith partly because I was more perceptive, more willing to seek, more spiritually inclined than others? Paul says the only reason I stand in grace today is that God has had mercy on me, and mercy means I didn't deserve it. Knowing this makes me humbler, not prouder, before those who don't believe. Look at Paul too: the surer he was of God's sovereignty, the more he wept for his own people. Does God's sovereignty produce the same in me: not a cold "God has already decided anyway," but more urgent prayer for my own people, my hometown, the community I grew up in, and for Paul's own kinsmen, the Jewish people today, because I know that God can call even someone like me, who was "not beloved," "beloved"? If, reading this chapter, I am afraid, "What if I am not one of the elect?", Paul does not send me to pry into God's hidden decree; he points me to the stone: "whoever believes in him will not be put to shame" (9:33). Pharaoh never worried about his own hardness; a heart that fears being far from God and longs to come to Christ looks like mercy at work. Jesus said, "whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (John 6:37). Today I can come to him without first solving the mystery of election. When I come to parts of this chapter I find hard to understand, can I first bow down as Paul does in 11:33: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!"?
