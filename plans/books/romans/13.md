## 本章脉络

第十二章末了，保罗说不要自己伸冤，「伸冤在我」。第十三章接着讲神在这个世代中设立的一个伸冤的工具：在上掌权的。

本章分三段。**顺服掌权的**（1-7节）：「没有权柄不是出于神的」；掌权的「是神的用人」，所以信徒要顺服、纳粮上税，「当恭敬的，恭敬他」。**爱的债**（8-10节）：「凡事都不可亏欠人，惟有彼此相爱要常以为亏欠」，因为「爱就完全了律法」。**黑夜已深，白昼将近**（11-14节）：「我们得救，现今比初信的时候更近了」，所以要「脱去暗昧的行为，带上光明的兵器」，「总要披戴主耶稣基督」。

这三段不是互不相关的劝勉。它们都发生在一个特别的时刻：基督已经来了，又将要再来。信徒活在「白昼将近」的时候，所以用新的眼光看政府、看邻舍、看自己的身体。

## 深读 13:1-7 掌权的是神的用人

**1. 权柄出于神。** 「在上有权柄的，人人当顺服他，因为没有权柄不是出于神的。凡掌权的都是神所命的。」（13:1）这是旧约一贯的教导：神「废王，立王」（但2:21）。保罗写这封信时，罗马皇帝是尼禄，他后来成为逼迫基督徒的人；可是在他执政初期（主后54-59年左右），罗马的治理相对平稳。保罗说，即使是一个不信神的政府，它的权柄也是出于神的。

**2. 掌权者的职分。** 「作官的原不是叫行善的惧怕，乃是叫作恶的惧怕……因为他是神的用人，是与你有益的。你若作恶，却当惧怕，因为他不是空空地佩剑；他是神的用人，是伸冤的，刑罚那作恶的。」（13:3-4）保罗在这里描写的是政府应有的职分：赏善罚恶，维护公义和秩序。12:19说「不要自己伸冤……听凭主怒」，13:4就用了同样的字（「伸冤」「怒」）：掌权者是「伸冤的」。神在今世藉着掌权者，部分地、暂时地施行公义；完全的伸冤要等到神审判的日子（2:5）。

**3. 顺服的界限。** 保罗的命令是普遍的，可是圣经其他地方清楚地表明，顺服掌权者有一个界限：当人的命令与神的命令冲突时，「顺从神，不顺从人，是应当的」（徒5:29；参但3章；6章）。保罗自己也曾因传福音被关进监里。福音派对这一段的理解大致有两种侧重：

- 有人强调，这段经文是在描写神设立政府的目的；一个政府若颠倒善恶、逼迫行善的人，就背离了神所赋予的职分，信徒可以用合法的方式抗议，甚至在必要时不服从（这是加尔文《基督教要义》4.20.31-32以来改革宗的一个传统）。
- 也有人强调，保罗（和彼得，彼前2:13-17）是在一个不认识神、后来更逼迫教会的帝国中写这些话的，所以信徒的基本态度应当是顺服和尊重，除非被要求直接违背神的命令。

两种理解都同意：顺服掌权者不是无条件的，而信徒最高的效忠属于耶稣（10:9）。

**4. 因为良心。** 「所以，你们必须顺服，不但是因为刑罚，也是因为良心。」（13:5）基督徒遵守法律，不只是怕被罚，也是因为在神面前有清洁的良心。

**5. 凡人所当得的。** 「你们纳粮，也为这个缘故……凡人所当得的，就给他。当得粮的，给他纳粮；当得税的，给他上税；当惧怕的，惧怕他；当恭敬的，恭敬他。」（13:6-7）这让人想起耶稣的话：「凯撒的物当归给凯撒，神的物当归给神。」（太22:21）诚实纳税，尊重在上位的人，即使我们不喜欢他们，也是对神的顺服。

> 要点：掌权的是神的用人，为要赏善罚恶。信徒因着良心顺服、纳税、尊重在上位的人；可是我们最高的效忠属于耶稣，当人的命令与神的命令冲突时，顺从神是应当的。

## 深读 13:8-10 永远还不完的债

**1. 只有一样债。** 「凡事都不可亏欠人，惟有彼此相爱要常以为亏欠，因为爱人的就完全了律法。」（13:8）13:7讲「凡人所当得的，就给他」：税、粮、惧怕、恭敬，这些都可以付清。可是有一样债是永远付不清的，就是爱。我们爱了别人，并不因此就「不欠」了，因为基督的爱叫我们一直欠着这份爱。

**2. 爱完全了律法。** 「像那不可奸淫，不可杀人，不可偷盗，不可贪婪，或有别的诫命，都包在爱人如己这一句话之内了。」（13:9；引利19:18）这与耶稣的教导完全一致（太22:39-40）。爱不是取代了诫命，而是诫命的总纲：诫命告诉我们，爱在具体的生活中是什么样子。

**3. 爱不加害与人。** 「爱是不加害与人的，所以爱就完全了律法。」（13:10）十诫后半部的诫命都是保护邻舍的，例如他的婚姻、他的生命、他的财物、他的名誉。爱邻舍，就是不伤害他，并且积极地为他谋求益处。

> 要点：税可以付清，爱的债却永远付不清。爱不是取代诫命，而是诫命的总纲；爱邻舍，就是不伤害他，并且为他的益处着想。

## 深读 13:11-14 白昼将近

**1. 认识时候。** 「再者，你们晓得，现今就是该趁早睡醒的时候；因为我们得救，现今比初信的时候更近了。」（13:11）这里的「得救」，是指基督再来时救恩的完成（参8:23）。每过一天，我们离那一天就更近一天。

**2. 黑夜已深，白昼将近。** 「黑夜已深，白昼将近。我们就当脱去暗昧的行为，带上光明的兵器。」（13:12）这个世代是「黑夜」，可是黑夜已经快过去了；基督的复活已经让第一道曙光照进来。信徒是「属白昼」的人（参帖前5:5-8），所以要像早上起床的人一样，脱下睡衣，穿上白天的衣服。

**3. 三对罪。** 「行事为人要端正，好像行在白昼。不可荒宴醉酒；不可好色邪荡；不可争竞嫉妒。」（13:13）保罗把「争竞嫉妒」和醉酒、淫乱放在一起。在教会里，我们常常认为醉酒和淫乱是大罪，争竞和嫉妒却只是个性问题；保罗却把它们一起列为「暗昧的行为」。

**4. 披戴主耶稣基督。** 「总要披戴主耶稣基督，不要为肉体安排，去放纵私欲。」（13:14）「披戴」是穿衣服的意思。信徒在受洗时已经「披戴基督」了（加3:27）；现在要每天重新穿上他：在思想、言语、行为上，活出基督的样式。奥古斯丁说，他在米兰的花园里读到13:13-14这两节，「顿觉有一道恬静的光射到心中，溃散了阴霾笼罩的疑阵」（《忏悔录》8.12，周士良译），从此归信基督。

**5. 不要为肉体安排。** 「安排」原文 *pronoia*，是预先筹划、准备的意思。罪很少是突然临到的，常常是我们事先给它留了门：去某个地方、留着某些东西、和某些人来往。保罗说，不要给肉体预备机会。

> 要点：黑夜已深，白昼将近。信徒活在基督再来之前的清晨，所以要脱去暗昧的行为，每天披戴主耶稣基督，不给肉体留下机会。

## 默想

**默想：** 「当得税的，给他上税；……当恭敬的，恭敬他。」我在报税、开发票、填报销单这些小事上，是否诚实？我是否觉得「大家都这样」，或者「政府不好，所以少交一点也没关系」？保罗写这段话时，罗马皇帝是尼禄。我的诚实，不是因为掌权的人都值得尊敬，而是因为我在神面前要有清洁的良心。再想想13:14：「总要披戴主耶稣基督。」披戴，不是靠自己织一件新衣服，而是穿上别人为我预备好的衣服。我今天的「白天的衣服」不是我自己的义，而是基督自己。我能不能在今天早上，像穿衣服一样，有意识地对主说：「主啊，今天我要穿上你」？然后看看：有没有一扇我常常为肉体留着的门，今天需要关上？

## The Shape of the Chapter

At the end of chapter 12 Paul said not to avenge ourselves: "Vengeance is mine." Chapter 13 goes on to speak of an instrument of justice God has appointed in this age: the governing authorities.

The chapter has three parts. **Submitting to the authorities** (vv. 1–7): "there is no authority except from God"; the ruler "is God's servant," so believers submit, pay taxes and give "honor to whom honor is owed." **The debt of love** (vv. 8–10): "Owe no one anything, except to love each other," for "love is the fulfilling of the law." **The night is far gone; the day is at hand** (vv. 11–14): "salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed," so "let us cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light," and "put on the Lord Jesus Christ."

These three sections are not unrelated pieces of advice. They all belong to a particular moment: Christ has come and will come again. Believers live as "the day is at hand," so they see government, neighbors and their own bodies with new eyes.

## Going Deeper: 13:1–7 The Ruler Is God's Servant

**1. Authority comes from God.** "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God" (13:1). This is the consistent teaching of the Old Testament: God "removes kings and sets up kings" (Dan 2:21). When Paul wrote, the emperor was Nero, who later persecuted Christians; but in the early years of his reign (c. AD 54–59) Roman government was relatively stable. Paul says that even an unbelieving government holds its authority from God.

**2. The ruler's task.** "For rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad... for he is God's servant for your good. But if you do wrong, be afraid, for he does not bear the sword in vain. For he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (13:3–4). Paul describes what government is meant to do: reward good and punish evil, upholding justice and order. Romans 12:19 said "never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God"; 13:4 picks up the same words ("avenger," "wrath"). In this age God carries out a partial, provisional justice through governing authorities; full vengeance awaits the day of God's judgment (2:5).

**3. The limits of submission.** Paul's command is general, but Scripture elsewhere makes clear that submission to authorities has a limit: when human commands conflict with God's, "we must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29; cf. Dan 3; 6). Paul himself was imprisoned for preaching the gospel. Evangelicals read this passage with two main emphases:

- Some stress that the passage describes God's purpose for government; a government that turns good and evil upside down and persecutes those who do good has abandoned its God-given task, and believers may protest by lawful means, and if necessary refuse to comply (a strand of Reformed thought going back to Calvin, *Institutes* 4.20.31–32).
- Others stress that Paul (and Peter, 1 Pet 2:13–17) wrote within a pagan empire that would later persecute the church, so the believer's basic posture should be submission and respect, except when commanded to disobey God directly.

Both agree that submission to authorities is not unconditional, and that the believer's highest allegiance belongs to Jesus (10:9).

**4. For the sake of conscience.** "Therefore one must be in subjection, not only to avoid God's wrath but also for the sake of conscience" (13:5). Christians keep the law not only for fear of punishment but to keep a clean conscience before God.

**5. Pay to all what is owed.** "For the same reason you also pay taxes... Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed, revenue to whom revenue is owed, respect to whom respect is owed, honor to whom honor is owed" (13:6–7). This recalls Jesus' words: "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Matt 22:21). Paying taxes honestly and respecting those in authority, even when we don't like them, is obedience to God.

> The point: Rulers are God's servants to reward good and punish evil. Believers submit for conscience' sake, pay taxes and respect those in authority; but our highest allegiance belongs to Jesus, and when human commands conflict with God's, we must obey God.

## Going Deeper: 13:8–10 A Debt Never Paid Off

**1. Only one debt.** "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (13:8). Verse 7 said "pay to all what is owed": taxes, revenue, respect, honor, all of which can be paid in full. But one debt can never be paid off: love. Having loved someone doesn't make us "even," because Christ's love keeps us always owing this love.

**2. Love fulfills the law.** "The commandments, 'You shall not commit adultery, You shall not murder, You shall not steal, You shall not covet,' and any other commandment, are summed up in this word: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself'" (13:9, quoting Lev 19:18). This matches Jesus' teaching exactly (Matt 22:39–40). Love does not replace the commandments but sums them up: the commandments show what love looks like in concrete life.

**3. Love does no wrong.** "Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the law" (13:10). The second-table commandments protect our neighbor, for example their marriage, life, property and good name. To love a neighbor is not to harm them, and actively to seek their good.

> The point: Taxes can be paid in full, but the debt of love never can. Love does not replace the commandments but sums them up; to love a neighbor is not to harm them and to seek their good.

## Going Deeper: 13:11–14 The Day Is at Hand

**1. Knowing the time.** "Besides this you know the time, that the hour has come for you to wake from sleep. For salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed" (13:11). "Salvation" here means its completion when Christ returns (cf. 8:23). Every day brings us one day nearer.

**2. The night is far gone.** "The night is far gone; the day is at hand. So then let us cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light" (13:12). This age is "night," but the night is nearly over; Christ's resurrection has already let in the first light of dawn. Believers are "children of the day" (cf. 1 Thess 5:5–8), so, like people getting up in the morning, they take off their nightclothes and put on the clothes of the day.

**3. Three pairs of sins.** "Let us walk properly as in the daytime, not in orgies and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in quarreling and jealousy" (13:13). Paul puts "quarreling and jealousy" alongside drunkenness and sexual sin. In church we often treat drunkenness and immorality as serious sins, and quarreling and jealousy as mere personality issues; Paul lists them all together as "works of darkness."

**4. Put on the Lord Jesus Christ.** "But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires" (13:14). "Put on" is the language of clothing. Believers "put on Christ" at their baptism (Gal 3:27); now they are to put him on afresh every day, living out his likeness in thought, word and deed. Augustine tells how he read these two verses (13:13–14) in a garden in Milan: "it was as though a light of certainty had been poured into my heart, and all the darkness of doubt vanished away" (*Confessions* 8.12.29, translated from the Latin), and he turned to Christ.

**5. Make no provision for the flesh.** "Provision" (*pronoia*) means planning or preparing in advance. Sin rarely ambushes us out of nowhere; usually we have left a door open for it beforehand: going to a certain place, keeping certain things, keeping certain company. Paul says: don't make arrangements for the flesh.

> The point: The night is far gone; the day is at hand. Believers live in the early morning before Christ's return, so they cast off the works of darkness, put on the Lord Jesus Christ each day, and leave no opening for the flesh.

## Meditation

**Reflect:** "Taxes to whom taxes are owed... honor to whom honor is owed." In small things like filing taxes, issuing receipts or submitting expense claims, am I honest? Do I tell myself "everyone does it," or "the government is bad, so paying a bit less doesn't matter"? When Paul wrote this, the emperor was Nero. My honesty does not depend on every ruler deserving respect, but on keeping a clean conscience before God. Think too of 13:14: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ." Putting on is not weaving a new garment myself, but wearing one someone else has prepared for me. My "clothes for the day" are not my own righteousness but Christ himself. This morning, can I deliberately say to the Lord, as I would while dressing, "Lord, today I put you on"? And then look: is there a door I keep open for the flesh that I need to close today?
